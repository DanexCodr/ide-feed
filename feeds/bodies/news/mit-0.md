In 2017, Apple [removed the home button](https://apnews.com/article/ad9e1a52b5dc4d48bc55256a1124315b) from its iPhones. Users lost a convenient, tangible control — part of a larger shift to digital buttons, knobs, and sliders we also see in cars (like with radio presets and air conditioning) and kitchens (see smart fridges).

We can’t leave physical controls behind entirely, though. They tend to be more convenient and efficient — think of how you can use a physical gaming controller without looking at it — and, at times, more precise, like when you use a dial to get the volume just right. But tangible controls are typically preprogrammed to do a specific thing, minimizing their usefulness.

A new system called “[TangiMap](https://yunyi-zhu.github.io/tangimap/),” co-developed by an MIT graduate student and researcher at MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL), helps bring tangible control back to your software. The system personalizes the physical controls on everyday devices by mapping digital widgets onto tangible inputs, turning things like editing photos, animation, performing music, and creating 3D models into more dynamic and tactile experiences. Just as a DJ’s pads switch between triggering cues, loops, and samples, TangiMap gives a device’s controls entirely new roles.

But how, exactly? TangiMap relies on an AI agent, or semi-autonomous programs that “think” and complete well-defined tasks, to find and act on context about the software you’re using, your tendencies, and the device used to control that program. To gather the required context, the agent can draw on sources such as the program’s API (the rules that let programs talk to each other), an MCP server (a standard that connects AI agents to apps), or a screenshot interpreted by a vision-language model. Then, the agent uses a large language model to put all the info it's gathered into a file before mapping specific digital functions to physical controls.

“TangiMap builds on our broader vision of reprogramming common objects to be more dynamic,” says Yunyi Zhu ’20, MEng ’21, a doctoral student in the MIT Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science and a CSAIL researcher whose advisor is MIT Associate Professor Stefanie Mueller. “Here, AI agents help us adapt physical controls to whatever we’re working on. We want to bring back physical controls so we can interact with our devices eyes-free, the way you adjust a car’s volume without looking away from the road. Physical controls also let you adjust several settings at once, like tweaking brightness and contrast together when editing an image, which is hard to do on a screen.”

The system helped the researchers turn a standard mixer into a photo-editing controller. That way, they could adjust color in Adobe Lightroom, with a complementary visualization of those controls displayed on their screen. The team did the same with sound-mixing software, so that performers can adjust their music in real-time with their own inputs to knobs and faders.

The researchers also reprogrammed a simpler mixer to work with the 3D modeling program Blender. Their interface included an area where users can type in a prompt, and once the model was generated, users could adjust features like shininess on a mixer using physical controls. Within Adobe’s Character Animator software, TangiMap connected specific expressions and movements to buttons on a control pad so users could animate (or “puppeteer”) a cartoon character live.

Zhu and co-author Chang Xiao, an assistant professor at Boston University, [presented their tool at the ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3830398.3830602) earlier this month.

Zhu and Xiao created a custom user interface (UI) for each application. While that adds another layer of personalization to the creative process, the team is hoping to create a more streamlined system that helps the designer to generate the UI for their device by taking a picture.

The researchers are also considering the physical aspects of user interactions. They want to use more ergonomic devices and also ensure that TangiMap accounts for where users position their hands when doing different tasks. For example, users typically hold a mixer differently when they’re using it to make music than, say, when playing a video game. You may want controls mapped closer or further apart, depending on what you’re doing.

Zhu is hoping to do a user study to get more feedback about TangiMap, and whether this kind of “smart DJing” could indeed improve those experiences. But the dynamic reprogramming she ultimately envisions is even more ambitious.

“The same way our phones and computers generalize to a range of tasks, we want other objects to be more intelligent,” she says. “I would like to build physical items that use an AI agent as a brain to decide what to reprogram into. They could understand what to transform into, given a specific situation.”

A chemical jar, for instance, could tell you if it’s sealed correctly when asked, and when you want to see what it’s typically used for, the container would instead display that information. While that kind of versatility and interactivity is a ways off, more powerful agents and more intricate fabrication pipelines could bring everyday objects to life.
