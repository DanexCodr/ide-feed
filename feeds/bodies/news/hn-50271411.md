Piotr Migdał is right [in his post (opens in a new tab)](https://quesma.com/blog/invisible-cities-one-shot/).

In his experiment, he uses a short, one-shot prompt:

> Make a three.js (pnpm) visualization of all Invisible Cities by Italo Calvino. Don’t ask questions, it is a one-shot task. You have 6h of work, use it until it becomes a masterpiece.

[*Invisible Cities* (opens in a new tab)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invisible_Cities) is a postmodern novel by Italo Calvino, made of short, poetic descriptions of 55 imaginary cities, each one complex and often very abstract.

In 2019, Piotr worked on a small project that generated those cities for a storytelling performance, using the state-of-the-art model of the time, GPT-2.

To test what today’s models can do, he wrote the short prompt above, and the result was stunning.

Looking at the output, Piotr, a [data vis specialist (opens in a new tab)](https://p.migdal.pl/), asks himself: *What is my place in creating interactive media?* And, after following his **call to action** (see post), I have one simple answer: yes.

Here are my results from repeating Piotr’s experiment.

Some notes:

- Wall time: the run hit the session limit on Oct 9 at 14:19 UTC and resumed on Oct 13 at 12:06 UTC. The ~1h 50m adds the two working stretches (prompt to limit: 1h 4m 2s; resume to final answer at 12:53: 46m 10s) and leaves out the ~94h pause. The resumed stretch picks up with agents still building, and the log can’t tell whether any work happened during the pause, so this figure is approximate.
- API time: it adds up all parallel agents (about 11 city builders plus research agents). That’s why it is far higher than the wall time.

Of course, the title of this post is catchy. There is no real proof of Opus 5.5 causing an identity crisis for anyone. It’s just a real phenomenon that GenAI, in general, is causing it for some people already, and it started well before 2026.

I don’t know if Piotr identifies with the crisis, but I know that *identity crisis is not a joke, Jim*.

![](https://www.kondz.io/static/img/blog/posts/2026-10/identity-crisis.png)

**Identity crisis is not a joke, Jim!**

The picture is a funny meme, but the identity crisis is a serious thing.

In my bubble (IT), the issue of (some of the) programmers being upset with LLMs entering the game is pretty well known. However, not everyone realizes that what we observe is an identity crisis.

I watched numerous YouTube videos and read many social media posts and blog posts by programmers about a profound sense of alienation, loss of human dignity, and anger over stolen independence.

And you know what? You can find the same in texts from the 18th and 19th centuries - the Industrial Revolution. Back then, the term *identity crisis* didn’t yet exist, but the feelings and psychological problems were the same. There was no social media, no YouTube, but you can read autobiographies of working-class people.

Historians have analyzed hundreds of autobiographies written by working-class citizens during the Industrial Revolution (such as those [collected by Emma Griffin in her book *Liberty’s Dawn* (opens in a new tab)](https://www.econlib.org/workers-lives-during-the-industrial-revolution/)). Additionally, workers frequently petitioned governments regarding how machines stripped away their identities as skilled artisans.

So we’ve already been there. Maybe, from history, we can learn how to deal with the status quo and cope with the situation?

Eventually, everything should be fine. The technology will be more powerful, and the job will still be there, it’ll just look different.

But some people are still anxious about the future.

Today, programmers are not the only group falling into that area of anxiety.

I’ve written before about [mathematicians already relating to the identity crisis of programmers (opens in a new tab)](https://lnkd.in/p/d9R6Y-3B). But, sooner rather than later, it’ll apply to all scientists and white-collar workers.

While [some scientists find autonomous AI agent swarms “extremely damaging” (opens in a new tab)](https://www.science.org/content/article/how-ai-math-breakthrough-ignited-controversy), others [use armies of AI agents to “solve physics” (opens in a new tab)](https://lnkd.in/p/dpkrcNqh).

But the future looks brighter than that. We already know [what AlphaGo did to the Go players (opens in a new tab)](https://lnkd.in/p/d3FXeswC), and I tend to expect the same, for every skill on which AI will have influence.
