When the godfather of AI suggests that we might be better off without AI, then you probably should listen. His lifetime quest to make AI real seems to have a bitter ending - or does it?  In case you do not know, let me explain that during the decades-long AI winter, Geoffrey Hinton kept working on neural networks, at a time when it was career suicide to base your research on such a discredited idea. He kept the lights on for neural networks when nearly everyone else moved over to symbolic reasoning, expert systems and kernel methods — the now-old science of machine learning. No one guessed that ML was going to prove a dead end that would be, more or less, killed off by the no-hope technology of the neural network. Strange how much the scenery of AI has changed in such a short time. What is more, even the neural network AI people didn't really see this coming. This is mainly because language was not a hot topic. Back in the day, natural language understanding was based on grammar, syntax and symbolic logic. Arcane sciences that few understood but were expected to lead somewhere someday. Neural networks were all about computer vision and, to a lesser extent, more general classification and decision making. Language just wasn't in the picture that they were looking at. Then the mechanism of attention was added to simple feed-forward networks and this gave them the power reserved for recursive networks that were next to impossible to train, but with much easier implementation. In no time at all, we were talking to LLMs that went well beyond anyone's expectations. Hinton was right all along: all that was needed was more and bigger neural networks. The models returned, and still return, astounding results for anyone familiar with the failures of the symbolic approach to natural language. Suddenly we had AI that worked on an industrial scale - no need to make the sort of excuses for running it in a playground world with deliberately restricted inputs, you could let anyone try it. At this point, things turned out not as the AI pioneers expected. Instead of computer vision being used for all sorts of automatic inspection tasks, we could talk to our AI and ask it to write a poem, or draw a picture, in the style of whatever or whoever you wanted to emulate. AI had come for the creative arts, and I include both programming and math in this category. Hinton more or less immediately grasped that this was not the brave new world he and the rest of us had been seeking, and started to issue warnings. His warnings were not about rogue AI, but about the fact that the technology had already surpassed what nature had constructed. Neural networks with backpropagation and attention learn faster than any biological system, and this is new. In terms of evolution it is as if some new form of life had just evolved that could out-compete the lot of us.  In the about-to-be-released documentary,_**AI: Probably Nothing to Worry About**_ , Hinton and Demis Hassabis put the point of view that AI isn't necessarily bad, but it is going to put a lot of creatives out of work. But they don't really take on the bigger problems AI poses. At a pre-release screening of the documentary, in an interview with the UK newspaper The Guardian, Hinton said: _"I feel I have a duty. I’ve got a reputation that makes me a spokesperson and I need to burn it all on this."_ He went on to say that while AI has the potential to lead to a _"wonderful future"_ , but: _"it might be that we would have been better off never developing it."_ He might be right, but given that AI has escaped the sandbox of the research lab and is already in the hands of entrepreneurs and the tech bros, such academic considerations are not going to get much of a look-in. We have gone beyond the pioneer phase and we are deep into exploitation, but we should still listen to what Hinton and Hassibis have to say.
