Housing is the single largest expense for most European households. This is
      not a controversial claim - everyone who pays rent or a mortgage knows it.
      Food, transport, healthcare: none comes close. The question is not whether
      housing dominates household budgets, but whether we can measure *how much*
      and *where*.

Eurostat publishes exactly such a measure. The EU-SILC survey asks households
      across Europe what they spend on housing - rent or mortgage interest, utilities,
      insurance, maintenance, taxes - and reports the total as a share of disposable
      income. Here is what it says for the five countries featured in this analysis.

At first glance, this looks like exactly the data we need. It isn't.

This is a country-level average. It flattens the extreme regional differences
      that define the European housing problem - the gap between Lisbon and Beira Baixa,
      between Paris and the Massif Central, between Amsterdam and Groningen. And Eurostat
      does not publish this indicator at any finer level of geography.

Worse, the statistic
      [counts](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=EU_statistics_on_income_and_living_conditions_(EU-SILC)_methodology_-_economic_strain_linked_to_dwelling)
      rents and mortgage *interest* but not mortgage
      *repayment*. A household paying €1,500 a month on a mortgage - of which
      €400 is interest and €1,100 is principal - shows up as spending €400 on housing.
      The other €1,100, the part that actually hurts, is invisible. And the split between
      interest and principal depends on the rate, the country, and when the contract
      was signed - making cross-country comparison unreliable even before we consider
      that countries have very different shares of
      [owners and renters](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/interactive-publications/housing-2025).

The result is a ranking that should give anyone pause. Portugal - a country
      going through a severe housing crisis - appears as the least burdened.
      The Netherlands, where the housing debate is real but less acute, appears as the most.
      The indicator counts rent in full but only the interest part of a mortgage, so its
      result depends heavily on how each country's households are split between renters,
      owners with a mortgage and outright owners - and, among mortgaged owners, on how much
      of each payment is interest. In Southern Europe, where outright ownership is common,
      a large share of households contributes no rent or mortgage cost to the average.

Eurostat publishes a second indicator from the same survey: the
      [financial
      burden of housing cost](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/ILC_MDED04/), where households themselves report whether housing
      is a heavy burden, some burden, or no burden at all. This indicator is sensational, in the sense that it
      measures sensations.

When you ask people how they actually feel, the ranking is very different -
      and the Netherlands moves from one extreme to the other. Only 6% of
      Dutch households report a heavy burden. In Portugal it is 26%, in Italy and
      Spain nearly 40%. The calculated statistic and the lived experience point in
      different directions.

In our analysis we will study regional differences,
      face mortgage repayments as what they are - a burden - and avoid relying
      on sensations to draw conclusions. Using metrics built from actual regional
      data, we will find all these five countries, and their NUTS-3 regions,
      in positions that better reflect the economic strain linked to dwelling.

Welcome to the European housing data crisis.

"The first casualty when war comes is truth" - a line often attributed to US Senator Hiram Johnson in 1917.
      Housing crises have their own version: the first casualty is data. You cannot measure
      a problem for which there is no objective data.

We have already seen how the best available Eurostat indicator - the housing cost
      burden - produces a ranking that contradicts lived experience. But the problems
      run deeper. Europe has no comparable, cross-country dataset of housing prices per
      square metre. It has no harmonised regional wage data at the level where people
      actually live and work.
      To build the analysis you are reading, we had to stitch together data from five different
      national statistical offices - INE in Portugal, INE in Spain, INSEE in France, ISTAT in
      Italy, CBS in the Netherlands - each with its own methodology, coverage, granularity
      and reporting lag. Some publish transaction prices; others publish appraisal values.
      Some report wages at the NUTS-3 level; others only at NUTS-2, requiring workarounds
      that introduce uncertainty.

Eurostat, the EU's own statistical office, does publish a European House Price Index.
      It sounds exactly like what you'd need. It is not. The HPI is an index - it measures
      how much prices *changed* within each country relative to a base year (2015 = 100).
      It can tell you that Portuguese prices rose 89% since 2015. It cannot tell you what a
      square metre costs in Lisbon versus Madrid. You cannot divide an index number by a wage
      to get affordability. The one EU-wide housing statistic is structurally useless for the
      one question citizens actually ask: *can I afford to live here?*

[Mapadomo](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/scientific-activities/mapadomo_en)
      comes to the rescue. "granular housing data for Europe", they promise. The dataset, with data for 2000-2020, is unavailable (as of November 2026).

Then there is the question of knowing where people actually live. Some countries
      maintain continuous population registers - the Netherlands and Spain update theirs
      yearly, France has run a rolling annual census since 2004, and Italy has had a
      permanent census since 2018. But even these registers struggle to track
      sub-national migration in real time. In Portugal, which still relies on a
      traditional decennial census, the president and the national statistics office have
      had a public debate over whether the official population figures reflect reality.
      A housing policy that cannot track where people actually live is steering blind.

Throughout this analysis, we use regional employment as a proxy for the number of
      people economically active in a region. Employment data is updated yearly and
      registered at the workplace, not the residence. It still undercounts - informal
      workers leave no statistical trace - but it tracks reality better than population
      figures that lag behind internal migration. In Southern Europe especially, people
      can hold formal jobs and pay rent in regions where they have no residence permit.
      The employment register captures at least part of what the population register misses.

Curating comparable price and wage transaction data across member states is exactly the
      kind of work the European institutions exist to coordinate. They have the mandate, the
      staff and the budget. Meanwhile, the analysis you are reading - built from raw national
      sources, cross-referenced and harmonised at the NUTS-3 level for 312 regions - was
      assembled by a single citizen, working for free, on evenings and weekends. If one person
      can do it, the absence of an official equivalent might be a political decision. Or is it
      the inertia of not deciding? Hard to tell.

Transparency is not about data not being secret. It is mostly about data being available
      at a reasonable effort. The effort to compare regional affordability does not seem reasonable.
      This text stems from very unreasonable amounts of effort.

To be fair, they did define the NUTS-3 regional classification and they keep
      employment data updated. While insufficient for illustration of the present housing crisis,
      both have been useful for this analysis. We cover 312 NUTS-3 regions
      across five countries; of these, 309 have both price and wage data for the
      effort calculation, and the combined scatter plots show 306 because four
      Paris-area *départements* are merged into a single data point.

This is the map that housing prices have to be read against. A region's
      affordability only makes sense relative to whether anyone can actually work there.

A square metre in Lisbon costs less than a square metre in Amsterdam. But does
      that make Lisbon more affordable? Only if the person buying it earns a Dutch salary.

Let's divide the price of the square meter in a region by the wage of that region, using
      means or medians, depending on what statistic is available.
      We call this metric **housing purchase effort**: the number of months of gross
      salary needed to buy a single square metre. Because it is a ratio of two local
      quantities measured in the same currency, it allows direct comparison across
      regions and years, eliminating the problematic effects of regional wage differences and inflation.
      At the national level, the picture is already telling.

Portugal and France look remarkably similar - around one month of salary
      per square metre. The Netherlands, despite far higher prices, comes out better because wages more
      than compensate.

These are national averages. The real story appears when we zoom in to
      NUTS-3 level - the smallest regional unit for which both housing prices and wages
      are available. White on the map marks an effort of 0.7 - roughly the threshold
      at which a 50 m² apartment can be repaid in about ten years at maximum
      mortgage effort. More on loan repayment in
      [Part eight](https://nuts.logical.li/#part-eight).

The maps reveal what national averages hide. Within a single country, the variation in effort
      is often larger than the variation between countries. A worker in the Algarve faces a
      fundamentally different reality than one in Bragança - same country, same labour law,
      but wildly different affordability.

What explains the variation? House purchasing effort reflects a demand-supply relationship:
      when more people compete for limited housing stock, prices rise relative to wages.
      To lower effort, a region needs more houses per resident. But building more is not
      always possible - you cannot upgrade buildings from 10 to 20 floors once they are
      built, and widening a road from four to six lanes does not help if single-lane
      bottlenecks lie between commuting points. Even when possible, building takes a long time.
      Population relocation or inflow can happen much faster than supply adjustment, which requires
      projects, permits, manpower and bricks. Because of this, regions that face continuous
      increase of demand may never fully adjust - they become dense under constrained supply.

It is worth saying that density brings efficiency up to a
      point, not indefinitely and not regardless of case-by-case circumstances.

Neither housing supply nor actual demand are reliably
      measured at the regional level - another data gap. What we do have is employment
      density: employed persons per square kilometre. In regions that are not overly touristic,
      this is a reasonable proxy for demand pressure on housing.

When we plot employment density against purchase effort, a pattern arises:
      denser regions are systematically harder to afford.

Portugal has only 26 NUTS-3 regions. Azores uses a NUTS-2 wage fallback
      because national wage data is unavailable for this region;
      plot captions give the number of regions shown, but fallback regions are
      excluded from the regression fits throughout. A log-linear fit to the 25
      non-fallback regions explains 33% of the variation in effort (R² = 0.33).
      That rises to 82% when
      we set aside four outliers - Algarve, Madeira, Alentejo Litoral and Alentejo
      Central - regions where tourism and/or other local factors push effort well above
      what density alone would predict. Outliers are marked with a red circle in the plot.
      Colour saturation encodes tourism intensity (overnight stays per worker) -
      the more saturated the dot, the higher the tourism pressure.

With a sample this small, a handful of regions
      can move the fit substantially, so the R² should be read with caution. Still,
      the direction is unambiguous: denser regions come with higher purchasing effort.

Spain provides a larger sample: 47 of its 59 NUTS-3 regions. The missing twelve
      are the Balearic Islands, the Canary Islands, and the autonomous cities of Ceuta
      and Melilla, where price or wage data is not available at this level of geography.
      The four foral provinces (Álava, Bizkaia, Gipuzkoa, Navarra) are included but use
      NUTS-2 fallback wages, since AEAT does not cover territories with their own tax
      administrations; these four are excluded from the regression fit, leaving 43.
      Density alone explains 64% of
      the variation, that value rising to 76% (R² = 0.757) by excluding touristic outliers Málaga and Girona,
      and a third outlier called Huesca.
      The larger sample, with a lower proportion of outliers, allows for a more stable estimate of the relationship.

When we pool Portugal and Spain together - two countries with very different wage
      levels, price structures and regional geography - the combined sample reaches
      73 regions (68 after excluding fallbacks). Density alone explains 50% of the variation (R² = 0.50), rising to
      72% when we exclude the five outliers of this combined fit (Algarve, Madeira, Alentejo Litoral,
      Alentejo Central, Málaga). The relationship survives crossing borders.

Adding France brings 90 of its 96 metropolitan *départements*. Three are
      missing because the DVF property transaction database does not cover
      Alsace-Moselle (Bas-Rhin, Haut-Rhin, Moselle), where a local land registry
      law predates the national system. The remaining gap is Paris: its NUTS-3 boundary covers
      only the 103 km² city proper, registering over 21,000 jobs per km² - a density
      so extreme it compresses every other region to the left of the chart. To make Paris
      comparable to other capital regions we merge it with the three *départements* of its
      *Petite Couronne* (Hauts-de-Seine, Seine-Saint-Denis, Val-de-Marne), weighting
      by employment. The merged Paris metro unit still leads the density ranking at around 6,500 jobs/km²,
      but the effort index drops from 2.1 to 1.61 - high, but no longer on a mathematical cliff.
      This is also why we switch to a logarithmic x-axis: even after merging, the density range
      spans from single digits to thousands. This merge is not cheating: data work is sometimes
      messy and the NUTS-3 classification is not clean either
      (see [*NUTS-3 regions are not equal*](https://nuts.logical.li/#appendix-nuts3) in the appendix).

With 163 regions across three countries (158 fitted), density explains 64% of the variation in effort
      (R² = 0.637, excluding 13 outliers). The logarithmic relationship holds across
      borders, wage systems and housing markets.

And it still holds directionally with all five countries combined, despite the
      complexity of Italy, the unusual affordability of the Netherlands relative to its
      job density, and the existing comparability issues
      (see [*Comparability caveats*](https://nuts.logical.li/#appendix-comparability) in the appendix).

The easy reading of this data is "large cities are expensive." Certainly, prices rise with job density
      and so do wages. But wages in dense areas don't rise proportionally to housing costs. The gap between what housing costs and what
      people earn widens as you move toward the economic centres. Then there are the
      obviously touristic regions: Algarve, Málaga, Girona, Alicante, the Côte d'Azur
      (Alpes-Maritimes, Var), Corsica, Rimini, Bolzano. These regions are low density,
      high effort - tourism inflates prices where local wages, driven by a non-touristic
      job market that is not competitive enough, have no chance of keeping up. These regions
      are a very specific minority where what happens does not follow the general rule. And
      they are usually not the ones that include the capital.

Removing these outliers reveals a strong density-effort relationship that holds
      even when mixing several countries. It does not remove the effect of tourism -
      it removes the effect of *extreme* tourism. The regions that remain still feel
      tourism to varying degrees, and that residual influence partly explains why the
      fit, while robust, is not perfect: moderate tourism pushes some regions above the
      density-only trend line without being extreme enough to flag them as outliers.
      Once we recognise this, the natural next step is not to keep discarding data points
      but to accept tourism intensity as a second variable alongside employment density.

A multivariate model using only two predictors - employment density and tourism
      intensity - explains 57% to 85% of the variation in effort, depending on the country.

We can isolate each variable's contribution by fitting for density and tourism
      separately. The table below shows R² for density-only and tourism-only fits, both
      with all regions and after excluding outliers.

Density alone explains 34% to 64% of the variation, rising to
      61% to 82% after excluding outliers - the touristic regions that break the
      density pattern. Tourism-only fits tell the opposite story: R² stays below 0.25
      for Spain, France and the Netherlands, and the already low values collapse
      further after outlier exclusion. Portugal's tourism-only R² of 0.57 is driven
      by the strong pull of a few touristic outliers like Algarve and Madeira - small
      samples (n=25 fitted) make such anomalous
      situations more likely; once those outliers are removed, tourism explains
      essentially nothing (R² = 0.02). Italy is the exception: tourism-only R² is
      0.39 with a large enough sample (n=106) to make accidental alignments unlikely,
      and it holds at 0.27 after outlier exclusion.

Unlike fitting for density, fitting for tourism alone does not expose a small
      number of outlier regions that could be removed to improve the fit. That's because,
      apart from Italy where the situation is more complex, tourism-driven regions are the
      exception, easily identified as outliers when fitting for density.

Comparing the two tables confirms this reading. The multivariate fit
      (density + tourism) improves on the density-only fit in every country -
      tourism always adds predictive power. More telling: the multivariate fit
      also beats the density-only-clean fit (outliers excluded) in four of the
      five countries. Even after removing the most touristic regions, the result
      is worse than with tourism as a second variable. The single exception is the
      Netherlands, which makes sense: it is not a tourism-heavy country, and
      its outliers are likely driven by other factors.

The takeaway: in most regions, tourism modulates the influence of job density -
      it nudges effort up or down around a trend that density sets. Only in a
      handful of regions does tourism override job density entirely and become the
      dominant force.

Capital regions concentrate a disproportionate share of national employment.
      Grande Lisboa alone holds 25.5% of all jobs in Portugal. Madrid holds 17.9%
      of all jobs in Spain, the Paris metro (Paris + Petite Couronne) 15.9% in
      France. Even in polycentric Italy,
      Roma accounts for 8.5%, matched only by Milano at 8.2%. Workers do not choose
      these regions on a whim - they go where the jobs are.

And the jobs are expensive to live near. Capital regions rank among the
      highest in effort - the Paris metro reaches 1.61, Grande Lisboa 1.30,
      Madrid 1.27, Groot-Amsterdam 1.09 - driven by the density relationship
      we have seen: more workers competing for housing in a bounded territory.
      Tourism-heavy regions like the Algarve (1.86) or the Côte d'Azur (1.42)
      can rank even higher. To make this concrete, we translate effort into
      mortgage repayment time for a 50 m² flat.

These are regions where a single earner needs nearly two decades or more to repay
      a modest flat. Even after combining four *départements*, the
      Paris metro requires 37 years. A 75 m² apartment instead? The monthly payment
      no longer covers the interest. The loan is never repaid. And these timelines
      cover only the mortgage itself - saving for the 10% deposit and transaction taxes,
      which add roughly 3% to 12% of the purchase price depending on the country,
      is time not included in the simulation (see [Part eight](https://nuts.logical.li/#part-eight)).

Paris itself is even more extreme. Its NUTS-3 region (FR101) covers only
      the dense urban core, where the effort index reaches 2.1 - pushing the
      mortgage calculation to 101 years. But that number sits on a mathematical
      cliff: at 4% interest, the monthly payment barely covers the interest on
      the loan. A small rate increase to 4.2% tips it over the edge - the loan
      becomes infinite. A drop to 3.5% brings it down to 56 years. No other
      region in our dataset is this sensitive to assumptions, which is why we
      lead with the Petite Couronne figure instead.

The trap is not just financial. Capital regions are large enough that
      living on their edge already means a long commute, and living outside
      them means commuting even further while inheriting some of the inflated
      housing prices. Workers in surrounding regions earn wages that do not
      match the centre but face prices driven up by proximity to it.
      (See [*NUTS-3 regions are not equal*](https://nuts.logical.li/#appendix-nuts3)
      in the appendix for how boundary size affects cross-country comparisons.)

In public debate, tourism is frequently cited as the primary driver of housing
      unaffordability. Short-term rentals, foreign buyers, holiday apartments consuming
      the housing stock. The narrative is compelling and, in specific places, not wrong.

The data tells a different story at scale.

Tourism does correlate with higher effort - the multivariate model confirms it has
      a small, positive coefficient in every country. But as a single variable it explains
      only 23% of the variation across all five countries (R² = 0.231), and as we saw in the
      single-variable fit evidence, that number collapses for most countries once outliers
      are removed.

Despite a similar R² (the five-country density vs effort fit has R² = 0.265),
      this plot is substantially different from the density vs effort
      plot for all countries: visually, we don't see a way for improving the fit by removing
      a handful of data points. Unlike what happens with the density vs effort plot where
      a clear group is identifiable by eye - even if not automatically marked as outliers -
      removing the oddities here leads to "vertical data", i.e. no correlation.

There are particular regions where tourism has a strong influence like the Algarve, the Côte d'Azur, the Balearics or Corsica.
      It is also true that some city centres are disproportionately under pressure, with both employment and
      tourist demand in the same square kilometres. It is even fair to say that tourism intensity
      should be actively monitored and managed in all the regions - there are other good reasons, apart from
      house prices.

Still, the contribution of tourism to the overall picture is secondary. Within each
      country, employment density - the sheer number of jobs per square kilometre - explains
      far more of the variation than tourism does. And Europe is not facing a city-centre-only crisis. NUTS-3 regions span entire
      metropolitan areas, suburbs and commuter belts. When a NUTS-3 region is unaffordable,
      it means that even workers willing to endure long commutes within it cannot escape
      the cost pressure. The problem covers the territory where people actually live, not
      just the postcard downtown. The problem of scapegoating tourism is that we may
      remain blind to the structural problem of density. And risk overshooting
      tourism restrictions without achieving meaningful results.

Is there a causal relationship between density and effort?
      Correlation does not imply causation, but it invites analysis.

When job density and effort move together, three
      readings are possible: effort causes density, density causes effort, or both
      are caused by something else.

The first is implausible - high housing costs do
      not attract employers. The third is more subtle: one or more external factors would need
      to drive both density and effort in such a way that the specific logarithmic
      relationship we observe between them is produced across different countries.
      It is not enough for a confounding variable to merely raise both; it would need to shape them into a
      specific mathematical form. Explanations based on confounding variables (geographic constraints, cultural traits, ...)
      would require many things to fall in the right place, with the right intensity, across several countries.
      The reading that best survives parsimony is the most
      direct: density causes effort because people need to work and tend to live
      within commuting distance of the workplace. NUTS-3 regions are typically large
      enough to contain both workplaces and residences. Within a region, prices react
      to demand pressure more vigorously than wages do, because building houses takes time and accepting a longer
      loan feels like a better option than not having a job.

Employment density is the candidate structural driver. Tourism amplifies specific hotspots
      and influences other regions, but it is not the master variable. Companies open offices where talent lives. People move to
      where they can work. Demand concentrates. Prices rise. More companies open offices
      where talent already moved. More people move there. The cycle feeds itself.

For some regions, housing is mathematically out of reach under any reasonable
      set of mortgage assumptions.

When we simulate a standard mortgage - 90% loan-to-value, 4% interest, 45% of
      net salary going to repayment - a large share of workers
      face a repayment period of 15 years or more for a 50 m² flat. This is roughly a studio or small
      one-bedroom apartment. In Paris and its Petite Couronne, the combined capital region, a 50 m²
      flat takes 37 years to repay. Paris itself sits on a mathematical cliff where the payment
      barely covers interest (see [Part six](https://nuts.logical.li/#part-six)). In Lisbon, the simulated average
      loan repayment for a 50 m² apartment is 24 years.

A 45% effort ratio is unusually permissive. It matches Portugal's regulatory DSTI limit, but
      exceeds France's 35% limit and the affordability thresholds commonly applied in Spain and Italy.
      The Dutch system uses income-dependent limits rather than a fixed percentage. The simulation therefore
      uses a relatively generous assumption, and actual borrowing capacity may be lower.

Furthermore, these figures count only the loan. Before the first payment, a buyer must save
      the 10% deposit plus transaction taxes and fees - which range from roughly 3% of the
      purchase price in the Netherlands to 8-12% in Spain and Portugal, with France and Italy
      in between. Early in a career, when salaries are lowest and savings hardest to accumulate,
      this can take years. The time required to become a homeowner can therefore extend well
      beyond the simulated mortgage repayment period.

The simulation pairs one salary with a 50 m² flat. This is a proxy for household
      effort regardless of household size: a couple earning two salaries would face a
      proportionally larger home. Household income and composition data are not available
      at NUTS-3 level, so we hold the ratio fixed - one income, one modest unit - to
      keep the comparison fair across regions and countries.

In Portugal, three quarters of employment is located in regions where a small flat
      requires a 15+ years repayment effort. In Spain and France, it is nearly half. Even in
      Italy, a third. Deposit comes on top. These are the regions where the jobs are. "Move somewhere cheaper"
      means leaving employment behind.

The metric we use throughout this analysis is original and opinionated. It introduces mortgage
      simulations based on regional data assembled from national sources and uses a 15-year repayment
      horizon as the threshold for unaffordability - a cutoff that is, at best, borderline reasonable.
      We build it because nothing better exists at this level of granularity, but a fair question is
      whether the picture it paints is accurate.

As a consistency check, we turn to an official source. In the opening section we saw that
      Eurostat's housing cost burden indicator excludes mortgage principal repayment, making
      cross-country comparison misleading. The
      [OECD Affordable Housing Database](https://www.oecd.org/content/oecd/en/data/datasets/oecd-affordable-housing-database.html),
      however, publishes a closely related indicator -
      the median mortgage burden as a share of disposable income - that includes principal repayment,
      without mixing in energy, maintenance, or insurance costs. This indicator is not granular - it
      irons out regional differences, as every national indicator does - and it measures something
      conceptually different from what we built: rather than estimating potential strain from simulated
      loans or simple effort ratios, it captures actual burden from self-reported mortgage payments
      in EU-SILC.

Because actual mortgage payments are sensitive to ECB key interest rates - and a large share of
      loans in these countries carries variable rates - we use a three-year average (2022-2024) to
      smooth year-to-year volatility.

The ranking is the same. Portugal carries the heaviest mortgage burden, followed by France,
      Spain, Italy, and the Netherlands - the order produced by our regional effort metric in
      [Part four](https://nuts.logical.li/#part-four). Two indicators built from different data, at different
      scales, with different methodologies, point in the same direction.

How concentrated is employment today? In Portugal, half of all jobs sit on just 11%
      of the country's land area. Spain packs them into 13%, France into 14%.
      And since 2000, the picture got worse for almost every country. In Spain the area
      needed to cover half of employment shrank by 3.8 percentage points, in the Netherlands
      by 3.6. Across the EU, the trend is even starker: Bulgaria lost 13 points,
      Hungary and Finland 12.

Every capital region gained employment share - from Paris (+0.5 pp) to Lisbon (+3.7 pp).

## [#](https://nuts.logical.li/#conclusion)
Conclusion
      The elephant in the house

Two findings stand out.

First, housing purchase effort is structurally
      correlated with employment density across all five countries under study.
      The simplest explanation is causal: when more
      workers compete for the same land, prices rise relative to wages. Second, that
      pressure has been intensifying. Since 2000, every capital region recorded
      double-digit employment growth - not as an isolated boom but as part of a
      broader trend in which the share of national employment concentrated around
      capitals has increased. More jobs, more people, same land - the
      mechanism is self-reinforcing.

The chronically lagging supply is not attributable
      to a single problem. Many have been publicly mentioned: lack of space, permit
      bottlenecks, manpower scarcity, expensive credit, zoning laws, political obstacles.
      Whatever mix affects one region is different from the mix of another, even the effect being the same.
      Some argue for more houses; others argue for price controls.
      It is hard to imagine that the affordability problem in dense regions will be solved
      using either simplistic approach. And the more concentration increases the more the
      solution is dependent on a few regional administrative structures - hostage to whatever
      logistical issues or political biases affect the dominating regions.
      As that happens, people will, most likely, remain strained - and productivity, fertility and well-being will suffer.
      Density and concentration are rarely part of that conversation, which is what motivated
      this study. Could decentralisation be part of the solution?

The COVID-era experiment with remote work briefly suggested a different future.
      Workers discovered they could live in affordable regions and earn urban salaries.
      Housing pressure on dense areas eased, if only slightly. Secondary cities saw
      new demand. In 2020, the question was suddenly legitimate: do we all need to be
      on 11% of the land to do our jobs?

That window is closing. Return-to-office mandates, the gravitational pull
      of face-to-face networks, and policy inertia are reassembling the old pattern.
      The structural incentives that concentrate employment have not changed: agglomeration
      economies, headquarters bias, infrastructure investment tilted toward capitals.

Return-to-office mandates, one after another, are not just HR policies. They are
      housing policies. Every RTO memo that says "three office days in Lisbon / Madrid / Paris"
      is also a memo that says "and therefore 24/23/37 years of mortgage for a 50 m²".
      We re-concentrated on purpose, after seeing what de-concentration looked like.

Every fiscal incentive that only rewards a headquarters in the capital, every
      high-speed line that only connects capitals, every new university campus that
      opens inside the same 11% - is a peanut in the corner. Employment concentration
      is the elephant in the house - the structural force that no amount of tinkering
      with mortgage rates, construction permits, or tourist regulations will fully
      overcome. The elephant will not move while we keep feeding the corner.

This analysis requires three datasets at NUTS-3 level for every country: housing
      prices per m², wages, and employment. No single European source publishes all three.
      Each country must be assembled from national statistical offices, tax authorities
      and property registries - each with its own methodology, coverage and access
      conditions. Portugal, Spain, France, Italy and the Netherlands are the five where
      the necessary data could be obtained at comparable quality. Germany was investigated
      but its regional price data (AK OGA) is published only as binned ranges, not as
      values usable in regression. Denmark's wages are available only at NUTS-2, too
      coarse for this analysis. Other countries face similar gaps. Expanding coverage
      is a matter of data access, not methodology.

2023 is the most recent year for which all five countries have both housing prices
      and wages at NUTS-3 level. France is the binding constraint - INSEE publishes
      regional wages with a one-year lag, so 2022 wages are the latest available and are
      used as a proxy for 2023. Portugal's transaction price series (INE 0012235) starts
      in Q4 2019, making anything before 2020 unavailable at comparable quality. Italy's 
      INPS wage data begins in 2019. Tourism data from Eurostat (tour_occ_nin3) switched
      to NUTS 2024 codes in 2023, making that the only year where tourism intensity can
      be matched to the current regional boundaries without manual code reconciliation.
      The housing cost burden chart uses the same 2023 reference year as the rest of
      the analysis.

### [#](https://nuts.logical.li/#appendix-comparability)Comparability caveats

Cross-country comparisons should be read with care. The wage concept differs:
      Portugal (MTSS Quadros de Pessoal) and Italy (INPS) cover private-sector employees
      only, while Spain (AEAT) and France (INSEE DADS) include all sectors. The
      Netherlands (CBS) reports compensation of employees - gross salary plus employer
      social contributions - which is corrected by dividing by 1.27 to approximate
      gross salary. Within each country all regions use the same concept, so regional
      rankings are unaffected.

Housing prices also differ in concept: Portugal and France use median transaction
      prices from notarial records; Spain and the Netherlands use mean transaction prices;
      Italy uses expert-appraised market value ranges (OMI quotations), not actual
      transactions. Mean prices are typically 5-15% higher than medians due to luxury
      outliers. The Italian figures are methodologically closer to valuations than to
      recorded sales.

There are also differences between national definitions of dwelling floor area. These
      include the treatment of common areas in apartment buildings, balconies and terraces,
      as well as other spaces such as cellars, attics and storage rooms. Consequently, the
      reported price per square metre is not based on a perfectly harmonised measure of floor area across countries.

Per-country scatter plots and regressions are reliable. The combined multi-country
      plots are useful for seeing overall patterns but should not be read as precise
      cross-country rankings.

Visitor nights per worker is a NUTS-3 average. In a capital region, tourists
      cluster near the city centre while the workforce spreads across the whole
      metropolitan area, so the metric undercounts tourism pressure where it is most
      intense and dilutes it across suburbs where it barely exists. In regions like
      Algarve or Corsica, where tourism is the defining activity across the territory,
      visitors are more distributed and this average is far more representative of
      reality. Naturally, when comparing regions, this nuance can only be evaluated
      qualitatively, because NUTS-3 is the finest granularity we have data for.

### [#](https://nuts.logical.li/#appendix-nuts3)NUTS-3 regions are not equal

NUTS-3 boundaries follow administrative divisions, not a standard grid.
      Paris (FR101) is 103 km² - just the city proper. Madrid (ES300) is 7,972 km² -
      the entire province, including sparsely populated countryside. Grande Lisboa
      sits between at 1,322 km²; Rome covers 5,280 km². This matters most for
      employment density: Paris registers 21,700 jobs per km² while Madrid shows
      484 - a 45-fold difference that is largely a boundary artefact, not a real
      difference in urban intensity.

Housing effort (price ÷ wage) is more robust, since both numerator and
      denominator are measured at the same geographic scale and area does not
      enter the calculation. However, large regions like Madrid average
      high-effort urban cores with low-effort rural fringes, which understates
      peak affordability pressure. Paris, by contrast, captures almost exclusively
      the dense, expensive centre - which is why its effort figure looks extreme
      even by capital-city standards. The adjacent Petite Couronne *départements*
      (Hauts-de-Seine, Seine-Saint-Denis, Val-de-Marne) have effort values of
      1.1 to 1.3 - still elevated, but far below the 2.1 of Paris itself.

This is why in the cross-country scatter plots we merge Paris with its
      Petite Couronne into a single employment-weighted data point. The case for
      merging is straightforward. At 103 km², Paris is the smallest NUTS-3 region
      in our entire dataset - smaller than any region in Portugal, Spain, the
      Netherlands, or Italy. Even after merging with the three surrounding
      *départements* the combined area is just 753 km², still well below the median
      NUTS-3 area of 4,500 km² across our five countries. The four regions form
      a contiguous urban mass where housing markets are deeply intertwined: Paris
      prices would not sustain themselves without the millions of workers who
      commute from the Petite Couronne every day, and those same workers face
      housing costs inflated by their proximity to the centre. Treating Paris
      as an isolated data point overstates its density by an order of magnitude
      and places its effort on a mathematical cliff where small assumption
      changes produce wild swings. The merge does not hide the problem - Paris
      + Petite Couronne still ranks as the highest-priced and densest unit in
      our dataset - it simply brings the measurement closer to what the other
      capital regions already capture by default through their wider boundaries.

If anything, this intervention errs on the side of caution: the functional
      Paris labour market extends well beyond the Petite Couronne into the
      grande couronne and further, and a case could be made for merging a
      larger area still. We stop at four *départements* because they form the
      obvious contiguous core and because extending further would require
      judgement calls about where the commuter basin ends - a question that
      the NUTS classification was supposed to answer for us.
      None of this is a criticism of the NUTS-3 classification itself. EU regulation
      requires NUTS regions to follow existing administrative divisions within each
      member state, and for good reason: administrative units have the data-collection
      infrastructure, legal authority, and political legitimacy that purpose-built
      statistical areas would lack. France's *départements* date to the Revolution,
      designed so any citizen could reach the capital in a day on horseback - sensible
      for 18th-century governance, less so for 21st-century labour-market analysis.

Not every country maps NUTS-3 to a traditional administrative division.
      The Netherlands is the clearest exception: its COROP regions are non-administrative
      areas designed around commuting patterns. Portugal's Comunidades Intermunicipais are a
      hybrid - created to coordinate EU structural funding, they have no role in
      day-to-day governance, which remains with the individual municipalities. But
      France, Spain and Italy map NUTS-3 directly to long-established administrative
      divisions - *départements*, *provincias*, *province* - whose boundaries reflect
      centuries of political history. The Paris merge is our way of working with
      what we have: not redrawing the map, but acknowledging that one boundary
      was drawn too tightly for the question we are asking.

Per-country scatter plots and regressions are not affected - regions
      within the same country are drawn from the same administrative system
      and are reasonably comparable. The combined cross-country scatter plots
      use the Paris + Petite Couronne merge described above, which mitigates
      the most extreme size asymmetry. The x-axis (density) still mixes
      differently sized containers across countries, but no other region
      presents a distortion as severe as unmerged Paris.

The effort metric uses gross salary - the broadest wage concept consistently
      available at NUTS-3 level across all five countries. The exact source differs:
      Portugal uses mean monthly earnings from employer reports (MTSS Quadros de
      Pessoal, "ganho" × 14); Spain uses mean annual salary from tax returns (AEAT);
      France uses gross annual salary in full-time equivalents (INSEE DADS/DSN);
      the Netherlands uses total compensation of employees divided by headcount
      (CBS); and Italy uses mean gross pay from social security records (INPS).

These concepts are not perfectly harmonised. The Dutch figure includes employer
      social contributions, making it broader than the others. The Spanish figure
      comes from tax returns, which may undercount informal or irregular earnings.
      The Portuguese "ganho" includes overtime and regular bonuses beyond base pay.

We use gross rather than net salary because net wages are only published at
      NUTS-3 level in France. For the remaining countries, computing net salary
      would require modelling each country's tax and social security system - which
      varies by household composition, deductions, and (in Spain) by autonomous
      community. Disposable household income is available at NUTS-3 in four of the
      five countries, but not in Italy, and it includes non-work income (pensions,
      transfers, investment), which measures something different from what a worker
      earns. Gross salary, despite its imperfections, is the only metric that
      isolates worker income at the regional level across all five countries.

Outliers are detected in two steps. First, a log-linear model is fitted
      to all points (effort against ln(density)). The residuals of this initial
      fit are then tested using the interquartile range (IQR): points whose
      residual falls more than 1.5 × IQR below the first quartile or above the
      third quartile are flagged as outliers. The regression is then refitted
      without them. This is a standard non-parametric method that does not assume
      normally distributed residuals, and it is applied independently within each
      country so that cross-country differences in wage or price concepts do not
      influence which points are flagged.

Despite the comparability limitations described
      [above](https://nuts.logical.li/#appendix-comparability), the single-variable
      fits are strong within each country. When countries are combined, the
      Portugal + Spain and Portugal + Spain + France subsets offer a good balance
      between sample size and methodological similarity: the Iberian pair shares
      a similar housing market structure and data tradition, while adding France
      extends the sample with a well-documented price source (notarial DVF data)
      and a comparable wage concept. In both combinations the role of the
      outliers identified in the individual Portuguese and Spanish fits is
      confirmed with higher statistical confidence thanks to the larger sample.

The two-variable model uses a logarithmic term for density and a linear
      term for tourism intensity. The logarithm captures the expectation that
      density's contribution to housing effort has diminishing returns: workers
      who pay for housing, whether through mortgages or rents that ultimately
      reflect purchase prices, are constrained by their wages and by
      credit rules. After the 2008 financial crisis, banking regulation tightened
      debt-to-income limits across Europe, not primarily to protect borrowers
      from high effort, but to protect the banking system from systemic risk.
      These constraints act as a ceiling: as density grows, effort cannot grow
      without bound. A logarithm approximates this saturating behaviour.

Tourism intensity, by contrast, enters linearly. The demand that tourism
      brings to a housing market can be inelastic to price: if a region reaches
      full accommodation capacity and prices rise, the visitor profile may shift
      to higher-paying segments rather than the volume declining. The supply of
      housing is locally rigid, but the pool of potential visitors is drawn from
      a global market whose purchasing power is large relative to any single
      region's stock, especially when that purchasing power is time-shared
      across the same property, as in short-term holiday rentals.

As an empirical check, we fitted the alternative model
      effort = a · ln(density) + b · ln(tourism) + c, replacing the linear
      tourism term with a logarithmic one. The R² was lower for four of
      five countries and for the pooled sample: the linear tourism term
      provides a better fit, consistent with the argument above.

Part one uses two Eurostat EU-SILC indicators. The first, ilc_mded01,
      reports total housing costs as a share of disposable household income.
      Housing costs are defined as rent (for tenants) or mortgage interest (for
      owners), utilities, structural insurance, mandatory services, maintenance,
      and housing-related taxes.
      Mortgage principal repayment is
      [explicitly excluded](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=EU_statistics_on_income_and_living_conditions_(EU-SILC)_methodology_-_economic_strain_linked_to_dwelling)
      on the grounds that it constitutes saving rather than expenditure.
      This means the indicator captures the interest component of a mortgage
      payment but not the principal component, systematically understating
      the cash-flow burden for homeowners with active mortgages.

The second indicator, ilc_mded04, is a subjective measure: households
      report whether housing costs represent a heavy burden, some burden, or
      no burden. For the five countries in this analysis, the subjective ranking
      (NL least burdened, ES/IT most burdened) contradicts the calculated one
      (NL most burdened, PT least). The divergence illustrates how the exclusion
      of mortgage principal and the averaging of very different tenure structures
      can produce a calculated indicator that points in the opposite direction
      of lived experience.
