# Has the Independent Print Scene Outgrown Expozine?

The 25th edition of Expozine, like every one before it, celebrates the artists who take part and offers a look inside the larger world of Montreal’s independent arts and literary culture.

As means of production shift and more independent media is distributed both on and offline, how do we keep making space for a growing community? And how do we make sure these artifacts remain accessible for future generations?

The fair has a long-running legacy and significant current impact on independent art production and distribution. A behind-the-scenes look at the industry with Expozine’s founder, Louis Rastelli, offers insight into how archival efforts are keeping up with increases in media production.

### A zine, a cigarette machine, and an archive

By the time Expozine took form, years of work by its founders in establishing a network of independent creatives had already been taking place.

In 1996, Rastelli [launched Fish Piss](https://expozine.ca/en/2026/09/01/collective-zines-in-montreal-from-fish-piss-to-niche/). Recently explored in the book [*Why Fish Piss Matters: On the Last Authentic Bohemia*](https://vehiculepress.com/shop/why-fish-piss-matters-on-the-last-authentic-bohemia-by-andy-brown/?v=5435c69ed3bc) by Andy Brown, the zine featured poetry, stories, and comedy by up-and-coming writers of the time like Heather O’Neill and Jonathan Goldstein. The magazine, which was being sold for $3.00 and ran for 11 issues, included mail-in info for contributors’ personal zine networks. It helped connect the local creatives that would contribute to the city’s growing ecosystem of independent culture.

![](https://www.themain.com/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fcdn.sanity.io%2Fimages%2F2edxk5v7%2Fproduction%2F278fe9d074e5bc22742183ba3ae161f292ac83cf-1999x1125.jpg%3Fw%3D1200%26q%3D82%26auto%3Dformat&w=2560&q=75)

*Photograph: Lori Noël*

Following the success of Fish Piss came [DISTROBOTO](https://distroboto.com/about/), a mycelium of defunct cigarette machines refurbished to sell mini art books, crafts, and comics for $2 to $4 a piece. The first machine was set up in Montreal’s [![](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/2edxk5v7/production/49232e765bea03380b91be28e131d67c4ba785fe-765x1020.jpg?w=48&h=48&fit=crop&auto=format)Casa del Popolo](https://www.themain.com/montreal/music-venue/casa-del-popolo) in 2001 and held around 300 items, selling out within a week. More machines soon sprinkled the city. Rastelli, along with local creators Carl Amabili, Billy Mavreas, Chloe Lum, Yannick Desranleau, Keith Jones, and Andy Brown, contributed to the project that was soon recognized by the New York Times and Canadian media. Since its launch, DISTROBOTO machines have distributed more than 100,000 art objects. The machines are still thriving at bars [![](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/2edxk5v7/production/8775e2dbd0602a8d02c36061e89e186c3b7765e3-1733x1155.jpg?w=48&h=48&fit=crop&auto=format)Vices & Versa](https://www.themain.com/montreal/bar/vices-versa) and [![](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/2edxk5v7/production/cceff63b1d50245c1233c3f277cfdd582c33911c-1360x1020.webp?w=48&h=48&fit=crop&auto=format)Le Cheval Blanc](https://www.themain.com/montreal/bar/le-cheval-blanc).

But Rastelli and the team still saw the need for more distribution opportunities for independent artists.

In the 1960s and ‘90s, independent zines, art, and music were distributed via mail. While this built up international networks, works were denied the official preservation of culture that mainstream art benefits from. Independently produced literature and music is not typically catalogued in national archives if it doesn’t come from a recognized publisher or label.

So in 1998, with a dual mandate for [promoting and preserving independent culture, ARCMTL](https://arcmtl.org/en/about/) was established.

### The archive that Expozine built

Seeing the need for more local distribution of independent publishing, ARCMTL took inspiration from events like [Anarchist Fair](https://www.anarchistbookfair.ca/) and Toronto’s Canzine, running from 1995-2024, to establish the first edition of Expozine.

Expozine launched in 2002, allowing independent creatives to showcase books and zines, connecting with one another and the public face to face. It brought together the best of the city’s independent arts. Publisher Derek Webster and [Maisonneuve](https://maisonneuve.org/), a now prominent part of Montreal’s literary milieu, tabled at that very first Expozine with the magazine’s first issue.

Today, the non-profit arts and heritage organization houses a large physical collection of independent literature, comics, music, and media—much of it acquired from the past 24 years of Expozine. The collection also includes historic copies of Beaux Arts, The Montrealer, and other media dating back to the 1920s. And for his contributions and work as ARCMTL director, [Rastelli received the Judy Mappin Community Award](https://qwf.org/prize/judy-mappin-community-award/) from the Quebec Writers Federation in 2019.

![](https://www.themain.com/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fcdn.sanity.io%2Fimages%2F2edxk5v7%2Fproduction%2Fc5138bc167500b7855f19dd711f4a6333539c680-7740x6192.jpg%3Fw%3D1200%26q%3D82%26auto%3Dformat&w=2560&q=75)

But Rastelli makes it clear that it is, and always has been, a team effort. ARCMTL’s calendar of arts programming and events includes [GRANDE MTL](https://grandemtl.art/), the [Montreal Printed Arts Festival (FAIMTL)](https://faimtl.ca/), and [Volume MTL](https://volumemtl.art/). The team behind the scenes consists of a core six to eight people, along with seasonal student staff and interns. That core team is responsible for archiving and digitizing the physical items, on top of applying for 30-40 grants yearly, spread across the programming, to keep the wheels turning.

[Expozine 2026](https://expozine.ca/en/2017/09/29/expozine-home/) will coincide with the launch of an online catalogue of the archives. The new catalogue will make over 10,000 zines browsable online, as well as historic concert posters, tens of thousands of audio and audio-visual recordings from Montreal, and more.

### Search online, read in person

Media can now exist in so many different forms. Discussions of the online catalogue today brings up the question of how and when we archive both digital-only media and the intentionally analog. Because for ARCMTL, it’s not just about sharing the work, but appreciating and preserving the physical item.

Online-only media is important for consuming information. But it begs the question of how this media will be properly archived, knowing that most types of digital archiving tools eventually become obsolete.

With a crop of new physical media practices and a resurgence of reverence for the old, a renewed appreciation for print media returns. “A growing response to the world of digital content and media has been finding refuge in a physical item,” says Rastelli. “A vinyl record, a DVD, a book, a zine.”

![](https://www.themain.com/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fcdn.sanity.io%2Fimages%2F2edxk5v7%2Fproduction%2F1842447e31c656a83e6bb78146c49b8b7e0f19c9-7740x6192.jpg%3Fw%3D1200%26q%3D82%26auto%3Dformat&w=2560&q=75)

A major incentive of returning to physical media is that it allows the artist to control their creative vision and tailor the way the reader experiences their creation: How their eyes move across the page, how the work is taken in, and how the physicality of it adds to the experience overall. It’s difficult to curate that type of experience online.
