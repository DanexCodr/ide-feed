Italy’s Gran Sasso National Laboratory is not a bad place to fret over the state of your career. The lab’s panoramic views of white-capped mountains and endless greenery are nearly inescapable. Physicists fuel their work with sharp espressos, rather than the watery coffee many Americans accept.

The lab also descends into the Gran Sasso mountain, forming one of the world’s largest underground research centers. Nestled beneath 4,600 feet of rock is an array of particle detectors. Thanks to the mountain’s thick skin, these instruments are shielded from [cosmic rays](https://news.uchicago.edu/explainer/what-are-cosmic-rays), the high-energy particles [produced](https://www.space.com/where-do-cosmic-rays-come-from) by black holes, exploding stars and even our own sun. In the quiet depths of this laboratory, physicists hope to pick up on much more elusive particles—including dark matter.

For the past few decades, scientists have been hunting for dark matter, the invisible substance that makes up [85 percent of the matter](https://www.energy.gov/science/doe-explainsdark-matter) in our universe. One primary target has been weakly interacting massive particles, or [WIMPs](https://aether.lbl.gov/www/science/wimp.html)—a hypothesized candidate for dark matter that [passes through normal matter](https://physicalsciences.ucla.edu/in-the-hunt-for-dark-matter-it-is-harder-for-wimps-to-hide/) with barely a trace. Massive underground detectors like those at Gran Sasso are leading the search.

But no one has definitively found a WIMP, so no one is sure that they exist—and challenges to spotting them are building. As detectors get more sensitive, new background signals are beginning to cloud them, reducing the odds of catching WIMPs. Funding is growing more precarious, leaving many wondering how many years this endeavor has left.

When I visited the lab in May, the mood among some of these physicists was muted. “Of course, we were a bit more depressed,” reflects physicist [Paolo Salomone](https://efi.uchicago.edu/people/profile/paolo-salomone/), laughing. Despite his clear enthusiasm for the dark matter hunt, Salomone, a strikingly blue-eyed University of Chicago postdoctoral scholar, knew he was entering a field some might diagnose as terminal.

Then, in September, a pinprick of hope emerged. An American lab also searching for WIMPs released a [preprint paper](https://lz.lbl.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2026/08/LZ_Preprint_260901_Dark_Matter_EFT_Nuclear_Recoil_Search_at_Higher_Energies.pdf) detailing a single—but intriguing—data point, rising above the expected background signals. Maybe, just maybe, it was [dark matter](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/scientists-may-have-finally-detected-a-hint-of-dark-matter-the-mysterious-invisible-stuff-that-holds-the-universe-together-180989440/).

Salomone was on vacation in Corsica when his phone started blowing up. Text notifications from physicist friends streamed in, asking him if he’d seen the news. “I was there on the beach trying to look at my phone, trying to zoom in on the plot,” he says. Watching the faraway excitement, he says, “I felt like I was missing out a lot.”

The finding was not a definite dark matter detection—one data point isn’t nearly enough to count as a discovery. Still, the result spawned [headlines across the globe](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/scientists-may-have-finally-detected-a-hint-of-dark-matter-the-mysterious-invisible-stuff-that-holds-the-universe-together-180989440/), a torrent of preprint papers and newfound hope for many physicists in search of WIMPs.

The question, now, is what’s next. Will this life raft be enough to overcome the field’s growing challenges? And will the few other detectors with similar sensitivity—most notably, one at Gran Sasso—be able to replicate the signal?

Nearly a century ago, Swiss astronomer [Fritz Zwicky](https://www.amnh.org/learn-teach/curriculum-collections/cosmic-horizons-book/fritz-zwicky) noticed a group of galaxies with some moving so quickly that they should have escaped the cluster’s gravity and flown apart. He theorized that some invisible material was gluing them together. Since then, scientists have [accrued more evidence](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/dark-matter/) for this dark matter: stars speeding [precipitously fast](https://www.amnh.org/learn-teach/curriculum-collections/cosmic-horizons-book/vera-rubin-dark-matter) around a galaxy’s rim; unexpectedly strong [gravitational lensing](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-highlights/shining-a-light-on-dark-matter/), in which distant light is bent by extreme gravity.

To explain it all, astrophysicists dreamed up WIMPs in the [1980s](https://cerncourier.com/a/search-for-wisps-gains-momentum/). As far as subatomic particles go, these would be relatively massive. In theory, they glide through space, on occasion lightly interacting with matter. Physicists have a pretty good sense of the density of dark matter scattered throughout our universe, but not much more about it. “It opens the possibility of having a lot of light particles or very few heavy particles,” says Salomone.

Directly detecting WIMPs, then, could clue us into the mass of individual dark matter particles and the number of them floating around, as well as how strongly these particles interact with normal matter. But finding them has been a challenge. Not only are physicists searching through a vast plain of possibilities, but they’re also looking for incredibly rare interactions that require extreme care in detection.

The smallest impurities can anger a detector. To illustrate the point, [Marcello Messina](https://www.gssi.it/people/professors/lectures-physics/item/12461-messina-marcello), the technical coordinator for Gran Sasso’s [XENONnT experiment](https://xenonexperiment.org/), brought up the humble banana. While we don’t tend to think of a banana as radioactive, the fruits are rich in potassium, leading to a tiny shower of particle decays. “A banana can completely destroy our data table,” he said.

Messina was my guide for the day at Gran Sasso, driving me six miles through one of Italy’s longest tunnels and into the mossy interior of the mountain. He led me through the lab’s sterile halls, ending up at his even more sterile detector.

Messina’s XENONnT experiment is a hulking cylinder that holds a chamber of nearly 9.5 tons of xenon—mostly liquid—inside. The hope is that when a WIMP flies into the detector, it jostles one of the large and stable xenon atoms, which would in turn [shoot out](https://xenonexperiment.org/time-projection-chamber/) electrons and a light ray, alerting sensors to the strange particle’s presence.

XENONnT, which has been collecting data for around six years, is the strapping [great-grandchild](https://xenonexperiment.org/history/) of the team’s original 2006 prototype. “XENON, for almost all its life, provided the most sensitive results,” Messina told me. “It was definitely leading the field.” Today, a few more rivals—like the American detector—have emerged.

We walked past another device that looked like a gigantic red Lego: a new detector Salomone is helping to build, [DarkSide-20k](https://darksideexperiment.org/darkside20k/), which will use a chamber of around 55 tons of argon instead of xenon. This enormous detector will reach a similar sensitivity to XENONnT and is set to begin collecting data in 2028.

Despite their materials’ purity, physicists expect some level of background fuzz—mainly radioactive decay products—and predict what that will look like. Sometimes, though, this “background” can behave in unexpected ways, so physicists must have a high bar for what anomalies in their data to take seriously.

Messina showed me a kind of memento mori for physicists: the latest generation of the [DAMA experiment](https://www.lngs.infn.it/en/dama/), which made a claim of dark matter detection that has been dismissed by most of the community.

In 1997, DAMA, which is made up of sodium iodide crystals, [detected](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v18/s117) a signal that varied yearly, swooping in every summer—just as dark matter was expected to do. But while the team has continued to [stand by](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04734) their results, other experiments later ruled this out.

After DAMA’s false alarm, “I think people are just very cautious now,” says [Ina Sarcevic](https://physics.arizona.edu/people/ina-sarcevic), a physicist at the University of Arizona.

Despite the fear of more detector-cried-wolf scenarios, many physicists quickly pulled together theories to explain September’s announcement of a possible detection, Sarcevic says. The signal came out of South Dakota’s [LUX-ZEPLIN](https://www.physics.lbl.gov/lz-dark-matter-experiment/) (LZ) experiment, which is currently the world’s largest WIMP detector.

Physicist [Richard Gaitskell](https://vivo.brown.edu/display/rgaitske), LZ’s spokesperson, has been searching for dark matter for nearly 40 years, starting with a detector “barely bigger than the top of my thumb,” he says. LZ, on the other hand, is a nine-foot-tall cylinder containing around 11 tons of xenon, packed within a 20-foot-tall outer structure.

To take advantage of LZ’s whopping size and sensitivity, Gaitskell says, the team “decided to really aggressively push” beyond the usual search parameters.

The longstanding assumption in these experiments has been that WIMPs swoop in at relatively low energies and bounce off the xenon like billiard balls. In this case, when a WIMP collides with a xenon nucleus, it scatters the atom’s protons and neutrons in what’s called a “coherent” manner, where their resulting energies add up, amplifying the signal. But if WIMPs were to barrel in at higher energies, the particles would scatter in a trickier way to track.

It was the switch to searching for these higher-energy collisions that led to LZ’s unusual detection. The signal also hinted that particles of dark matter would be quite heavy and collide with matter in a different way than many expected. Rather than rebounding like billiard balls, the WIMP may lose some energy (and mass) in the crash, exiting as a different, slimmer form of dark matter.

Notably, this detection couldn’t be explained by known forms of background events, like stray neutrons. The signal, then, could be dark matter, but it may also be a previously unknown type of background.

In announcing this event, Gaitskell says, the team was looking for input from the wider community, as they continue to pore through their own data. And input he did receive.

Amid the excitement and wave of preprint papers, though, many have retained a sense of caution. Messina says he was initially skeptical of the single data point. “The immediate reaction was, ‘Come on, one event?’” he says. But he recognized XENONnT’s potentially crucial role in the next step toward discovery.

Given their detector’s comparable sensitivity, the team is now rushing to replicate the finding, looking through the past two years of their own data for signals that chime with the American detection. Or they could find nothing.

Either way, Messina would like to let the team’s data analysis do the talking, rather than get too excited now. They hope to release a paper in the next few months. “Data matters,” Messina says. “All the rest is fake.”

When Messina led me around in the spring, he wasn’t optimistic about the future of his field. He was continuing to support the search for dark matter with XENONnT, but he had also been devoting time to detecting [neutrinos](https://icecube.wisc.edu/outreach/neutrinos/), another kind of subatomic particle generated by stars, radioactive decay and the Big Bang. “Many people like me are thinking, ‘You know what, I want to investigate different fields,’” he said.

Some WIMP proponents point out that even though the detectors haven’t found one of these fabled particles, they have made a less obvious kind of progress over the decades. In their searches, scientists have [ruled out](https://supercdms.slac.stanford.edu/science-results/dark-matter-limit-plotter) many potential WIMP masses and densities, ticking away at the options and possibly making it easier to zero in on their true properties in the future.

But to many onlookers—including the funding bodies that control cash flow—the lack of clear progress in particle physics has been disappointing. “It’s quite hard to convince politicians to support you if you have a significant discovery each 30 years,” Messina said.

On top of the current fragile science funding ecosystem in the United States, he wasn’t sure how much more time WIMP detection had. He doubted the proposed next-generation detector, [XLZD](https://xlzd.org/)—which would dwarf LZ and XENON and merge the collaborations—would move forward.

After LZ’s finding, though, Messina’s tune has changed. While he still stresses the uncertainty that this signal will lead to a discovery, he believes it has imparted “some momentum” back into WIMPs.

Many physicists still see more promise in other dark matter models—the hypothetical WIMPs might not be the best (or only) explanation.

[Ben Safdi](https://physics.berkeley.edu/people/faculty/benjamin-safdi), a theoretical physicist at the University of California, Berkeley, says that LZ’s detection may well be dark matter, but his initial excitement has waned. At first, the signal seemed to match an existing theory for WIMP behavior, but several preprint papers—including [one of his](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04175v1)—challenged this idea. “My personal belief that it is dark matter has gone down,” he says.

Safdi divides his research between WIMPs and a different dark matter candidate called axions. These theorized particles would move in a “continuous, almost fluid” wave through the universe, he says. Individually, axions would be super light—much less massive than WIMPs—but together, they could [account for the missing mass of dark matter](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-dark-matter-made-of-axions/).

Attention to axions has surged as people have [grown tired of WIMPs](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-dark-matter-might-be-axions-20191127/). While Safdi still thinks the WIMP hunt is worth continuing, “my heart is in axions,” he says.

Did you know? Other strategies to find WIMPs

- Some scientists hope to indirectly detect WIMPs by looking for neutrinos produced when they collide with their proposed antimatter counterpart and annihilate.
- Others are hoping to create WIMPs. According to some theories, WIMPs might be produced by energetic collisions between normal particles.

Even if interest in WIMP detection is revitalized, technological challenges remain. One specter still haunting the field is a fog of neutrinos that’s threatening to descend on the instruments.

With increased sensitivity, the detectors are starting to pick up the constant swarm of neutrinos. These tiny particles don’t interact much with matter—[trillions](https://icecube.wisc.edu/about-us/facts/) move through your body every second—but they can clutter data taken by detectors that are precise enough to notice them. As this [neutrino fog](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/after-decades-of-searching-are-physicists-closing-in-on-dark-matter-180985246/) wafts in, potential WIMP signals could get lost in the haze. XENONnT [reported](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17868) its first glimpses of the fog in 2024.

This isn’t a huge problem for the detectors yet, and not everyone agrees it will be soon. But once neutrinos start meaningfully gumming up the signal, scientists don’t know what they’ll do.

[Ronald Walsworth](https://ece.umd.edu/clark/faculty/1205/Ronald-Walsworth)’s group is experimenting with a workaround: tracing the direction of incoming particles. WIMPs are expected to flood in like a “headwind” against the solar system’s path through the galaxy, he says. On the other hand, a lot of neutrinos emanate from the direction of [the sun](http://neutrinos.fnal.gov/sources/solar-neutrinos/), produced by the churn of fusion.

A sea of liquid xenon functions “like an antenna,” says [Daniel Ang](https://danielang.net/), a quantum scientist in Walsworth’s group. The flash of light only clues scientists into a particle’s collision—not the direction it rode in. So to trace a signal’s source, physicists might have to take a whole new approach.

Walsworth decided to try this by using a more glamorous substance: [diamonds](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024APS..DMPD00136L/abstract). From his previous work, he knew that when particles cut through diamond crystals, they leave behind a finely detailed track—so their incoming direction is clear. “That’s the grandiose idea we had,” says Walsworth, a quantum physicist at the University of Maryland.

But the team has only tested their blinged-out detector on a small scale, and getting the funds to go bigger will be a challenge—especially given the “sagging of enthusiasm” for WIMPs, Walsworth says.

Once Salomone, the postdoc, got back from vacation, his excitement about LZ’s signal was slightly tempered by his boss, he says. “He was, of course, more skeptical and less enthusiastic than me.”

But even among Salomone’s liquid argon detector community, scientists are paying serious attention to the result. His collaboration has a regular meeting, which only one or two dozen people usually show up to. But at a recent one, some 100 people attended. Even people who never participated were interjecting with ideas, he says.

Salomone’s workdays are much like before, continuing to help build the DarkSide-20k detector, placing orders for special screws and popping over to the café for an espresso. But he feels a renewed excitement about the theory behind his daily tasks.

“You’re still doing what you were doing before but with a different mind-set,” he says. “Spending more time trying to read papers, spending more time trying to be even a better physicist, so that’s what I’m really happy about.”
