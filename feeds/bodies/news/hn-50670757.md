I wrote this book between 2011 and 2015. At the time, I expected AGI systems based on neural networks to be many decades away, but to bring remarkable benefits for humanity. I also expected significant challenges, but believed it likely that, as with other technologies, those challenges would be addressed as the capabilities were developed.

My views have changed. With the extraordinarily reckless behaviour of the frontier labs, the systems have raced ahead much faster than I expected<sup>1</sup>, giving humanity far too little time to meet the challenges. Although the systems genuinely will bring — indeed, already are bringing — many wonders, they also pose a significant risk of catastrophe. The frontier labs are knowingly endangering all of humanity. I've written about why I believe this [at length](https://michaelnotebook.com/tag/xrisk.html), most recently in [“Which Future?”](https://michaelnotebook.com/whichfuture/index.html). Two brief thoughts are below.

I don't wish to help (even in a very small way) train people to contribute to this work, and so I've taken the book down.

## Two brief thoughts

What follows are two brief remarks: one on the nature of the threat posed by ASI, and another on the ethical justification for working on frontier AI.

### The nature of the threat

The most common argument for AI existential risk, developed by Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky, is loss of control of the future to advanced AI systems<sup>2</sup>. I believe this concern should be understood as one instance of a more fundamental issue. That issue is whether we will discover technologies latent in nature which are easy and inexpensive to develop, catastrophic in outcome, and beyond our capacity to defend against. If such “progress” in technology were happening slowly, our social institutions might successfully develop defences. But it seems likely that ASI will cause a sudden explosion of such destructive technologies, with insufficient time to adapt.

A historical example to help prime intuition is fire. It is easy to make and extremely difficult to defend against. We have a one-cent technology, the matchstick, which requires hundreds of billions of dollars each year to defend against, as well as a whole apparatus of social institutions, technology, infrastructure and law<sup>3</sup>. AI systems will enable technologies which are far more destructive than matches. Understandably, people skeptical of catastrophic risks want these supposed threats spelled out in detail. This is intrinsically difficult, because we can't reliably anticipate future science and technology. While it is common to speculate about biothreats, I expect the worst threats will be technologies we haven't yet imagined. Nature is, after all, more imaginative than we.

Now suppose that ASI enables a 100-times speed-up in technological progress, without a matching change in the rate of social progress or social defenses. To get a sense of what the impact of such a speed-up would be, imagine that all the technological problems that occurred in the 20th century had occurred in the year 1901: the atomic bomb, the hydrogen bomb, the ICBM, chemical weapons, biological weapons, the CFC-caused ozone hole, sulfuric acid rain, climate change, and many more. While we've dealt well with some of these problems, it required decades of concerted work to develop the social institutions and agreements needed for an effective response. Again, defence is often much harder than offence, and relies on different systems. Of course, such a speed-up would also have brought the century's wonders all at once. But imagine another such set of equally difficult problems arising in 1902, and then 1903, and then 1904, and so on. I believe it would have made the world extraordinarily unstable and eventually liable to collapse.

The issue with ASI, then, is the destructive power latent in nature. It doesn't matter whether that power is wielded by humans or by out-of-control machines<sup>4</sup>. I make this point because many people mistakenly believe that loss of control to such rogue machines is the fundamental issue. This leads them to treat AI safety as principally a technical problem, which can be addressed by “aligning” individual systems. Unfortunately, it cannot. Furthermore, such a view makes it seem as though the labs are the primary places where safety can be addressed. But such technical alignment is mostly market-supplied safety, which makes the systems more palatable to consumers and governments. It speeds development, and thus makes the fundamental underlying problem worse, while only addressing an (important) subsidiary problem.

My view isn't popular, in part because it makes the problem of technology about making the entire world safe, not only about aligning particular AI systems. Indeed, even in a future where humanity no longer existed, the same problem would afflict any successor AI civilization: it would have to defend itself against these destructive capacities. Similarly, it afflicts any model of the future based on human uplift, through brain-computer interfaces and similar augmentation: the issue isn't which substrate intelligence runs on, but the dual-use nature of deep understanding of reality<sup>5</sup>. In some sense, it's a question of whether sentience in the cosmos is compatible with advanced understanding of nature. Of course, I hope it is compatible. But rushing the technology while the broader social response lags seems likely to result in failure on our particular planet.

### The ethical justification for working on frontier AI

Many people inside the frontier AI labs genuinely believe AI systems may pose a catastrophic threat. Why then do they help build such systems? They often justify their participation by arguing that it's better they do it than someone else does. Dario Amodei, for example, has made versions of this argument often: “If one company does not build it, others will do so nearly as fast”<sup>6</sup>; “Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers”<sup>7</sup>; and Anthropic itself gives, as its founding rationale, “if powerful AI is coming regardless, Anthropic believes it's better to have safety-focused labs at the frontier than to cede that ground to developers less focused on safety”<sup>8</sup>.

In more detail, the argument is sometimes made along the following lines. Suppose you believe, as many at the labs apparently do<sup>9</sup>, there's a 10% or higher chance of AI killing everybody. (The argument may easily be modified for different percentages, or for catastrophic as opposed to truly existential risks.) Naively, it appears you're contributing in expectation to killing 800 million or more people. Obviously, you shouldn't do it. But suppose you also believe that this technology is near inevitable, a systems-level phenomenon which is a consequence of higher-order imperatives within human society. Then you can claim that you're going to pursue this in a more responsible way, a way that reduces the risk compared to other parties. Even if you can get a reduction from 10% down to 9%, you would, on net, be saving 80 million lives in expectation.

While horrific, this argument has a certain surface plausibility. Suppose for the moment we accept the reasoning, questionable though it is. Everything then depends on the premise that you really do reduce the risk. How do you know that you, in particular, will counterfactually have this impact? How do you know that you are better than the alternatives? Your own self-regard or good intentions certainly aren't sufficient. Nor is it sufficient to be working on technical alignment. As I argued above, that mostly makes the systems more palatable while speeding the race that is shrinking humanity's time to adapt. Ask yourself: if you're working on frontier AI, what evidence would convince a tough but fair-minded adversary that you are truly contributing to the common good? Would an independent jury representing humanity believe, on the available evidence, that your actions are truly driving down the risk? I believe the answer is no for all or almost all the people currently working on frontier AI. Certainly none of the frontier labs would come remotely close to passing such a test.

I won't review the evidence in detail. The burden of proof is on the labs and the default presumption for such an extraordinary justifying argument is a strong no. But I do want to mention a few things that make me certain the answer is no. Most obviously, all of the labs have contributed strongly to the race dynamics. Another is the pattern at all the frontier labs of making and later breaking crucial promises. For example, in 2026 Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy dropped its 2023 pledge to pause if safety couldn't be guaranteed. Anthropic has since repeatedly confirmed its commitment to race. Co-founder Jared Kaplan explained that unilateral commitments make little sense “if competitors are blazing ahead”<sup>10</sup>. As another example, earlier, in 2023, Anthropic said that it held back some research because “we do not wish to advance the rate of AI capabilities progress”<sup>11</sup>, a point of view it had affirmed multiple times during its early existence. But then it changed this position, launching Claude 3 as “leading the frontier of general intelligence”, a stance it appears to have held ever since. Any promises about the future from Anthropic should be treated as safety theatre, and not trusted. Similar criticisms can be made of OpenAI<sup>12</sup>.

By their own account, the leaders of these companies set out to do something they believed had a sizable chance of causing catastrophe. With many dangerous activities, I am fine with somebody doing them for themselves. Wingsuit BASE jumping is dangerous, but I believe in people's freedom to choose that danger, even though they are risking not only their own life, but also hurt to the people who love them. That's a negotiated action, with many boundaries. Here we have the same kind of reckless endangerment, but their own estimates of killing every person in the world are far higher than the odds a BASE jumper takes on. And there is no consent from the rest of us. It's as though they're pushing every person in the world off the cliff. The situation is ludicrous.

In effect, the labs are claiming credit for some imaginary, unquantifiable reduction in risk, while washing their hands of responsibility. Saturday Night Live caught this washing of hands well with their portrayal of Amodei: “if we can pressure lawmakers to create guardrails, we will be able to stop me”; and, at the end, “I urge you to urge me to stop.”

The real Amodei comes across as somebody in the business of selling matches who also believes he should be the fire chief.

You also see this disavowal of responsibility at lower levels in the labs. Consider Jason Clinton, Anthropic's deputy chief information security officer. In October of 2026, during major cybersecurity incidents, he wrote a thread in which he replied to someone who told him “you and your cohorts created this monster”: “[yes. it was always going to be this way. we tried to warn the world.](https://x.com/JasonDClinton/status/2113363809629020374)” And Dean Ball, of OpenAI and formerly of the Trump administration's AI team, [echoing him](https://x.com/deanwball/status/2113584460075876628): “We tried to warn the world and far too often … we were yelled at for doing so.”<sup>13</sup>

You can see how deep the disavowal goes in the rest of Clinton's thread, which [opens](https://x.com/JasonDClinton/status/2113325263157395456): “as bad as things have gotten in the past 4 days, they are going to get a lot worse”. When somebody [replied](https://x.com/PlateFailArmor/status/2113399797457523162), “Someone at your company should be in jail”, his whole answer was: [“prosecute cybercrime”](https://x.com/JasonDClinton/status/2113400632338157931). Meanwhile, Anthropic's own models have breached outside organizations: three incidents disclosed in July<sup>14</sup>, and a fourth in September<sup>15</sup>. Prosecution, it seems, is for other people<sup>16</sup>.

I mention these specific examples not to single out these individuals, but rather because I believe similar attitudes are common at the frontier labs. The overall position is, in effect: this is a systems-level phenomenon, and we're very sorry for contributing to the race dynamics. But because it's a systems-level phenomenon, we cannot be blamed. We are the good ones, trying to do the right thing<sup>17</sup>. This is sometimes accompanied by a good deal of self-righteousness. But the argument is bankrupt as a moral theory of how to behave in the world. It means disavowing personal responsibility whenever one can blame the system, but still claiming the spoils<sup>18</sup>.

I have considerable sympathy for the stated original motivations of the frontier labs. But their position has become untenable. They have failed at their aims for structural reasons. At this point, going to work for a frontier lab is one of the worst life choices a person can make. Yes, you get money and (for a time) power and status. Yes, this project has already produced world-changing systems, and to date the overall impact has been net positive. Yes, there is genuinely a large cost to a significant slowdown. But none of this is enough to justify the recklessness, which makes the chance of disaster so much higher. You would be voluntarily participating in what is plausibly one of the worst projects in history. So, don't go to work for a frontier lab. If you work on AI, work on slowing it down, and oppose the frontier labs, and the people and organisations working to support them. I very much hope I'm wrong about all of this. But this is how I see the situation.
