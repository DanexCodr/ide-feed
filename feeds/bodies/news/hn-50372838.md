Edward Hopper had certainly started *Nighthawks, *his iconic vision of a sparsely populated New York diner aglow with artificial light, by late November 1941. When Arnold Newman photographed him in front of the empty canvas that month, Hopper had clearly decided the picture’s dimensions; he had also probably completed many of the nineteen preparatory sketches in both pencil and chalk, several of which featured numeric notes detailing how to scale up the composition to its final form.

As winter set in, Hopper made numerous excursions, trekking up to the Dixie Kitchen diner in Midtown to sketch the coffee urns, as well as salt and pepper shakers, cups, and napkin dispensers. For the figures, Hopper modeled himself for the two suited men using a full-length mirror, while Jo modeled for the auburn woman clad in burgundy. The blond barman Edward dreamed up from scratch, though Jo recognized this character as “practically Capt. Ed Staples,” suggesting once again the resurgence of Hopper’s painterly alter ego.

A gesture of endurance through the nation’s darkest hours, *Nighthawks *celebrates a poignantly American resilience.

Edward later stated that the diner itself was first “suggested by a restaurant on Greenwich Avenue where two streets meet,” adding that “*Nighthawks *seems to be the way I think of a night street.” A *Vogue *article claimed in 1954 that the picture was “based partly on an all night coffee stand Hopper saw on Greenwich Avenue in downtown New York, only more so.” he certainty that the source of *Nighthawks *once existed on Greenwich Avenue has led to many rounds of detective work to identify Hopper’s famous diner. Wedge shaped establishments at 70 Greenwich Avenue—a newsstand in Hopper’s day and now a florist—and a 1940s restaurant named Riker’s at the junction of West Fourth Street and Seventh Avenue South, where the Hoppers frequently dined, have been identified as potential sources.

No consensus exists, though the most likely origin was identified by Carter Foster in 2013 as a long gone establishment called Crawford Lunch, which once stood where Greenwich Avenue meets Twelfth Street, just north of the Sheridan Theatre. The existence of the word “Lunch” on the diner window in one of Hopper’s final studies for *Nighthawks *lends credence to this theory, as does the distinctive shape of the premises.

Many details of Hopper’s diner differ, however, not least the large, curved glass-plated window in *Nighthawks *that lends the painting its iconic fishbowl effect. The advertisement for $5 Phillies American cigars crowning the cornice and the diner’s sleek, art deco design were also missing from these suggested sources. This has led to credible suggestions that Hopper amalgamated details of several establishments in combination with imagined elements. The long, seamless window of the diner has not been identified as a feature of any historic restaurant on Greenwich Avenue, suggesting the possibility that Hopper derived this element from the renowned glass-fronted prow of the famous Flatiron Building, which once housed a cigar store. If so, Hopper’s intent was surely to create a stylized diner with a distinctly New York shape and feel.

Indeed, the overwhelming Americanness of *Nighthawks *has long been felt and observed: from the aquarium style architecture to the artist’s inclusion of an ad reading “America’s no. 1 Cigar”; from the presence of cigarettes and coffee to the hardboiled, fedora clad “nighthawks” themselves. Beyond its dramatic aesthetic—masterfully conjured through a utilitarian approach to light, color, and form—the painting’s mood and substance feel palpably American in a manner that countless writers have spilled acres of ink on, in attempts to explain its singular aura.

It is important to return to the original contexts of *Nighthawks *to plumb its depths. The work was produced during a period of extraordinary existential tension across the United States and specifically in New York. Though *Nighthawks *was planned in the weeks before the devastating attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, the event had shaken America to the core, creating a dramatic new context for the painting. The United States was finally dragged into the Second World War, resulting in nightly blackouts and very real concerns about the bombing of their cities. New York was a primary enemy target, a worry expressed by Jo in a letter to Marion Hopper on December 17:

Ed refused to take any interest in our very likely prospect of being bombed—and we live right under glass skylights and a roof that leaks whenever it rains. He refuses to make any more precautions and only jeers at me for packing a knapsack with towels and keys and soap and check book, shirt, stockings, garters—in case we ran to race out doors in our nighties. For the blackout we have no shade over the sky light—so can’t have lights in studio at all—so no way to cook even.

Jo continued that, amid the blackouts and trepidation, Edward had become particularly obsessive about his new painting:

He’s doing a new canvas and simply can’t be interrupted! he Rehn gallery invites E to remove some of his pictures to a store house so that the whole collection won’t be in one place. Frank Rehn is very concerned and making many precautionary measures. I can’t say I’m a bit panicy but I’m the kind that believes in precautions, and in a matter that everyone is so concerned in, I can’t see why anyone refuses to take an interest. Hitler has said he intends to destroy New York and Washington. It’s certainly indicated that he will try. We’re right close up to that center of Jewry, the NY University. We should have heavy curtains for front of house to keep glass from smashing in all over the floor—but with glass overhead, what good would they do. It takes over a month for E to finish a canvas and this one is only just begun…E won’t now discuss any emergency  Surely, this is the time to think things out…But E. doesn’t want me even in the studio…Can it be that E. Hopper senses in his bones the admonition: “Be still and know that I am God!”

Painting continued for over a month. On January 22, 1942, Jo revealed that Edward had “just finished a very fine picture—a lunch counter at night with 3 figures. Night Hawks would be a fine name for it. He was about a month and a half working on it—interested all the time, too busy to get excited over public outrages. So, we stay out of fights.”

The extraordinary extent of Edward’s absorption in *Nighthawks *suggests that the painting was a product of exceptional, almost obsessive focus, with little of the typical delays or doubts plaguing its genesis. It seems clear that the anxiety of war facilitated the conditions for such sustained labor, *Nighthawks *providing not only distraction from pressing worldly concerns but also a channel for Hopper’s private thoughts and fears. The environment of wartime New York rings out silently but urgently from *Nighthawks; *as a temple of fluorescent light persisting through the infernal darkness, Hopper’s diner figuratively and symbolically resists the blackouts imposed upon the city. A gesture of endurance through the nation’s darkest hours, *Nighthawks *celebrates a poignantly American resilience.

Through stoicism, *Nighthawks *spins the dark side of American life as *cool; *its quiet menace is slick and alluring, and its lonesome, shadowy characters are iconic.

Even beyond its historical context, *Nighthawks *evinces hidden depths, its psychological and emotional properties endlessly arresting. Notions of loneliness and alienation are routinely attached to the painting, *Nighthawks *now deemed by many to be a totemic ode to the isolation of modern life. Even before the work, these themes had gained currency among interpreters of Hopper’s work, one critic in 1940 having praised the artist’s ability to evoke “the pathos of the big city, the loneliness of the individual in an impersonal setting.” Years later, Edward himself offered that he “didn’t see it as particularly lonely. I simplified the scene a great deal and made the restaurant bigger. Unconsciously, probably, I was painting the loneliness of a large city.”

The way Hopper reverted to the workings of his subconscious for an explanation of his intent shows how far he had internalized Freudian conceptions surrounding repressed emotions. Still, this did not stop him from railing against those who too liberally used loneliness as a generalizing description of his work. “The loneliness thing is overdone,” he told Brian O’Doherty. “It formulates something you don’t want formulated.” For Hopper, words alone were too boundaried, too concrete to describe an oceanic emotional world. As he later put it: “If you could say it in words, there would be no reason to paint.”

*Nighthawks *poses a series of questions and references that together offer the viewer traces of meaning. Commentators have identified in *Nighthawks *potentially telling cultural sources, including the artist’s early illustrations of dining scenes and paintings of night cafés by artists including Van Gogh and Degas, such as Degas’s despondent painting *Absinthe, *from 1876. Others have seen in the mystery of *Nighthawks *the continuation of a surrealist vein in Hopper’s mature work, citing New York exhibitions of Joan Miró’s and Salvador Dalí’s work in November 1941 as influences, as well as the ongoing impact of Giorgio de Chirico’s paintings. Critics, too, would increasingly note the surrealist undertones in Hopper’s painting; one year after *Nighthawks*’ completion a writer stated that “he has the psychological insights of the best of the ‘Surrealists’ without their circus methods.”

Although Hopper’s relationships with impressionism, postimpressionism, and surrealism are noteworthy, and Van Gogh’s *Café Terrace at Night *(1888) is an arrestingly comparable exploration of the evocative effects of outdoor urban lighting at a night café, the sheer Americanness of *Nighthawks *places that picture within a national cultural lineage that has generally been overlooked in favor of European models. Paintings of urban dining were commonplace among the Ashcan portfolios of John Sloan and Robert Henri, the latter whose mysterious nocturnes of outdoor night cafés seem particularly relevant. Everett Shinn’s *All Night Café *(c. 1900), like *Nighthawks, *portrays the outside of a lit restaurant on a dark night, though it includes a lonely down-and-out figure gazing through the windows of the busy establishment.

In Hopper’s *Nighthawks, *the viewer becomes this isolated onlooker, peering in at the fishbowl from the deserted street with no discernible doorway to access this haven of light. As in former works, meanwhile, Hopper’s diner is streamlined, simplified, the world around it emptied to its essentials, adding to its dreamlike quality. Continuing his exploration of the mystery and poetry of night cafés from earlier paintings such as *Soir Bleu, *in *Nighthawks *Hopper reimagines this trope in a singularly American environment, bridging impressionism and surrealism with a national realist tradition and his own transcendentalist philosophical inflections.

It is the painting’s unmistakably American setting and aesthetic that elevates it beyond a mere evocation of loneliness, rooting it in historic ideas about national identity. Specifically, it emanates that heroic sense of isolation, which to generations of audiences has denoted something exceptional about the American condition. Bringing Emersonian individualism into the contemporary urban arena, Hopper conveys self reliance as a constant, uniting his traditional values with a profound modernity. Through stoicism, *Nighthawks *spins the dark side of American life as *cool; *its quiet menace is slick and alluring, and its lonesome, shadowy characters are iconic.
