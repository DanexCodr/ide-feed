Students are abandoning traditional computer science courses, choosing instead dedicated AI tracks and core engineering fields. We look at why this is happening and what it actually means. As explored back in July 2025 in _[Computer Science Under Threat](<https://www.i-programmer.info/news/150-training-a-education/18147-computer-science-challenged-to-change-.html>)_ , and revisited last month in [ _Computer Science Enrollment In Decline_](<https://www.i-programmer.info/Computer%20Science%20Enrollment%20In%20Decline>), traditional computer science undergraduate enrollments have dwindled. The comes after a two-decades in which we saw record-breaking levels of Computer Science enrollments, fueled by high starting salaries and an industry appetite for entry-level coders. The cause, as we've already explored, is that, in the era of AI, the tech industry has shifted away from mass hiring of junior developers for routine coding; such tasks are increasingly handed over to AI. Looking at the choices that are now being made by students there's a divergence. Some are opting traditional branches of engineering. The logic here is that physical-world engineering, hardware integration, and complex systems design are less vulnerable to displacement by LLMs. A mechanical engineer working on automated robotics or smart grid infrastructure still needs to interact with the physical world, a constraint that insulates them, at least until the robots come along, from automation. On the other hand, enrollments in specialized AI and data-centric tracks are surging as students attempt to keep abreast of the technology. Students are opting for courses in machine learning and neural architectures. Rather than choosing abstract software paths, incoming students choosing dedicated curricula. A prime example is MIT’s **Artificial Intelligence and Decision Making,** which integrates machine learning, perception, optimization, and cognitive science directly into the core undergraduate curriculum, ignoring standard generic programming components.. Seeing students bypass general computer science out of fear of AI-driven coding agents tends to raise fears about the future of the software industry. Are we scaring away the future architects of our digital infrastructure just because we can pass over trivial tasks to chatbots? Or could this be a healthy and long-overdue market correction away from traditional comp sci.  For years, the generic computer science degree functioned as a catch-all "pipeline" for tech employment. It drew in many students whose primary incentive was a fast-tracked, well-paid software job rather than an intrinsic fascination with computational theory, operating systems, or software architecture. If generative AI has successfully automated the lowest rungs of routine coding, it has also broken the illusion that simply learning a programming language equals a guaranteed career.  What about the universities? Traditional computer science curricula, overly reliant on introductory programming courses, risk becoming obsolete if they don't evolve. Equally, we need education to help mitigate the threats posed by AI; computer science faculties must offer courses about how to dominate AI, rather than be dominated by it.
