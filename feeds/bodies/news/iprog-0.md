Robots have made incredible advances thanks to reinforcement learning. See the Unitree G1 vault barriers, climb ladders, and more, in a manner fit for the real world. At I Programmer, we've been following the evolution of humanoid robot mobility. Looking back to the early milestone of the DARPA Robotics Challenge, robots were still learning to stand upright without falling over, although we did see [Atlas Walking Over Randomness](<https://www.i-programmer.info/news/169-robotics/6632-atlas-walking-over-randomness.html>) very slowly and falling over before completing the course. Atlas continued to make progress with mobility, impressing us with its ability to walk over uneven ground and to do both dance and parkour even before it made the transition from hand-coded control systems to reinforcement learning in 2024-25.  Meanwhile the Chinese made a big push into robotics and in 2025 hosted the first half marathon in which robots raced against humans. In that event only only 6 of 20 robot entrants finished the course and the fastest time recorded was 2 hours and 40 minutes, well over twice the time of the men's winner of the race who had a time of 1 hour and 2 minutes. Fast forward to 2026, when 300 robots competed, and several robots outpaced the fastest human runners. The three winning robots had times that were 15 minutes faster than the winning human. For this second event about 40% of the robots used fully autonomous navigation and stability was also much improved. However there was evidence that speed was being optimized at the expense of other factors and that the robots competition performance would not generalize to "normal" situations. Now the Robot Systems Lab at ETH Zurich has introduced a new approach to training humanoid robots for deployment in real world scenarios which it presents in its latest research paper, _**[Humanoid Mobility via Attention-based Contacts](<https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31608v1>)** ,_ posted on arXiv and also presented in this video:  VIDEO Let's demystify the jargon of the paper's title. The [paper](<https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31608v1>) starts by outlining the challenges involved in achieving "_**humanoid mobility**_ ": _Deploying humanoid robots in human-centric environments requires capabilities well beyond stable bipedal walking. Traversing cluttered and geometrically constrained environments often demands stepping across sparse footholds, climbing onto obstacles, crawling through confined spaces, or leveraging the upper body for support. These multicontact behaviors fundamentally diverge from conventional locomotion. Instead of adhering to a prescribed pattern of foot contacts, the robot must continuously determine the spatial, temporal, and anatomical distribution of its environmental interactions._ The project's [GitHub repo](<https://leggedrobotics.github.io/ACP/index.html>) is where we find how the Unitree G1 learns the skills required to move in the physical world through "_**contacts**_ ". summarizing it as: _13.2 minutes of human motion teach the robot where its hands, feet, knees, elbows and pelvis should meet the world. Reinforcement learning fills in the rest of the body. At deployment, the reference is gone: the robot plans its own contacts from a LiDAR height map and a goal, with 1 policy for every skill._ "_**Attention-based**_ " refers to the use of a neural network attention mechanism (similar to the self-attention used in modern Transformer models) to dynamically weigh and select the most relevant environmental features and temporal cues for predicting physical contacts. Its key mechanisms are

  * Weighing Spatial and Temporal Context: As the humanoid robot moves, its onboard sensors take in terrain data. The attention mechanism allows the planner to dynamically "attend to" the critical parts of the terrain (such as a stepping stone, a ledge, or a flat patch of ground) while ignoring irrelevant background noise.
  * Predicting Future Contacts: The model uses attention to correlate what the robot currently senses with future contact events—determining precisely when and where a foot or limb needs to make contact with the terrain to maintain balance and momentum.
  * Flexible Information Flow: Instead of rigidly following a predetermined motion trajectory, the attention mechanism lets the network decide how much importance to assign to different data inputs on the fly, allowing the robot to adapt its stepping strategy seamlessly when faced with unexpected obstacles.

The novel approach introduced in this project centers on treating physical contact as a unified physical abstraction rather than trying to map full-body joint trajectories or rely on rigid motion references at runtime. Key aspects of this novel methodology include:

  * Using Motion Data Exclusively as a Training Prior: Instead of forcing the robot to mimic reference motion capture data line-by-line during deployment, which often breaks down when facing unexpected obstacles, the framework uses human motion clips strictly to teach the system the structure of natural interactions during training. At deployment, no reference inputs are needed. 
  * Contact-Centric Interface (9 Body Parts): The system decomposes complex whole-body movements (walking, climbing, crawling, and balancing) into a generalized contact plan across nine distinct body parts. By predicting where and when these contact points interact with the environment, it gives the underlying control policy room to figure out the rest of the body's posture via reinforcement learning. 
  * Self-Supervised Attention Mechanism: An attention-based planner processes onboard sensor data (such as LiDAR-derived elevation maps) to dynamically select and evaluate relevant footholds and surfaces. This prevents the robot from generating physically impossible trajectories (like stepping into empty space) and allows it to adapt seamlessly to sparse, challenging terrains. 
  * Emergent Goal-Conditioned Multi-Skill Behavior: Because the framework separates the high-level contact plan from low-level execution, a single generalist policy can autonomously branch into completely different behaviors based purely on user-specified goals. For instance, when facing the same obstacle box, the robot will step up, use its hands to climb, crawl underneath, or turn laterally depending entirely on the target command, without needing manual state machines. 

So now RSL has tweaked reinforcement learning in a way that both reduces initial training and makes learning more adaptable, showing an approach that does have potential for real-world deployment.
