How can you get people to remember to carry out regular maintenance tasks - emptying a trash can or watering a plant? One possible answer is by harnessing the power of Tamagotchis.  Students from Carnegie Mellon University have published a toolkit called Object-Agent Twins (OATs) that can be used to create Tamagotchi-like companions for inanimate everyday objects, giving them a personality to encourage people to look after them better.  Those of you old enough to have accidentally killed your Tamagotchi back in the 90s will know the power of apparent emotions in inanimate objects. The idea of the Tamagotchi was that you "hatched" a virtual egg and raised the character through life stages by feeding meals and snacks (or forgetting to feed it then noticing too late that it had died), cleaning up waste, giving medicine, and teaching it to be a good - er - person by disciplining or praising it. The startling success at the time of virtual pets such as Tamagotchi showed that people will treat inanimate objects as though they are sentient, even when the object expresses only sparse needs and personalities. Such 'pets' don't need to be realistic, they get the attention and affection through the use of lightweight, socially intelligible cues that invite repeated acts of attention and caregiving.  In the experiment by Carnegie Mellon, OATs were used to allow non-expert users to easily create small personality-bearing agents tied to real-world artifacts such as a tea kettle, pet bowl, or a trash can. So if you forget to refill the water filter, you'll see a sad little face, and if you add enough water, it will change to be happy and even excited.  The idea was dreamed up by Daehwa Kim, a fifth-year PhD student in Carnegie Mellon's Future Interfaces Group. Kim presented the project at the ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology in Detroit earlier this month.  The OATs use sensors and make the information available to the toolkit to use to present simple needs that can also be expressed as moods or behaviors. By exposing the data as though it was emotions from a character, the information is made more "socially legible and emotionally engaging".  The paper in the ACM says this approach means everyday objects are treated as lightweight companions that deserve to receive attention and care. Using the toolkit, users can define what makes an object "happy" or "unhappy." Google Gemini then interprets the sensor readings to work out what emotion the object is feeling, from a range of 12 emotional options. These are then rendered with image generation from Gemini's Nano Banana service. The modules can track moisture, temperature, humidity, light, occupancy, motion, and sound levels.  The web-based client interface allows users to create, modify, and access their OATs. The backend server is hosted in the cloud and implemented in Python using the FastAPI framework. The server is responsible for data storage and management. It queries Gemini models for avatar generation, behavior generation, and emotion reasoning, displays current OAT status and reason, and manages synchronization between the web interface and the OAT client hardware. The OAT client device communicates with the server over HTTP. Whenever a user updates the OAT information, such as new sensors, behaviors, or avatar appearance, the server marks such updates so that OAT can download the latest changes. The client device also sends sensor data to the server, where Gemini performs emotion reasoning and returns the resulting emotion.  OATs are currently designed for single object use, but the students say that future work could explore collections of OATs that communicate with one another and work collectively. For example, one participant in the study noted that different sections of a refrigerator are intended for different storage purposes and therefore maintain different temperatures; they suggested placing multiple OATs within the fridge and combining their readings into an aggregated emotional state. The current version of OATs relies on cloud-based language models for reasoning, but another potential development would be to perform this locally on embedded hardware as models become more efficient, so even devices such as the Raspberry Pi Zero, which can run compact language models on-device, might provide ways to extend OAT to more mobile use cases such as bicycles or carried water bottles.
