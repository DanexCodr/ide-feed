Researchers from the National University of Singapore (NUS) have demonstrated a wearable touchscreen made from electronic fibers that can function as pixels and can also sense touch. The researchers from the NUS's College of Design and Engineering (CDE) integrated 54 of the fibers into a sleeve to create the world's first touchscreen built into a garment. The team, led by Assistant Professor Tural Khudiyev from the Department of Materials Science and Engineering at CDE, published their findings in Nature on 21 October 2026. The sleeve displays images and video, responds to finger presses and connects wirelessly to other devices, yet stays as washable, breathable and flexible as ordinary clothing. The team says the invention enables the creation of garments that act as a direct interface to a wearer's health data and their digital devices, with no rigid screen involved. Currently, screens are limited to flat, rigid panels, but the new technique open the way to full-color, touch-sensitive displays that can be threaded like yarn. According to Asst Prof Khudiyev: _"What made this possible was our ability to construct the GUI system from electronic building blocks in fiber form - similar to traditional yarn, but with the sophisticated electronic functionality built directly into the fibers,"_ The team developed a programmable method that creates connection points along tiny, insulated wires exactly where each microchip needs them. A laser exposes these locations, and controlled surface wetting is used to guide molten solder to bond only at the programmed points. This enabled the team to integrate 5,000 chips through 20,000 electrical connections. VIDEO The strip of interconnected digital LEDs was then integrated into a multilayer fiber using thermal drawing to precisely form the touch-sensitive and pixel layers within a thread just a millimeter thick and 10 meters long. A layer beneath the LEDs senses touch so that pressing the fiber completes a circuit, and the signal reveals exactly where your finger landed so the nearest pixel lights up. Asst Prof Khudiyev explained: _"A fiber has a one-dimensional, axially symmetric geometry, which makes it difficult to build complex circuitry using approaches developed for conventional two-dimensional electronics,"_ adding _"We overcame this limitation through a new interconnection architecture, which enables thousands of microelectronic devices to be integrated and interconnected along a fiber."_ In the team’s prototype, 54 pixel fibers, each carrying 96 LEDs, were threaded into the forearm of a sleeve to form a 96 by 54-pixel display measuring 168 mm by 105 mm, refreshing 30 frames per second with 8-bit color depth. Each fiber has its own control channel, so a fault in one line leaves its neighbors working normally, and the system can register several touches at once. Alongside the pixel fibers, the team integrated peripheral fibers made the same way, carrying a microphone, a speaker and sensors that track movement, find magnetic north, measure temperature and gauge how bright the surroundings are. Together, the fibers support the functions early generations of smartphones could perform, so could run a calculator, show a photo gallery, play a video at 30 frames per second (25 frames per second with synchronized audio), work as a compass, respond to voice commands, and receive images over Bluetooth. They can even rotate the screen and adjust brightness automatically. When positioned under the arm, the temperature-sensing fiber lets the sleeve read and display body temperature. The garment still behaves like fabric, and light output and touch response were nearly unchanged after 50 detergent washes in a typical washing machine, and after 1,000 bends around a 5 mm radius. In addition, the built display is as breathable as cotton, polyester and denim, and stays within established safety limits for skin contact. The team says that smaller microchips and thinner fibers, both compatible with their method, offer a route to sharper and larger displays. Because the display and sensing elements sit along the length of the fiber, the thinner fibers can be knitted, weaved and embroidered using standard industrial machines. Asst Prof Khudiyev pointed to healthcare as one particularly promising application: _“A garment could continuously collect physiological information through fiber-based sensors and allow the wearer to access and interact with that information directly through their clothing. Combined with artificial intelligence, this could enable personalized health monitoring, feedback and assistance. Just as smartphones enabled applications that were difficult to imagine when they first appeared, garment GUIs could ultimately enable entirely new applications that do not yet exist.”_ The full article can be read in [Nature](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11128-9>).
