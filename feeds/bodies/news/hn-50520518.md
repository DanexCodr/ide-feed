**New Regency says the director can’t reclaim rights in “Heat” because he wrote it for his own company. Mann says he wrote the early drafts on his own, not as his loan-out’s employee.**

The [most famous scene](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-features/heat-30th-anniversary-best-crime-movie-1235480130/) in Michael Mann’s *Heat* is a six-minute cup of coffee. Al Pacino’s cop and Robert De Niro’s thief, sharing the screen for the first time, sit across a table and discover they’re more or less the same guy.

A [lawsuit filed Friday](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/michael-mann-new-regency-heat-screenplay-lawsuit-1236947532/) in Los Angeles federal court describes a similar meeting. According to New Regency’s complaint, Mann is trying to reclaim rights in his early *Heat* screenplay drafts based on a 1988 deal in which Michael Mann met with Michael Mann and assigned those drafts to two companies he controlled. Nobody wrote anything down. The studio says Mann only recently “discovered” the alleged oral agreement with himself “after forgetting about it for nearly four decades,” and that when it asked for proof, his story changed.

The complaint, [*New Regency Productions, Inc. v. Mann* (read it here)](https://copyrightlately.com/pdfviewer/new-regency-productions-v-mann-complaint/), asks a judge to declare that three copyright termination notices Mann served this spring are invalid. If Mann’s notices hold up, New Regency could lose rights in the underlying material, along with control over future *Heat* projects. The studio’s main argument will be familiar to regular readers of Copyright Lately: Mann wrote *Heat* as an employee of his own loan-out corporation, which makes the screenplay a work for hire that can’t be terminated.

I’ve been writing about this problem since 2012, when I called the collision between termination rights and loan-out companies “[a ticking time bomb for copyright-intensive industries](https://copyrightlately.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Copyright-Termination-and-Loan-Out-Corporations.pdf).” Since then, we’ve had [John Waite](https://copyrightlately.com/copyright-termination-loan-out-corporations/) (couldn’t terminate grants his loan-out signed), [*Bad Boys*](https://copyrightlately.com/studio-loan-out-company-copyright-termination-fight/) (settled before any rulings) and [*Road House*](https://copyrightlately.com/amazon-mgm-road-house-copyright-termination/) (summary judgment motions coming soon). Now it’s the writer and director of *Heat*, with cameras set to roll on *Heat 2* in nine weeks. And by New Regency’s account, Mann isn’t taking the position that his loan-out was a sham. His argument is more interesting than that, but it may be hard to prove.

## How Amazon Bought *Heat 2* Twice

But first, to see why this lawsuit landed now, we need a bit of the *Heat 2* backstory.

Mann [published *Heat 2*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_2), a novel he co-wrote with Meg Gardiner, in 2022. According to the complaint, New Regency then licensed the sequel film rights to one of Mann’s companies. Warner Bros. came aboard in 2023 to co-produce, and Mann delivered a script in March 2025. Warner Bros. reportedly balked at Mann’s $170 million budget and let him shop the project elsewhere. In October 2025, the trades announced that Amazon MGM’s United Artists was [picking up the project](https://www.indiewire.com/news/breaking-news/michael-mann-heat-2-leonardo-dicaprio-amazon-mgm-1235154703/), with Leonardo DiCaprio and Christian Bale set to star. Asked about the move at a festival in Lyon, Mann [told reporters](https://www.avclub.com/michael-mann-heat-2-amazon-mgm), “I can’t get into all the politics of it.”

The complaint gets into some of those politics. New Regency says Warner’s license reverted to it when the studio passed. Then, on April 29, 2026, months before the Amazon agreements described in the complaint were signed, Mann served New Regency with a notice purporting to terminate its rights in *Heat*. Two more termination notices followed in May.

Amazon’s apparent response was to buy the sequel rights twice. According to the complaint, Mann granted Amazon in October whatever *Heat 2* rights he “may personally recapture” under his termination notices, and a few days later, New Regency granted Amazon “certain of” its own rights. Both agreements acknowledge the dispute, and some of each side’s promises about title to the *Heat* screenplay depend on how it comes out. It’s like buying insurance from two companies, each of which insists the other’s policy is worthless.

New Regency sued on November 13. Mann’s spokesperson [told Variety](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/michael-mann-new-regency-heat-screenplay-lawsuit-1236947532/) the case is “unrelated to production of *Heat 2*,” which is scheduled to start shooting January 19. With agreements from both sides, Amazon may be covered for the sequel either way, though the complaint says nothing about what comes after it.

## The Trouble With Hiring Yourself

A quick refresher for anyone who skipped my [last](https://copyrightlately.com/copyright-termination-loan-out-corporations/) [few](https://copyrightlately.com/studio-loan-out-company-copyright-termination-fight/) [articles](https://copyrightlately.com/amazon-mgm-road-house-copyright-termination/) on this (it’s fine, they were long).

[Section 203(a) of the Copyright Act](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html) lets authors terminate copyright grants, generally starting 35 years after they’re made. The idea is to give creators a second bite at the apple once a work has proven valuable. Works made for hire are an exception. The employer is treated as the author from day one, and only individual authors and their heirs get termination rights.

Now add the loan-out company. Hollywood writers and directors commonly work through corporations they own, which formally employ them and lend their services to studios (hence the name). If you write a screenplay as an employee of your own company, the company is the legal author. It can’t terminate, because it’s a company. You can’t terminate, because you’re not the author. And even when the artist is the author, courts haven’t let artists terminate deals their loan-outs signed. As the judge in John Waite’s case put it, “people cannot use a corporate structure for some purposes—e.g. taking advantage of tax benefits—and then disavow it for others.”

So Mann needs three things: a piece of *Heat *he wrote as himself, a grant of that piece that he made personally, and a link between that grant and the rights New Regency actually holds. New Regency says he has none of them.

## The Other Michael Mann

*Heat* took almost twenty years to reach the screen, and New Regency says Mann called his writing a work for hire at every step. The complaint lays out the paper trail. In 1976, his new loan-out, the Michael Mann Company, lent his services to Columbia Pictures for a TV crime project. In 1989, the paperwork for an NBC movie called *L.A. Takedown* recited that he wrote the teleplay as “an employee-for-hire” of the loan-out and “reserved no rights.” And in 1994, the loan-out sold Warner Bros. the *Heat* screenplay as material Mann wrote “as a [work made for hire](https://copyrightlately.com/glossary/work-made-for-hire/).” Mann signed for the company and again for himself.

That 1994 sale is central to New Regency’s case. It says all its *Heat* rights, including sequels, trace to that assignment. The Copyright Office’s records are consistent with that: the registration Warner Bros. filed that November lists the screenplay’s author as “Michael Mann Company, Inc., employer for hire,” not Michael Mann personally.

But none of those agreements dates from 1981 to 1985, and that’s the period Mann is focusing on. According to the complaint, Mann now says he wrote early *Heat* drafts in those years on his own, as what New Regency calls “an unemployed author.” (That window covers *Thief*, *The Keep* and the launch of *Miami Vice*, so as unemployment goes, it was a pretty productive stretch.) Mann’s spokesperson [told *Variety*](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/michael-mann-new-regency-heat-screenplay-lawsuit-1236947532/) the dispute concerns “original and personal writing Michael Mann did on multiple screenplays during the 1980s.”

That’s different from the argument made by *Road House* writer Lance Hill, who calls his loan-out an “alter ego” that should be disregarded for purposes of termination. Mann, as New Regency tells it, accepts that his loan-out was real. He just says he wasn’t working for it at the time he wrote the good stuff.

Next Mann needs a personal grant. His first termination notice identified an October 1, 1988 agreement with two companies he controlled: his loan-out and ZZY, Inc. New Regency says that when it asked for a copy, Mann’s lawyer explained there wasn’t one, because the agreement was oral. When the studio pressed for evidence, it says, the loan-out dropped out of the deal. A second notice followed, this time identifying an agreement between Mann and ZZY alone. New Regency likens Mann to “a Shakespearean actor playing multiple parts.”

The third termination notice concerns a written 1989 agreement assigning *L.A. Takedown* material to another Mann company, Ajar, Inc. The complaint says the rights Mann assigned personally came from an earlier Columbia project, which was never produced and which he may have reacquired under a Writers Guild agreement. New Regency replies that reacquiring rights doesn’t change authorship: if the 1976 project was Columbia’s work for hire, it still is.

So altogether, Mann has served three termination notices aimed at two different grants. Nearly all of this is New Regency’s version of events. Mann hasn’t yet answered the complaint, and his spokesperson [has said only](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/michael-mann-new-regency-heat-screenplay-lawsuit-1236947532/) that he’s “exercising the right of authors to reclaim their work and in this case Michael Mann’s original creation.”

## Why an Oral Assignment?

The strangest thing about this case is that Mann is trying to terminate a grant that, by New Regency’s account, nobody can document. Why not the 1994 Warner Bros. agreement, which is in writing?

I can think of a couple of reasons. First, that deal was made by his loan-out, which assigned the screenplay as material Mann wrote for it “as a work made for hire.” Mann signed too, but only to be personally bound “to the extent necessary,” which isn’t the same as granting rights himself. That’s John Waite’s problem all over again. Then there’s the calendar. Even if Mann could terminate the 1994 deal, he couldn’t do it before December 2030. A 1988 grant, on Mann’s reading, gets him there in 2028.

But even if an oral grant can be terminated (New Regency says it can’t, because it was never “executed”), what does Mann get back? An exclusive license or transfer of ownership requires a signed writing, so the most ZZY could have received via an oral conversation was a nonexclusive license. New Regency says its exclusive rights come through the 1994 deal, not through ZZY. Ending a nonexclusive license to ZZY wouldn’t touch rights that reached New Regency some other way. So Mann could win the termination fight and still not get back what he actually wants.

## What Comes After *Heat* *2*?

*Heat 2* may be protected even if Mann wins. Under the Copyright Act’s derivative works exception, a [derivative work](https://copyrightlately.com/glossary/derivative-work/) “prepared” under a grant before termination can keep being exploited afterward. The 1995 film is safely on that side of the line. But the statute says “prepared,” not “completed,” and exactly how far along a movie must be before the termination date is an open question. *Heat 2* starts shooting in January 2027, and Mann’s notices take effect in 2028 at the earliest. Fortunately, Hollywood productions are famous for finishing precisely on schedule. (This one was supposed to start shooting in November.)

The larger prize may come after *Heat 2*. In July, [TheWrap reported](https://www.thewrap.com/creative-content/movies/heat-2-development-history-amazon-mgm-studios-explained/) that Amazon is already exploring *Heat 3*, and that the franchise’s long-term value was part of why it took on the sequel. If Mann succeeds in reclaiming rights that New Regency currently controls, he could have real leverage over future *Heat* projects, while New Regency would keep the 1995 film and whatever was added to the story after those early drafts. Depending on what Mann actually gets back, neither side may be able to make another *Heat* movie without the other, which would put them in the same spot as Pacino and De Niro at the diner: across a table from each other, with a lot to talk about.

## My Take

New Regency looks to have a strong case, at least on the facts we have now. But not because Mann supposedly made a deal with himself. Contracting with yourself is the whole business model of a loan-out, and New Regency is the one insisting the court respect Mann’s.

Mann has a plausible answer to the work-for-hire argument. He doesn’t appear to be saying his loan-out was an artifice or should be disregarded, but rather that the work at issue was written outside of it. A court could agree without blowing up anybody’s tax returns. And the work-for-hire labels aren’t conclusive. In August 1994, Mann signed a certificate declaring that everything he had “heretofore or hereafter” written relating to *Heat* was a work made for hire. But in *[Marvel Characters, Inc. v. Simon](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=15347546289251433498)*, the Second Circuit held that simply calling a work “made for hire” after the fact doesn’t make it one. So a certificate signed in 1994 isn’t necessarily determinative of what actually happened in 1982.

The difficulty for Mann is proving it. “Outside my employment” is hard to show when writing was your job. Two years after *Simon*, the same court held in the [*Martha Graham* case](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=14065729160231294638) that dances Graham created while employed full-time to choreograph belonged to her employer, however much creative freedom she had.

And the paper trail here may defeat Mann’s theory in any event. According to New Regency, in the 1989 Ajar agreement, signed less than six months after the supposed oral deal, Mann warranted that the “Hanna” rights hadn’t been transferred to anyone, even though his first notice describes the work he orally assigned as “Heat a/k/a … Hanna.” And while Mann can argue the work-for-hire language was boilerplate, he’ll have to explain why it appears in agreements spanning nearly two decades, some of which he signed twice. (Then there’s the question I raised in 2023: if his best writing fell outside his employment, what exactly was his loan-out getting? The IRS might find that as interesting as a federal judge does.)

Even if Mann clears all that, he still has to connect a 1988 oral grant to rights that, by New Regency’s account, came through the 1994 deal. The picture could change once Mann answers the complaint and the early drafts surface. Until then, New Regency’s documents beat Mann’s memory.

Mann hasn’t exactly kicked the loan-out habit, either. According to the complaint, the company that licensed the *Heat 2* film rights from New Regency in 2022 is his original loan-out under a new name, Forward Pass, Inc. And the *Heat 2* novel is registered at the Copyright Office to “Michael Mann Books LLC, employer for hire.”

## The Bottom Line

Robert De Niro’s character in *Heat*, Neil McCauley, lived by a rule against getting attached to anything he couldn’t walk out on in thirty seconds. Mann has been attached to *Heat* for fifty years and to his loan-out for just as long, and this case may force him to choose between them.

Or he may never have to. Most termination fights settle, and one where the financier already has agreements with both sides looks like a good candidate. That would disappoint those of us hoping for a ruling, but then again, Hollywood has never settled cases with copyright scholars in mind.
