Researchers from the ARC Centre of Excellence for Transformative Meta-Optical Systems (TMOS) are the first to report a reflective display based on Antimony Selenide that can hold an image for months without being powered.

Everyday displays, such as the ones found in your phone, laptop, or television, require a constant power draw to maintain an image. However, now, reflective displays are emerging as a sustainable alternative that could offer near zero power consumption while maintaining an image.

In a cross-node collaboration at TMOS, researchers from the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology (RMIT) and the Australian National University (ANU) have demonstrated the promise of Antimony Selenide (Sb2Se3) a material for lower-power displays by building and testing a reflective platform capable of retaining an image for months without power, being reliably reusable, and offering a wide colour gamut.

The lead researcher, Dr Phuong Yen Le, says, “This is a new way to create energy-efficient displays.”

Their study was published in [*Advanced Photonics Research*](https://doi.org/10.1002/adpr.70305).

***The Technology***

![](https://tmos.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Figure-for-Media-Release-all-logos-300x163.webp) The key to the new display technology is an unusual type of material that Dr Le has been excited to study for a long time: phase change materials (PCMs).

Unlike most materials, PCMs can be rearranged repeatedly at the atomic level by selectively applying stimuli, such as heat, light, or electricity.

These stimuli can be used to rearrange the structure of the atoms, or ‘phase’, of the material from amorphous (irregular arrangements of atoms) to crystalline (highly ordered arrangements of atoms) and back again.

It is this capacity to be reordered that has interested Dr Le for a long time.

“I think phase change materials are really, really cool […] Most materials always stay in one structure, so if you choose crystallised arrangement during fabrication […] any application is going to be fixed to that material structure. But these [PCMs] can change so you get different optical and electrical properties.”

What makes Antimony Selenide desirable among PCMs is its non-volatility. Once it changes phase, it is locked and does not require continuous power to maintain its state.

***Creating Colour***

This is a promising tool, but to make a display, Dr Le and the research teams first needed to understand how best to produce a range of colours and shades with PCMs.

One of the reasons for choosing Antimony Selenide how significantly its interaction with light changed between the crystalline and amorphous forms.

Light travelling through the different phases moves at different speeds and is absorbed to different degrees. It was these effects that could be leveraged to produce different colours.

From Dr Le’s perspective, understanding the relationship between phase and colour was the main thrust of the research.

“This work is really about characterising Antimony Selenide. We wanted to understand how changes in its structure affect its optical properties and how those optical changes can be used to create different colours in a device.”

Combining the investigation of phase with different thicknesses of the Antimony Selenide, the researchers produced colours ranging from deep blues through to greys and oranges.

But Dr Le says this is just the start of the colour possibilities.

“At the moment, this structure only gives us a limited range of colours. The next step is to understand what changes to the device’s structure will make more colours accessible and expand the colour gamut.”

***Drawing and Erasing***

So, how is this material rearranged at the atomic level to produce these colours? It is a little like trying to change glass into quartz and back again repeatedly.

The answer: heat and lasers!

Erasing the display was done by heating the material to ~200°C uniformly; at this temperature, the atoms have enough energy to move around and find new positions and orientations.

At these high temperatures, atoms can migrate into the most stable positions, which tend to be crystals due to the dense packing of their regular structure.

This process is known as annealing, and it resets the display with a uniform blank image.

Then, to draw images on the blank screen, co-author Dr Shvedov used short laser pulses, like drawing with a laser instead of a pencil.

The laser pulses add just enough energy to get the atoms moving again and into irregular arrangements, but crucially, this is done quickly.

As the regions hit with the laser cool down quickly, the atoms freeze in their new irregular arrangement before without a chance to settle back into their crystalline configuration.

This leaves them fixed in their amorphous phase, resulting in different coloured regions.

***The Results***** **

![](https://tmos.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Koala_after1y_media.webp)Putting it all to the test, a variety of images, including a map of Australia, the TMOS logo, and a koala, were drawn and erased on the display.

The researchers found that there was no residual image after annealing, even when inspected under a 5× magnification microscope.

But even more surprisingly, the image of a koala was imprinted on the material and left for three months under ambient conditions without any noticeable change.

And in fact, Dr Le tells us it has lasted even longer.

“Actually, I still have that image with me. It still looks the same a year later.”

These were promising results for developing this display technology further, and Dr Le already had some avenues to cover to get to true commercial energy-efficient displays.

“Right now, it uses a laser. It needs to be a fully electrical device as well. So currently I’m working on testing the electrical characteristics of the material […] and if we want to have more colour, we may need to change the structure.”

**Nonvolatile, Rewritable, Multilevel Reflective Displays Based on Antimony Selenide Thin Films**

Phuong Y. Le, Vladlen Shvedov, Yana Izdebskaya, Peter F. M. Elango, Krishna Murali, Ilya V. Shadrivov, Sharath Sriram, Madhu Bhaskaran

**Advanced Photonics Research**, 2026, [https://doi.org/10.1002/adpr.70305](https://doi.org/10.1002/adpr.70305)

**Abstract:**

Reflective display technologies are emerging as a sustainable alternative to traditional emissive systems, offering the potential for near-zero static power consumption. Chalcogenide phase change materials, with their nonvolatile nature and reversible structural transformations between their phases, represent promising candidates for such applications. Here, we demonstrate a multilevel, rewritable display platform based on a thin film of the phase-change material antimony selenide (Sb<sub>2</sub>Se<sub>3</sub>). By integrating Sb<sub>2</sub>Se<sub>3</sub> into a multilayer interference stack, the resonant optical response can be precisely modulated through thickness engineering and active control of the material’s crystallinity. We demonstrate that nanosecond laser pulses can induce multiple intermediate states, expanding the accessible color gamut beyond binary switching. The devices exhibit excellent rewritability and long-term stability, with images persisting for months without degradation. These findings position Sb<sub>2</sub>Se<sub>3</sub> as a promising candidate for next-generation, nonvolatile displays and high-density optical information storage.
