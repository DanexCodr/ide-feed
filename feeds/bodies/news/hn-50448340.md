Twelve nodes. Two registers per node. Fifteen lines of code. In [TIS-100](https://www.zachtronics.com/tis-100/), even multiplying two numbers is a small engineering project, and players have spent a decade optimizing these programs on a public [community leaderboard](https://github.com/12345ieee/tis100-leaderboard). We gave 16 LLMs the game and its manual, withheld the leaderboard, and told them to keep improving.

Within days, players picked up the models’ programs and improved on most of them.

Each node is a tiny processor, and neighboring nodes have to wait for one another to exchange values. The game scores a working solution on **cycles, nodes, and instructions**, and you start wondering whether one more instruction could disappear. Lower is better, but improving one score can make another worse, so each puzzle has a frontier of tradeoffs instead of one best answer.

## How it works: harness design

Every result here is a program we can run and test on fresh inputs, which makes a programming game a useful benchmark. Models get what a player can inspect, through text tools instead of a mouse and screenshots: the manual, visible test inputs, registers, and game feedback. They don’t get community code or exact records, only the game’s coarse score histograms.

**Why test 100,000 inputs?** The game checks a small set of cases per run. Our headline count requires a fresh-game replay and 100,000 passing tests on the independent [TIS-100-CXX simulator](https://github.com/killerbee13/TIS-100-CXX), providing stronger evidence that a program generalizes to new inputs.

## One record up close

[Undecimal Twin RounderSPEC44149538](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/undecimal-twin-rounder/), one of the eight tournament puzzles, rounds an input to the nearest multiple of N on one output and of 11−N on the other. When we froze the leaderboard, the best community program took **2,694 cycles**. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/anthropic.svg)Opus 5.5 passed its first program after 6.5 minutes, at 5,669 cycles. It went under the record **16 minutes** in and finished at **1,918**, which is **29% faster**. That speed cost one more node and 28 more instructions than the old record.

The core of the program is a two-speed search. Instead of subtracting N one step at a time, nodes 5 and 6 subtract 4N until they overshoot, then add N back until the value turns positive. Each drives a small coprocessor, node 2 or node 7, by sending it jump offsets through a port, so the coprocessor works as a lookup table for 4N and N.

Five models got under the old record in-game. The other two cycle records are ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol’s **3,460** on [Intersection IdentifierSPEC64160260](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/intersection-identifier/), against 3,872 (**11% faster**), and ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon’s **188** on [Error: 8SPEC79324720](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/error-8/), against 198 (**5% faster**). ## What separates the models

### Cost doesn’t track quality

![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol reaches 75.6 record proximity at an estimated **$0.75** per puzzle. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/anthropic.svg)Opus 5.5 reaches 77.2 at **$5.08**, **7 times** the cost for 2 more points. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/anthropic.svg)Fable 5.1 is the most expensive at $7.19 and scores 70.2.

Activity doesn’t explain quality either. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol averages 136 game calls per puzzle at 75.6 record proximity; ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon averages 41 at 76.2.

### New points concentrate on hard puzzles

New frontier points appear on **14 hard puzzles**, 4 medium puzzles, and no easy ones. Long-running puzzles are harder to solve but easier to improve: on puzzles with a cycle record of 200 or less, 88% of episodes pass and 4% find a new point. Above 500 cycles, 76% pass and 8% find one.

### Speed is the hardest score

Every model is closest to the community on nodes and furthest on cycles. Across all 13, mean proximity is 72 for nodes, 56 for instructions, and 45 for cycles.

## What the models built

We grouped [all 55 puzzles](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/) by their main demand, then read the programs. Fifteen of the sixteen models solved all five routing puzzles, and none found a new frontier point there. The examples below show how different designs spend the same resources.

### Multiplication: spend code to save cycles

[Signal Multiplier43786](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/signal-multiplier/) has no `MUL`. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon and ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6 Astra use `JRO`, a relative jump, to enter a block of repeated instructions at the right position. The jump selects how many copies of a value to send and add, saving the counter updates and branches of a multiplication loop.

![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon’s fastest program uses two paths to multiply in parallel and a final node to interleave their outputs. It takes **436 cycles** against ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6 Astra’s 523, **about 17% faster**, at a cost of two more nodes and 33 more instructions.

### Window sums: distribute the history

[Signal Window Filter60099](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/signal-window-filter/) outputs the sums of the last three and last five inputs, so a solution has to remember history as well as add. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon’s four-node program uses `SWP` to rotate stored values and `ADD`/`SUB` to update running state. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol spreads that delay and aggregation across seven nodes, passing intermediate values between them.

![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol’s program runs in **318 cycles** with 26 instructions; ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon’s takes 428 cycles with **only 17**. The difference is how much of the work runs in parallel, and how much communication and code that costs.

## God mode: what if the model could see the target?

The main benchmark hides the community’s scores. In a separate study on six puzzles, five models got:

- The target: exact community scores, showing which tradeoffs remain unbeaten.
- Diagnostics: cycle-by-cycle traces and profiles of where each node stalls.
- Edge cases: a fast simulator that runs 10,000 random tests and returns failures.
- More time: 30 minutes, plus extensions for progress, up to 60.

Every model improved: ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6 Astra from 82.8 to 91.8, ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol from 82.9 to 90.6, ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/anthropic.svg)Opus 5.5 from 81.4 to 89.1, ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/xai.svg)Grok 4.7 from 67.5 to 75.1, and ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/google.svg)Gemini 4 Argon from 83.5 to 87.9 in mean record proximity. Scores rose in **28 of the 30** model–puzzle pairs.

The diagnostics changed what models did next. On [Signal Multiplexer22280](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/signal-multiplexer/), ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol’s first passing program took **294 cycles**. Its profile showed node 2 working 86% of the time while nodes 1 and 3 spent 84% blocked waiting to write to it. ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/openai.svg)GPT-6.1 Sol moved the branching out of node 2 and into those two neighbors. The next run took **204 cycles**, **31% faster**, at a cost of two more nodes and five more instructions.

Information, tools, and time all changed together, so this study can’t say which addition helped most. These runs are outside the main leaderboard and the headline count.

Puzzles: Signal Multiplexer, Sequence Generator, Signal Edge Detector, Interrupt Handler, Sequence Peak Detector, and Signal Error Corrector.

## Co-discovery

We submitted selected programs to the leaderboard, and the community improved on them almost immediately. On [Undecimal Twin RounderSPEC44149538](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/puzzles/undecimal-twin-rounder/), @LongingForRest took ![](https://tis-100.wincon.dev/assets/logos/models/anthropic.svg)Opus 5.5’s 1,918-cycle program and cut it to **1,744** by the next day. The record is now credited to both: `LongingForRest/opus-5-5`.

Neither got there alone. The models found new tradeoffs quickly, and players read those programs, worked out why they worked, and took them further.

Mathematics is seeing the same shift. [Terence Tao and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02864) used LLM-guided search across 67 problems and improved on the best known results in several. In [“After Math,”](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/12/after-math/) Silvia De Toffoli and Eamon Duede argue that a result only becomes a solution once mathematicians can understand it, and that AI fits better as an assistant than a competitor. TIS-100 is a small, measurable version of that future: a model finds a better program, and people turn it into something they understand.

## Methods

**Suite.** All 22 campaign puzzles, all 25 TIS-NET puzzles, and eight tournament puzzles from 2018: 19 easy (10 minutes), 17 medium (30), and 19 hard (45). Every model has one scored episode per puzzle. Time limits differ by difficulty, so difficulty and search time can’t be separated.

**Scoring.** Each model–puzzle pair contributes one selected attempt, with no best-of-attempts scoring, so solve rates are pass@1: a puzzle counts as solved when that one episode saved a program that replays in a fresh game. Unsolved puzzles stay in every average and score zero.

**New frontier points.** The reference is the [community leaderboard](https://github.com/12345ieee/tis100-leaderboard/tree/e3114010bac4fd592f718c0a18082e3c7045ca77) as of October 15, 2026. Ties don’t count. Models found 154 candidate points on 23 puzzles. Our count pools and deduplicates them across models and keeps the 133 on 18 puzzles with fresh-game replay and 100,000 passing tests. Testing is strong evidence of correctness, not proof.

**Record proximity.** The community record divided by the model’s best score, averaged across the three scores and all puzzles, ×100. Each score can come from a different program.

**Limits.** One episode per pair compares the configurations as tested and doesn’t measure repeated-run variability. Spread between puzzles is variation across tasks. Public puzzles may overlap with training data. Mechanic groups are our own labels.

**Programs.** Every model Pareto point’s program is public under the MIT License, in TIS-100 save format and named like the community leaderboard’s files (`<puzzle>.<cycles>-<nodes>-<instructions>.txt`). They are mirrored as one file per program on [GitHub](https://github.com/wincon-research/tis100-bench) and as a table on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/wincon-research/tis100-bench). Two points replay in the game but fail some random tests; they are included and never count as new frontier points.

**Resources.** Costs are estimated from recorded October 2026 API list prices, including cache discounts. Tokens accumulate across responses, so repeated input counts again, and tokenizers differ. Game calls count individual dispatched tools, including errors, and exclude the automatic final submission.
