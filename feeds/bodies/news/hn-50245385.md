**After defying projections, solar's dramatic growth means it is now overtaking nuclear as a global power source. Here are seven reasons for solar's success, and where it might go from here.**

The world's solar power is crossing a threshold. A decade ago, it barely registered on a global scale, providing just 1% of the world's power. In 2026, it will almost certainly increase to 10% of power, and overtake nuclear, a mainstay of electricity since the late 1970s.

Globally, solar electricity generation quietly overtook nuclear power (just) in 2025, according to [statistics from UK-based energy think tank Ember](https://ember-energy.org/data/yearly-electricity-data/). And BBC analysis of Ember data over [the past 12 months up to September 2026](https://ember-energy.org/data/monthly-electricity-data/) shows it clearly outstripping nuclear, producing 13% more power.

"[Solar] generating more electricity than all the world's nuclear power plants is a pretty insane stat, for sure," says Dave Jones, co-founder of Ember.

From China's vast arrays to a recent solar upsurge driven by escalating oil prices, the incredible rise of solar power has defied all projections. Here are seven ways it has become an apparently ["unstoppable force"](https://ember-energy.org/latest-updates/world-surpasses-40-clean-power-as-renewables-see-record-rise/) – and what the future looks like from here.

## **1. An underestimated force**

"Solar is now the engine of the power transition," says Jan Rosenow, a professor of energy and climate policy at the University of Oxford in the UK.

Solar has become the [fastest-growing source of new electricity in history](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/global-electricity-review-2026/), and by far the largest source of new electricity globally. In 2025, it met three-quarters of the growth in the world's demand for electricity, overtaking wind power generation (wind power is also growing, just a little more slowly).

"It wasn't expected," says Rosenow. Mainstream projections, he says, have [underestimated solar year after year](https://www.carbonbrief.org/exceptional-new-normal-iea-raises-growth-forecast-for-wind-and-solar-by-another-25). Outlooks such as [those from the International Energy Agency (IEA),](https://storage.googleapis.com/rua-lse/files/books/27/cbaf5b5a-914a-498c-bace-179d7f5b0fd5.pdf) an influential intergovernmental organisation based in Paris, "assumed linear growth and slowly falling costs, and got both wrong".

Over 2026, multiple sources indicate [solar electricity generation will almost certainly far exceed nuclear](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/supply) globally. Nuclear's electricity generation [has remained more or less constant](https://ourworldindata.org/electricity-mix) since 2000, while solar's has increased some 2,700-fold.

## **2. 'Offensively cheap'**

Solar, a mature technology for some 15 years now, became [the cheapest electricity in history](https://www.carbonbrief.org/solar-is-now-cheapest-electricity-in-history-confirms-iea) in 2020. Since then, solar has scaled, efficiency has risen, and the amount of [silicon](https://smartenergy.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Silicon-in-Solar-Panels-1.pdf) and [silver](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/solar-industry-accelerates-shift-silver-costs-soar-2026-02-19/) used in panels has gone down, leading prices to [fall yet further](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/solar-pv-prices), says Jones.

In particular, [a large fall in the price of panels in 2023-24](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/solar-pv-prices) pushed us into the current era of what Jones calls "offensively cheap" solar. "A panel in itself is just so unbelievably cheap," he says. "It's well under $100 (£75)… and that's going to keep you generating electricity for 30, 40 years without any input at all."

Of course, it's not just in purely economic terms that solar is bringing down costs. Researchers have calculated the US's rising wind and solar generation brought it $249bn (£186bn) in [climate and health benefits between 2019 and 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949790624001654).

## **3. Global uptake**

China is still the world's biggest solar power by far but is by no means the only player.

Despite the US imposing [a variety](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/overview-trade-and-policy-measures-us-solar-manufacturing) of [tariffs on solar](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cdrvn686dljo), it remains the [second country when it comes](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/installed-solar-pv-capacity) to installations, which are still skyrocketing – [especially in states won by President Trump in 2024](https://www.woodmac.com/press-releases/u.s.-solar-capacity-can-now-power-over-50-million-american-homese). In fact, the US now has [enough solar to power more than one-third of its households](https://www.woodmac.com/press-releases/u.s.-solar-capacity-can-now-power-over-50-million-american-homese).

"Tariffs and other federal policies that constrain renewable energy growth are certainly expected to raise costs and slow expansion relative to what we otherwise would have seen," says Alicia Zhao, an expert in US mitigation pathways at the University of Maryland.

"However, economics are a key part of why solar has been able to grow so quickly. Even with rising costs, solar is still one of the cheapest sources of new power in the US. And it is [getting built regardless of political lines](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-how-us-renewable-energy-growth-persists-despite-federal-policy-uncertainty)."

## **4. Unreliable fossil fuels**

In many places, escalating diesel prices, [driven by the Russia-Ukraine conflict and US-Israel war with Iran](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cr0qk1j7q0eno), has swung the cost needle yet further in solar's favour. This is especially the case in many African countries where [home diesel generators are a mainstay](https://energyforgrowth.org/article/sizing-africas-shadow-power-system/) due to unreliable grids. Rooftop solar panels, nearly all of them imported from China, have now [taken off](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/the-take-off-in-african-solar-that-official-statistics-cant-yet-see/) as an alternative.

Solar is also rapidly providing new electricity [where fossil-based grids can't keep up](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/the-solarisation-of-pakistans-energy-economy/) with demand. Pakistan, for example, has become a surprising poster child for the solar revolution due to its [extraordinarily rapid rise in installations](https://janrosenow.substack.com/p/the-solar-boom-outrunning-the-grid). In 2019, solar provided less than 2% of its electricity; now it's 24%, driven by costly, unreliable grid electricity combined and cheap solar imports from China.

"What's really incredible in Pakistan is… generally most of the solar boom has been out of people's pockets," says Jones. "People can afford it, without needing to take out loans."

## **5. Battery revolution**

Solar power, of course, can only be produced during the day when the sun is shining. To turn it into electricity that can be dispatched at any time, it needs to be paired with batteries.

Fortunately, batteries have been having [their own cost revolution](https://ourworldindata.org/battery-price-decline), transforming their ability to support the solar rollout. "Solar plus storage is now routinely cheaper than new fossil plants in sunny regions," says Rosenow. "Batteries have largely solved the daily problem of shifting midday sun into the evening."

In California, for example, solar and batteries [met on average over a quarter of electricity demand](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/batteries-have-unlocked-the-era-of-anytime-solar/) from 19:00-21:00 (the evening peak) over the first half of 2026. US states such as Vermont are [now rolling out](https://www.bbc.co.uk/future/article/20260928-a-virtual-power-plant-hidden-in-vermont-homes-is-keeping-the-lights-on-during-stor) grid-integrated systems of home batteries, often paired with solar.

Barriers still remain for longer-term storage of solar across seasons, cloudy periods and monsoon seasons, though, both Rosenow and Jones say. It means the integration of solar into the wider electricity system still matters, says Rosenow.

## **6. Electrify everything**

Solar is seen as a key part of the push towards [electrification](https://unfccc.int/news/cop31-presidency-announces-new-targets-on-global-electrification-cutting-waste-resilient-cities), reducing fossil fuel use in areas that previously didn't use electricity, such as via electric cars. Electrification has been chosen as a major theme at [this year's UN climate talks in Turkey](https://unfccc.int/news/cop31-presidency-announces-new-targets-on-global-electrification-cutting-waste-resilient-cities).

"That's where solar's going, to not just be king of electricity, but king of energy," says Jones.

Still, it all requires a plan, Julia Souder, chief executive of the Long Duration Energy Storage Council, a global executive-led non-profit established at COP26, [warned recently](https://www.ren21.net/the-race-to-electrify-everything-needs-a-storage-plan/). "There is an inherent danger in uncontrolled electrification, and adding massive new loads without built-in flexibility creates a fragile system," she wrote in a blog for international policy network Ren21. "Electrification is not automatically resilient."

The limits on solar are now grids, storage and how fast we electrify demand, says Rosenow – not panels or cost. "The bigger question is no longer whether solar grows but whether our power systems and institutions can keep up."

"Renewables [already make up over 90% of new capacity](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-how-us-renewable-energy-growth-persists-despite-federal-policy-uncertainty) in the US," she says. "This need for secure, quickly deployable electricity could create a critical opportunity for [further] renewable energy growth even without support from the current federal government."

## **7. Expanding tech**

In the past few years, [plug-in panels](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c4g3y6398nwo) (or "balcony solar"), [have started spreading rapidly in Germany](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/EN/2026/20260108_EEG.html). These generate less electricity than rooftop panels but feed it directly into homes via a standard socket.

## **What's next for solar**

In 2026, the world's solar capacity will go on rising, but the rate of expansion may slow down for the first time ever, says Jones.

This is down to China: last summer, it changed its regime [for managing and supporting new solar installations](https://www.carbonbrief.org/chart-why-chinas-solar-boom-is-slowing-down), leading to a burst of installations in 2025 to fall under the old price regime. Elsewhere, it's a different picture. "If you exclude China, there's more panels being installed this year than last year," says Jones.

"Wherever grid power is costly and unreliable, the economics keep pulling people towards solar," says Rosenow. "There is one caveat: if Chinese solar panels are difficult to import this will not happen at speed."

There has been a lot of [bad news about the climate recently.](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/10/1168634) It's now incredibly likely [the 1.5C global limit set in Paris in 2015](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c93d59d4zy1o) will be breached, the world is seeing [rising climate impacts](https://www.unep.org/en/news-and-stories/story/climate-crisis-explained-seven-graphs) and is edging towards several climate tipping points this century.

But the solar success story shows we are also making progress in ways that were never anticipated. A decade ago, "no one was getting close to expecting that to happen", says Jones, noting that the growth in clean electricity has directly translated to fewer coal plants being built.
