It’s a cloudless Himalayan morning in June, and the 65-year-old taps the back of a pipe to check if it has frozen. Then walks to a stream of meltwater – fresh water that comes from melting snow, ice or glaciers – that snakes down to his village, Sakti in Leh district. He measures the shrinking artificial glacier and finds that too much of it is melting too soon. After his inspection, he settles on a rock beside the glacier, as though keeping vigil for an old friend.

Gyurmet’s skin is darkened by a life spent under the high-altitude sun, with deep worry lines carved across his forehead. His eyes are dry behind the glasses but sorrow rests heavily on his face. He used to be a shepherd like many other families in Sakti, back when the village had more grassland and the traditional way of life – pastoralism – was economically feasible. “I’m scared for the future of my grandchildren. We have nothing to give to the next generation,” he says as we make our way to the spot where the original glacier stood for centuries. We stare for a while at the empty spot between two mountains, like people who are at a crime scene after the body has been removed.

Gyurmet plays a vital role in the effort to recreate in the glacier’s place – an artificial one they call ‘khangri.’ That is, a human designed ‘glacier,’ a structure used to store winter ice for meltwater. This they release slowly when water is scarce. Ladakh is a high-altitude cold desert region, and this meltwater will help farmers irrigate crops when the natural runoff from glaciers is not happening.

Gyurmet draws an invisible line in the air, his hand sweeping from one side of the mountain to another. “This was all ice. From there... to here.”

We are standing in an otherworldly terrain, without the trees that cover mountain slopes and soften them. The mountains are vast, elemental and stripped down to the rock. A small village, Sakti, can be viewed from the glacier. “In our childhood, we had to wait for days to clear the snowfall. That has not happened in the last two or even three decades,” says 65-year-old Phunchok Gyalpo, who has spent his entire life in Sakti. “The winters are substantially warmer too,” he says.

Around four hundred families, overwhelmingly from the Boto (also spelt Bot) – the largest Scheduled Tribe in Ladakh, live in Sakti, at an altitude of 3,900 metres. The community, hoistorically, have been mainly pastoralists rearing yak, goats, highland cattle and sheep. When Gyurmet and Gyalpo were young – in the '60s and ‘70s – people had to climb up, navigate ice and could not stand where we are now having this conversation. “There was no road and the ice spilled over the entire space that has now turned into a meadow. Our glacier died five years ago. The deepest sorrow of my life was to watch it melt away,” Gyurmet explains.
