In remote communities where cellular networks and internet service are unavailable or unreliable, high-frequency (HF) radio can provide an alternative for long-distance communications. [Rhizomatica](https://www.ardc.net/apply/grants/2025-grants/grant-finalizing-an-open-source-digital-hf-network-stack-and-modem/), a nonprofit expanding communications access in underserved communities, recognized this potential and developed technology to make HF communications more accessible.

With support from ARDC, they developed the [High-frequency Emergency and Rural Multimedia Exchange System (HERMES)](https://hermes.radio/), a system that allows users to exchange messages, emails, files, and other data over HF. Designed for people without extensive radio or technical experience, Rhizomatica helps communities use and maintain this technology through deployments and local training in areas such as the Ecuadorian Amazon.

In addition, Rhizomatica released [Mercury](https://mercury.hermes.radio/) – an open-source HF data modem – [in May 2026](https://www.ardc.net/wp-content/uploads/Mercury-Press-Release-07-MAY-2026.pdf) to gain greater flexibility to adapt and improve its technology. Integrating Mercury not only made the entire HERMES system open-source but also introduced opportunities for experimentation beyond community deployments. Because Mercury can operate independently of HERMES, amateur radio operators and other experimenters can readily adapt the modem to their own radios and applications and contribute improvements for its continued development.

## Making HF Communications More Accessible

Although HF radio can provide long-distance communications without relying on conventional telecommunications infrastructure, setting up and maintaining HF data systems can present technical and financial challenges. Equipment costs, software licensing fees, and the knowledge needed to operate these systems can make them difficult to adopt, particularly in communities with limited resources.

To help users overcome this learning curve, Rhizomatica works with local partners to provide hands-on training for HERMES. Through this training, users learn to install, operate, and maintain their systems, with the end goal of managing their own communications infrastructure.

Rhizomatica also addressed the cost and flexibility challenges of the modem used in HERMES. The system previously relied on [VARA](https://varamodem.com/), a proprietary modem that required a paid license for each radio. As an alternative, Rhizomatica developed Mercury, an open source modem that provides similar functionality without the licensing fees and makes the entire HERMES system open source. Beyond reducing costs, Mercury’s open source design allows Rhizomatica and other contributors, such as amateur radio operators, to improve the modem’s functionality and adapt it to different equipment and applications. As an example, the team has adapted Mercury to run on a [Raspberry Pi Zero](https://www.raspberrypi.com/products/raspberry-pi-zero/), creating opportunities to use the modem with compact hardware, such as the [HF Signals](https://www.hfsignals.com/) [zBitx](https://www.hfsignals.com/index.php/zbitx/).

## Real-World Impact

Rhizomatica’s largest HERMES deployment includes nearly 90 radios in Waorani Indigenous communities in the Ecuadorian Amazon, where some installation sites take days to reach by foot or boat. Given these distances and the resources required to support this number of systems, Rhizomatica works with local partners to focus the training on a smaller group of community members. This approach helps communities keep their systems operational without relying solely on outside support. As a result of these efforts, HERMES helps communities overcome barriers to staying connected. Older Waorani residents, for instance, can now keep in touch with friends and family in other villages, something that was much more challenging before the HERMES deployment.

Beyond the Ecuadorian Amazon, Rhizomatica has deployed HERMES in [fishing communities in Bangladesh](https://cnlearning.apc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Bycatch-Beyond_ABH_updated.pdf) as part of a conservation project and also established early warning systems in the Central African Republic. These projects demonstrate how HF communications can support different needs in places where conventional communications infrastructure is limited or unavailable.

Mercury’s impact extends outside of Rhizomatica’s deployments. Since its release, amateur radio operators and developers have experimented with different hardware and software configurations, reported bugs, and contributed improvements to the project. For example, amateur radio operators have used Mercury to exchange Winlink emails, an application that the Rhizomatica team had not previously tested. Experimentation from the amateur radio community helps the team learn about additional use cases for the technology, while feedback and contributions inform Mercury’s continued development.

ARDC has supported Rhizomatica through multiple grants, [beginning in 2021](https://www.ardc.net/apply/grants/2021-grants/grant-digital-hf-telecommunications-for-civil-and-amateur-uses/). This funding has helped the team develop and test the technology, make ongoing improvements, and ultimately reach the milestone of a fully open-source HERMES system.

## Looking Ahead

Rhizomatica continues to develop HERMES and Mercury, focusing on improving performance, reliability, and support for additional communications protocols. The team has integrated [Reticulum](https://reticulum.network/) into HERMES, allowing HF links to connect with other networks and route communications between them. Rhizomatica is currently working to integrate [Meshtastic](https://meshtastic.org/), which could connect HF communications with long range (LoRa)-based networks, giving users additional ways to exchange information.

Rhizomatica also plans to expand HERMES’s broadcast capabilities over the coming year, providing another method for distributing files, images, and other information. This would allow users to share information without relying on automatic repeat query (ARQ), reducing delays associated with repeated acknowledgements between stations.

In addition to data transfer, HERMES supports digital voice through its compatibility with [D-STAR](https://www.icomamerica.com/lineup/amateur/D-STAR/). The team is also working to integrate [FreeDV RADE](https://freedv.org/radio-autoencoder/), giving HERMES users additional options for voice communications over HF.
