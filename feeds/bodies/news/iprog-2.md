Here's a philosophical question for you. Can a pop star generated by AI become pregnant, and if they can, what is the politically correct response? I have to admit that back in the dark ages of my Computer Science degree, while sitting through the module on social implications of computing, this wasn't a topic we were asked to debate, but there you go, times change.  For those of you in the back row who may not be fully up-to-date with the oddities of the AI world, here's the back story. There is a Chinese AI-generated K-pop-style girl group called BabyQueen. The group is made up of four pretty, young, AI-generated girls, and is well-known in China and surrounding areas.  BabyQueen hasn't really released much apart from an AI-generated video of their cover of K-pop girl group Aespa's song "Next Level", but the group does apparently have a lot of fans. If you doubt this, recently they really hit the headlines because one of its members (Mity) posted on the social media platform Xiaohongshu (RedNote) that she was leaving the group to go on maternity leave:  The annoucement translated reads: _"I’m pregnant, and after much consideration and thorough discussions with the company and my bandmates, I have decided to temporarily leave the group to focus on rest and recovery.”_ That's quite strange enough to be going on with, but what was really weird was the reaction from the fans of the group. The post was viewed 78 million times, and to begin with was greeted with shock, jokes about who the father could be, and questions as to what maternity benefits an AI-person could claim. If you think this is odd, it's not that strange compared to other recent cases. For example, how about taking advice from an AI-generated actress as to how comfortable contact lenses are, as in another case in China. The AI-generated actress, Fang Taozi, is best known for her role in a video drama series called "Laid-off Girl", in which she "plays" a would-be-model who moves to the large city of Shangnan and tries to make her living. The series has so far had around 250 million views, and Fang Taozi has become so popular she's been hired by an advertising agency at a fee of around $37,000 for a one minute commercial. For reference, this is higher than the rates charged by some human influencers with 10 million followers. In one high-profile advert, she endorses colored contact lenses, saying they felt comfortable after wearing them all day. Obviously, if you're a human trying to choose the most comfortable contact lens, you'd take the advice of an AI-generated actress because she'd know how good they are. No-one seems to have asked why, if you're an AI-generated being, you'd use colored contact lenses to change your eye color rather than just get your creators to change the color of your avatar's eyes, but there you go. Get the right online personality and people will buy what you recommend, no matter how illogical.  So is Mity's pregnancy just a cynical attempt to set up a personality that can then sell commercials about baby food and clothes? It's a lucrative market sector, after all. Unfortunately, if that was the case, Mity's creators misjudged things, because there was a huge backlash saying the post trivialized the experiences of real women in experiencing pregnancy and childbirth, the original post was deleted, and the creators said they were very sorry, they were just a small group of enthusiastic k-pop fans who'd been overwhelmed trying to manage the online AI personalities and they'd been trying to find a way to cut back. Perhaps, but the cynic in me says there will still be plenty of fans who would buy the same baby clothes that Mity's baby wears, though. We'll just have to wait and see.
