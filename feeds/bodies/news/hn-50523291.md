The biggest remaining gaps are in the SSR, SSGI and BRDF rows: Hi-Z tracing and coverage for SSR, the same coverage for SSGI, and above all the pluggable indirect lighting that ties both cascades into the BRDF.

It has moved this fast because of the Three.js maintainers. Michael Herzog ([@Mugen87](https://github.com/Mugen87)) has reviewed most of these PRs, and more than once has reworked one into a better design that fits how Three.js as a whole is built, adjusting the renderer's internals to make room for it. mrdoob has expressed strong support for many of them. I am very lucky to have both.

So far, though, these pieces have been proposed and reviewed one at a time. Nobody has been asked to sign up to the whole. This series is my attempt to put them together into one picture, so that each PR can be judged for where it fits as well as for what it does.

## The pipeline, stage by stage[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#the-pipeline-stage-by-stage)

### The G-buffer[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#the-g-buffer)

**What the games require.** SSR, GTAO and the denoisers run in screen space before lighting, so they need per-pixel depth, normals, roughness, metalness and motion vectors. A deferred engine also stores base color and emissive, because its lighting pass reads only the G-buffer. Three.js renders forward and its beauty pass reads those from the material, so the G-buffer fits in 16 bytes per pixel. [Part 2](https://ben3d.ca/blog/aaa-quality-on-a-web-budget) has the layout and the memory budget for the whole frame.

**What Three.js has.** WebGPURenderer already renders a G-buffer prepass with multiple render targets, and the [SSR examples](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35432) use it today:

```js
const scenePass = pass( scene, camera );
scenePass.setMRT( mrt( {
	output: output,
	diffuseColor: vec4( diffuseColor.rgb, materialMetalness ),
	normal: vec4( packNormalToRGB( normalView ).rgb, materialRoughness ),
	velocity: velocity
} ) );
```

**What is missing.** Normal precision. SSR is unforgiving of normal error: a ray reflected off a slightly wrong normal lands somewhere visibly wrong at the end of a long trace. With RGB8 normals, smooth curved surfaces snap to a limited set of directions, and their reflections show banding and clumping.

AAA G-buffers use **octahedral encoding**: fold the sphere of directions onto an octahedron, unfold it into a square, and store two coordinates. It is the most accurate of the encodings [surveyed by Cigolle et al.](https://jcgt.org/published/0003/02/01/) for its size. [PR #35480](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35480) adds `packNormalToOctahedral()` and `unpackOctahedralToNormal()`:

| Encoding (4 bytes / pixel) | Mean error | Max error | Availability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RGB8 (today) | 0.17° | 0.38° | Everywhere |
| Octahedral RG16F | 0.011° | 0.040° | Everywhere in WebGPU |
| Octahedral RG16 snorm | 0.0013° | 0.0037° | 93% of WebGPU devices |

RG16 snorm is the target, with RG16F as the fallback.

*Reflections from RGB8 normals (left) and octahedral RG16F normals (right).*

*Normal error: RGB8 (left) and octahedral RG16F (right).*

### Global illumination[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#global-illumination)

**What the games require.** Bounce light baked into lightmaps for everything that does not move, and a probe volume to light dynamic objects, ideally one that does not leak light through walls.

**What Three.js has.** Every standard material takes a `lightMap`, and there is now a complete baking pipeline in the browser to make one; that is [Part 4](https://ben3d.ca/blog/adding-robust-light-baking-to-threejs). For dynamic objects, `LightProbeGrid` places a grid of probes through a scene, each storing incoming light as L2 spherical harmonics, and lights each object from the probes around it ([example](https://threejs.org/examples/webgpu_lightprobes.html)).

*Sponza lit by `LightProbeGrid`.*

The grid's limit was how long a capture took. Profiling showed the bake was CPU-bound on submitting render passes, so the [batched capture](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/34909) renders eight probes per pass with one array camera, and a resolution-12 grid dropped from 5.6 s to 1.2 s. Culling each draw against its own sub-camera doubled that again. The grid now bakes about eight times faster than it did at the start of September, fast enough to [re-capture a few probes per frame](https://threejs.org/examples/webgpu_lightprobes_sponza.html), and supports multi-bounce light. Integrating the spherical harmonics exactly also cut their error from 2.4% to 0.08%.

**What is missing.** Probes leak. A probe on one side of a wall can light an object on the other, so a dark room glows with the light of the bright room next door. [DDGI](https://jcgt.org/published/0008/02/01/) solves this by storing, with each probe, the distance to the nearest surface in every direction, and ignoring probes that cannot see the point being lit. I have [DDGI-style visibility weighting](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35174) working as a draft PR. Testing visibility against all eight surrounding probes was costly, so the draft can use tetrahedral interpolation, which blends four:

| On Sponza | Added render time |
| --- | --- |
| Eight probes, trilinear | 30% |
| Four probes, tetrahedral | 10% |

*Two rooms with probe visibility weighting.*

### Ambient and specular occlusion[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#ambient-and-specular-occlusion)

**What the games require.** Lightmaps are too coarse for contact shadows, and they know nothing about dynamic objects. Games add screen-space occlusion on top, and Activision's [GTAO](https://research.activision.com/publications/archives/practical-real-time-strategies-for-accurate-indirect-occlusion) is the standard. It also produces a **bent normal**, the average open direction, which gives **specular occlusion (GTSO)**: a reflection pointing into a fold of the surface is occluded, one pointing out of the opening is not.

Horizon-based SSGI, which is what Three.js has, runs the same depth-buffer search as GTAO and gathers light along the way. With SSGI on, its pass supplies the occlusion and the bent normal, and GTAO runs only when SSGI is off.

Which light the occlusion applies to matters as much as how it is computed. Occlusion is a shared buffer, set with `builtinAOContext`, and each source decides whether to read it:

- The lightmap, probe grid and environment apply AO to their indirect diffuse, combined with the material's AO map as min( materialAO, gtao ).
- The reflection probes and environment apply GTSO to their indirect specular.
- SSR and SSGI apply neither. A screen-space hit is something the ray found, so it is already correctly occluded.
- Behind SSGI, the diffuse sources skip AO. SSGI's coverage is the share of the hemisphere that something on screen blocks, which is the occlusion itself. The cascade already scales the lightmap or probe grid by 1 − coverage.
- Occlusion darkens a source's color and leaves its coverage alone. Lowering an occluded probe's coverage would let the next source's unoccluded light leak into the corner.

**What Three.js has.** GTAO already feeds the lighting through a context, which is the pattern the indirect lighting design in Part 5 extends:

```js
const aoPass = ao( prePassDepth, prePassNormal, camera );
scenePass.contextNode = builtinAOContext( aoPass.getTextureNode().sample( screenUV ).r );
```

**What is missing.**

- Specular occlusion. GTAO outputs a single visibility value, and the lighting model turns it into specular occlusion with a heuristic. It does not know which directions are blocked, so metal keeps reflecting the sky inside folds. Bent normals and GTSO are up as a draft PR. The bent normal comes out of the same horizon search; the cost is an AO target that grows from one byte per pixel to four.
- Edge-aware upsampling. Half-resolution GTAO upsampled bilinearly leaves dark outlines around objects. `jointBilateralUpsample()` uses the full-resolution depth and normals as guides (Kopf et al. 2007), so the result stops at the edges. It is drafted and not yet submitted; half-resolution SSR and SSGI need the same filter.
- Bent normals from `SSGINode`, so that specular occlusion does not need a second pass when SSGI is on.

*GTSO (left) and scalar specular occlusion (right).*

### Screen-space reflections[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#screen-space-reflections)

SSR has its own series, [Fixing Three.js's Screen Space Reflections](https://ben3d.ca/series/fixing-threejs-ssr). This section is the summary.

**What the games require.** Shipped engines agree on a recipe, set out with its sources in [How Games Do Screen-Space Reflections, and What Three.js Does Instead](https://ben3d.ca/blog/how-games-do-screen-space-reflections). In a cascade, SSR has to:

1. Trace the previous frame's lit image, so the reflection exists before the frame is shaded.
2. Trace exactly over any distance, using a Hi-Z depth pyramid.
3. Trace at half resolution and reuse rays between pixels, which is what brings the trace under a millisecond.
4. Output radiance and coverage, so the probes can fill what it misses.
5. Denoise that radiance and coverage, and leave the BRDF to the material.

**What Three.js has.** `SSRNode` importance-samples the [full GGX lobe](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35243) with [correct Fresnel](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35242), can [validate and refine its hits](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35240), and its result is reprojected and accumulated over time. The trace is a fixed-step march with short rays. The three SSR examples find 98%, 82% and 46% of the hits within their own ray length, in 2 to 3 ms, and the denoiser fills the gaps from neighbouring pixels. The result is composited over the image, as in the [SSR example](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35432):

```js
const litColor = scenePassColor.rgb.add( denoisedReflections.rgb );
```

**What is missing.** All five requirements, each prototyped or in review except ray reuse.

**Exact tracing.** A **Hi-Z pyramid** is a mip chain where each texel stores the nearest depth of the four below it. The ray takes large steps through empty space at coarse levels and single-pixel steps near surfaces, so it finds every hit the depth buffer contains and has no maximum distance to tune. My [implementation](https://github.com/bhouston/three.js/pull/155) adds a `HiZNode` that builds the pyramid in a compute pass. Trace time at 1920×1080, against the fixed-step march raised to the same hit rate:

| Example | Linear march at full quality | Hi-Z |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ssr_mirror` | 27.6 ms | 2.9 ms |
| `ssr_stochastic` | 13.7 ms | 9.2 ms |
| `ssr_animation` | 8.1 ms | 5.2 ms |

That is still a ray for every pixel. Tracing at half resolution with a [joint bilateral upsample](https://github.com/bhouston/three.js/pull/110) is written, and ray reuse between pixels, as Frostbite does it, is still to build. [Exact Screen-Space Reflections on a Budget](https://ben3d.ca/blog/exact-screen-space-reflections-in-threejs) has the full plan.

*Hi-Z ray marching.*

**Coverage.** Today a ray that misses samples an environment map inside SSR: an equirectangular texture passed in as `environmentNode`, which cannot be the PMREM-filtered `scene.environment`, let alone a local probe. Adding SSR over the image also counts the environment reflection twice. In a cascade, SSR outputs one thing: radiance premultiplied by coverage, with coverage 1 for a hit, 0 for a miss, and fading toward the screen border. The environment input and the BRDF estimate leave the node. This is prototyped as part of [Pluggable Indirect Lighting for Three.js](https://ben3d.ca/blog/pluggable-indirect-lighting-in-threejs).

**Tracing the previous frame.** SSR cannot be part of the lighting while it reads the lighting's output. DOOM's answer is to trace the *current* depth buffer but read colors from the *previous* frame: reproject each hit point into last frame's TRAA output and sample it there. A hit that fails the check against last frame's depth gets zero coverage. Last frame's image already contained last frame's reflections, so a mirror facing a mirror gains a bounce each frame. [Previous-frame tracing](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35430) is in review.

The loop needs two rules. The image SSR reads must be **linear HDR radiance**, taken after TRAA but before tone mapping and bloom, or reflections pick up tone-mapped colors and bloom halos. And the fed-back radiance must be **clamped**, because each frame's reflections become the next frame's input and a single firefly compounds frame after frame.

*Reflections with previous-frame feedback.*

**Denoising.** Three.js has two reflection denoisers. The pair the SSR example runs, `TemporalReprojectNode` and `RecurrentDenoiseNode`, reads every black pixel as missing data and fills it from its neighbours. That hides the tracer's misses and leaves nothing for the probes to answer. zalo's [SVGF-based `DenoiseNode`](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35318) replaced the pair in the SSR example and was [taken back out](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35461) five days later. I traced the flicker that caused it to the missing coverage: at the edge of a reflection a hit and a miss were averaged as two samples of one signal, and the history kept resetting.

My [`ssr-coverage`](https://github.com/bhouston/three.js/tree/ssr-coverage) branch carries coverage through every stage of `DenoiseNode` and takes its statistics from covered samples only. Still to do are a two-lookup history, a history cap that follows roughness, and skipping mirrors and matte pixels. The denoiser filters incoming light and the material applies its BRDF afterwards, so it needs no albedo in the G-buffer and no demodulation step. [How Games Denoise Reflections, and What Three.js Does Instead](https://ben3d.ca/blog/how-games-denoise-reflections) has the comparison and the plan.

### Screen-space global illumination[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#screen-space-global-illumination)

**What the games require.** Engines that use SSGI as part of their lighting put it in front of their baked light as the first stage of the diffuse cascade. That means SSGI has to output coverage, trace the previous frame's lit image, and accumulate over time at half resolution.

**What Three.js has.** `SSGINode` is built on the visibility-bitmask approach of [SSRT3](https://github.com/cdrinmatane/SSRT3), which extends GTAO's horizon search to gather light as well as occlusion. One search of 24 depth samples yields a GI texture and an AO texture, and it is much less noisy than a single random ray, because each sample accounts for a whole arc of directions. The [SSGI example](https://threejs.org/examples/webgpu_postprocessing_ssgi.html) traces the current frame's beauty pass and composites over it.

**What is missing.**

- Coverage output. The sectors the search marks as blocked are the directions in which it found a surface, so coverage is 1 − ao, and the node already computes it. What is missing is handing the GI and that coverage to the cascade as a pair, so SSGI can replace the baked bounce where it has an answer.
- Previous-frame tracing. SSR reads the previous frame once per pixel, where its ray lands. SSGI reads it at every sample that finds a surface, so reprojecting at each read is paid many times per pixel. A draft measures the options on an Apple M3:

| Reprojection | Frame rate cost |
| --- | --- |
| Every sample, by camera motion | 14–22% |
| Every sample, by per-pixel velocity | 32–44% |
| Once, by velocity, into a half-resolution buffer the search reads directly | None measured |

The third option also follows moving objects, and shows no visible artifacts in the test scenes.

### Reflection probes[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#reflection-probes)

**What the games require.** Indoors, one environment map is wrong: walk from a red room into a blue corridor and the floor should reflect red, then blue. Every game in [the survey](https://ben3d.ca/blog/how-aaa-games-light-indoor-scenes), and Unreal's [reflection captures](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/reflections-captures-in-unreal-engine), use **local reflection probes**: cubemaps captured inside each room, each with a box describing the volume it influences. Inside its box a probe is **box-projected**, which removes most of a cubemap's parallax error ([Lagarde's write-up](https://seblagarde.wordpress.com/2012/09/29/image-based-lighting-approaches-and-parallax-corrected-cubemap/) is the classic explanation). Where boxes overlap, probes blend.

**What Three.js has.** `CubeCamera` captures a cubemap, `PMREMGenerator` prefilters it, and `getParallaxCorrectNormal()` box-projects it, as the [box-projected environment map example](https://threejs.org/examples/webgpu_materials_envmaps_bpcem.html) shows. That is one probe per material, set by hand.

**What is missing.**

- A probe system. Box-projected reflection probes for WebGPURenderer adds scene-level probes with influence boxes and blending, and is up as a draft PR.
- Fast access to many probes. Cube array textures put every probe in one texture; sampling them is merged, and the texture class is in review.
- Probe normalization. A probe is captured at one point in a room, so a dark corner or the inside of a cupboard reflects a brightly lit room. Call of Duty's fix, introduced in Black Ops II and carried into Infinite Warfare, is to scale the probe's reflection by the ratio of the diffuse light at the pixel to the diffuse light at the probe. The lightmaps and probe grid already provide both. Still to do.
- Small probes. PMREMGenerator has a minimum size of 256². A 128² probe is enough for blurry room reflections, since SSR handles the sharp ones, and takes a quarter of the memory. Still to do.

*Two rooms lit by three box-projected probes.*

### Indirect lighting in the BRDF[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#indirect-lighting-in-the-brdf)

**What the games require.** The bounced light has to go *through* the material's BRDF, so Fresnel, roughness and metalness apply to it as they do to direct light. In a forward renderer the beauty pass needs two inputs:

- Indirect diffuse: SSGI where it has coverage, then the lightmap on static meshes or the probe grid on everything else, then the environment map.
- Indirect specular: SSR, then the box-projected probes, then the environment map.

Three.js renders forward, so SSR, SSGI and AO are computed *before* the beauty pass and each material reads them while it shades. Tracing the previous frame is what makes that order possible.

**What Three.js has.** Indirect diffuse is mostly in place: `lightMap`, `LightProbeGrid` and `scene.environment` all feed it, and AO reaches it through `builtinAOContext`. Indirect specular comes only from `scene.environment`, or a material's own `envMap`.

**What is missing.** A way to plug a screen-space result, or a cascade of sources, into a material's indirect specular, the way AO is plugged into its diffuse. This is the biggest gap in the pipeline, and my proposed design is in [Pluggable Indirect Lighting for Three.js](https://ben3d.ca/blog/pluggable-indirect-lighting-in-threejs).

### TRAA and TAAU[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#traa-and-taau)

**What the games require.** Temporal anti-aliasing whose output can also be next frame's input to SSR and SSGI, so it has to stay sharp under motion and must not feed ghosts or fireflies back into the reflections. Every engine this series measures itself against also renders below the output resolution and reconstructs the difference over time: TSR in Unreal, FSR 2 in Godot, DLSS and FSR in Cyberpunk 2077.

**What Three.js has.** A TRAA node with Halton jitter, motion-vector reprojection and variance clipping of the history, and `TAAUNode`, which follows Karis's temporal supersampling and FSR 2. The [TAAU example](https://threejs.org/examples/webgpu_upscaling_taau.html) shows it at a quarter of the pixels:

```js
scenePass.setResolutionScale( 0.5 );

const taauNode = taau( scenePassColor, scenePassDepth, scenePassVelocity, camera );
```

TAAU takes TRAA's place as the last stage of the frame. Everything before it runs at the render resolution, its linear HDR output is what the next frame's SSR and SSGI read, and bloom and tone mapping run after it. At a scale of 0.5, the screen buffers at 1080p drop from about 157 MB to 81 MB.

**What is missing.**

- **Wider clipping at rest.** Variance clipping stops ghosting by rejecting history that no longer matches the current frame, and it also rejects good history. TRAA tightens its clip box to 0.5σ under fast motion, but at rest the box was 1σ, tight enough to clip converged sub-pixel detail, so a still image never fully resolved and longer histories scored worse. A [drafted change](https://github.com/bhouston/three.js/pull/124) widens the box to 2σ at rest and adds a `historyLength` control. Against a supersampled reference: ViewPSNR changeStatic+2.15 dBStill camera, moving objects+1.3 dBFast camera motionWithin 0.06 dB
- **Sharper history.** History sampled bilinearly blurs a little more every frame the camera moves. [Catmull-Rom history sampling](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35447), a draft PR, keeps it sharp. Both refinements apply to TAAU as well.
- **TAAU in the full pipeline.** The example upscales a single scene pass. The pipeline has a prepass, half-resolution effects and a beauty pass that all have to agree on resolution and jitter, and none of that has been tried together yet. With TAAU at 0.5, half-resolution SSR and SSGI would run at a quarter of the output resolution on each axis, so it may be better to run them at the full render resolution and skip the bilateral upsample.
- **Texture mip bias.** Rendering at half resolution makes the GPU pick blurrier texture mips than the output deserves. Temporal upscalers pair with a negative mip bias of about log2 of the resolution scale, and Three.js does not apply one yet.

## Future work[#](https://ben3d.ca/blog/building-the-aaa-lighting-pipeline-in-threejs#future-work)

- Cheaper noise. Houston Stratified Noise costs about 400 operations per sample, on every pixel, every frame. I want to move some of that into a small map generated at startup, so each sample is a lookup and a few adds.
- Auto-exposure. Walking from a dark room into a bright courtyard needs eye adaptation, and Three.js does not have it yet.
- A physically based sun and sky. A room lit through its windows needs a sun and sky in real units that agree with each other and with the exposure. A prototype does single scattering; multiple scattering and clouds are still to do.
- Reflection probes in glTF. There is no standard way to ship reflection probes. The minimum is each probe's position and influence box, so a loader can capture the probes on load.
- Directional lightmaps. The lightmaps here store one irradiance value per texel, so normal maps cannot respond to baked light. The options are an ambient term plus one dominant direction as in The Last of Us and Call of Duty, L1 spherical harmonics as in Frostbite, or spherical Gaussians as in The Order: 1886.
