Not every Game Boy game had Nintendo's blessing. This one certainly did not. [Exodus: Journey to the Promised Land](https://www.gameboymuseum.com/single-post/exodus-journey-to-the-promised-land-the-unlicensed-bible-game-on-the-game-boy) is a Bible game, published in 1993 by a company called Wisdom Tree, sold not in toy shops but in Christian bookstores, and made entirely without a licence. It is one of the stranger entries a DMG-01 collection can hold: a fully finished, boxed retail game that Nintendo never approved, never certified, and, famously, never sued.

## A publisher that found a loophole

Wisdom Tree did not start out holy. It was created in 1990 as a subsidiary of Color Dreams, a California outfit that had been making unlicensed games for the Nintendo Entertainment System and running into Nintendo's famously tight grip on who was allowed to publish. Color Dreams hit on an audacious idea: wrap its games in Bible stories, sell them through Christian bookshops rather than games retailers, and bet that Nintendo would never risk the headlines of suing a maker of Christian children's games. The bet paid off. Nintendo never sued Wisdom Tree.

The formula worked commercially, too. Wisdom Tree's first release, Bible Adventures, sold more than 350,000 copies, a staggering number for an unlicensed title. The company went on to put biblical games on the NES, the Game Boy, the Sega Genesis and even the Super Nintendo, where its Super 3D Noah's Ark was, unforgettably, the Wolfenstein 3D engine with the Nazis swapped for animals you lulled to sleep by feeding them. Color Dreams eventually drifted out of games altogether, moving into surveillance cameras in 1996 and selling Wisdom Tree on in 1997.

## The Bible on a grey screen

On the Game Boy, Wisdom Tree released a small run of titles, around four in all, including Exodus and Joshua & the Battle of Jericho, both in 1993. Exodus casts the player as Moses, working through one hundred mazelike stages, clearing a path with his staff or the Word of God and leading his people onward. Between the action it quizzes you: some two hundred and fifty questions drawn from the book of Exodus, so that progress depends as much on knowing your scripture as on quick reflexes.

There is a nice piece of trivia hiding under the robes. Exodus was not built from scratch. It is a biblical reskin of Crystal Mines, a digging-and-puzzle game Color Dreams had already made in its secular days. That was the house style: take an existing engine, drape it in a Bible story, and sell it to a new audience. It makes these cartridges a strange sort of double artefact, both a Sunday-school lesson and a recycled arcade puzzler.

## The cartridge that broke the rules

The most interesting part is the thing the game runs on, because getting an unapproved cartridge to work at all was a technical feat. On the NES, Nintendo guarded the console with a lockout chip, the 10NES, that checked for a matching partner inside every cartridge. Color Dreams got around it with a small circuit that hit the lockout chip with a voltage spike to knock it briefly offline, a trick first seen in its baby-blue Baby Boomer cartridge.

The Game Boy worked differently, and so did the workaround. The DMG-01 has no lockout chip. Instead, its boot ROM checks the cartridge for the Nintendo logo before it will start, a hurdle unlicensed publishers had to clear just to get their game to boot. What Wisdom Tree is best remembered for by cartridge collectors, though, is its memory controller. Where a normal Game Boy cartridge uses one of Nintendo's own MBC chips to switch between banks of ROM, Wisdom Tree used a mapper of its own, built from a single cheap, off-the-shelf logic chip. It was crude: it swapped the entire lower 32 kilobytes of memory in one go, and, oddly, it chose which bank to show based on which address the game wrote to rather than the value it wrote, which meant the cartridge header and start-up code had to be duplicated into every bank so the machine could always find its footing. It is simple enough, and strange enough, that emulators still carry a dedicated Wisdom Tree setting just to run these games correctly.

## Why an unapproved game belongs in a DMG-01 collection

It would be easy to dismiss a bootstrapped Bible game as a curiosity, but it is exactly the kind of object that fills in the edges of the Game Boy's story. The grey brick was not only home to Nintendo's polished first-party hits. Around it grew a whole unofficial economy of publishers who found their way onto the platform without permission, and Wisdom Tree is one of the most successful and peculiar of them. A complete, boxed [Exodus](https://www.gameboymuseum.com/game-boy-hardware-guide/games/wt-exodus-cib-gb/game-boy-exodus-journey-to-the-promised-land) sits naturally beside the collection's other unlicensed and grey-market pieces, documented in [Bootlegs and Grey Markets: the Game Boy's shadow economy](https://www.gameboymuseum.com/single-post/game-boy-piracy-grey-market) and across the [Bootlegs and Flashcarts](https://www.gameboymuseum.com/single-post/bootlegs-and-flashcarts) series. It is proof that the Game Boy's library was always wider, and odder, than the official catalogue admits.
