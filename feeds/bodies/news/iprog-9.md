Robotic Rabbits are being used to fight back against invasive Burmese pythons in the Florida Everglades. The robotic rabbits are used to attract the pythons, which can then be removed by specialist python hunters.  Burmese pythons are a big problem in the Florida Everglades. They are efficient killers of small mammals, and because they're not native to the Everglades, their prey has no natural caution to avoid them. One potential solution is the use of decoy robot rabbits to lure the pythons into locations where they can be removed.  The pythons came into Florida as exotic pets, and were introduced to the Everglades when they grew too large and their owners released them into the wild. Pythons can grow up to 20 feet long, and a a study from the U.S. Geological Survey found that they are causing major problems for small mammals. Researchers say that that raccoon sightings are down by 99.3%, there are 98.9% fewer opossums, and 87.5% fewer bobcats. Marsh rabbits have disappeared completely. Things have got so bad that the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) and the South Florida Water Management District (SFWMD) hold an annual competition for python removal. The 10-day public event is held annually in July, and entrants have used various techniques including sniffer dogs, live traps, thermal cameras, and even manual pinning and wrestling where snake wranglers grabbed the snakes behind the head - have all had some measure of success. The 2026 challenge winner, Tom Rahill, removed 96 to 98 snakes during the active periods. Over 23,500 invasive pythons have been removed since tracking has been expanded. However, there are still a lot of pythons out there. There are estimated to be around 300,000 pythons in the main infected area of the Everglades, but the concern is that there's little to stop the snakes spreading.  In a high-tech alternative, researchers from the University of Florida (led by Professor Bob McCleery at the Department of Wildlife Ecology and Conservation) and the South Florida Water Management District are testing the use of robot rabbits to attract the pythons for removal. VIDEO This is an extension of earlier research by Professor McCleery and his team, who began by using radio-collared rabbits to see what happened to them. The short answer was that the pythons ate the rabbits, and within nine months 77% of the radio collars were transmitting from inside python stomachs. A later experiment used live rabbits in protected pens as bait. This worked well, and attracted a python a week. However, the rabbits needed care and feeding, and the swampy nature of the Everglades makes it difficult to scale the experiment to a large enough size. In 2025 the researchers replaced the rabbits with solar-powered robot rabbits that are a combination of toy rabbits with their stuffing removed and replaced with thermal pads running at 99 degrees, twitching ears, the right 'rabbit' smell, and vibrating feet that mimic real rabbits. The robot rabbits also have AI-linked cameras that alert a contractor when a python appears. Once a python arrives, AI takes over. The AI uses the onboard cameras to track where the python is, and sends an alert to the nearest licensed trapper who can go and safely remove it and euthanize it.  Each robot rabbit costs around $4,000 to manufacture, but once created run on solar power. The researchers currently have around 120 of the decoy rabbits deployed in the Everglades and are testing what works best to attract pythons. Each rabbit can be programmed differently to test whether its best to use heat, scent, movement, vibrations or a combination of all four. **Photo Credit: Robert McCleery/University of Florida** ****
