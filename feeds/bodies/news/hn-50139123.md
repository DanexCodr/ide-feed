The interpretation of Ui-te-Rangiora reaching Antarctic waters has been questioned.<sup>[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-5)</sup> Anderson et al. note that there is no mention of an Antarctic voyage in the original legend, and that it is first mentioned in the story of his descendant Te Aru Tanga Nuku, who wished to "behold all the wonderful things on the ocean" seen by his ancestor.<sup>[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-Andersonetal2021a-6)</sup> Anthropologist [Te Rangi Hīroa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Buck_(anthropologist)) assessed the legend as having "so much post-European information" that it cannot be accepted as accurate and ancient.<sup>[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-7)</sup> As the [Cook Islands Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Islands_Māori) language had no pre-European word for 'ice' or 'frozen', interpreting *Tai-uka-a-pia* as a frozen sea may be a mistranslation, and an alternate interpretation is "sea covered with foam like arrowroot".<sup>[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-8)</sup> New Zealand [iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi) [Ngāi Tahu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngāi_Tahu) considers the legend to be a mythic origin story rather than a historical voyaging narrative.<sup>[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-9)</sup>

It has been suggested that the folklore of the islanders reflected an actual event, namely a sea area covered with a dense layer of floating pieces of [pumice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice) resulting from some undersea [volcanic eruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_eruption). Such a 25 000 km<sup>2</sup> sea surface was sighted in 2012 in the area of [Kermadec Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermadec_Islands), with a 60 cm thick bright white layer resembling a shelf glacier.<sup>[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-10)</sup>

It has been claimed that in 1886 [Lapita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita) pottery shards were discovered on the [Antipodes Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodes_Islands), indicating that [Polynesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_people) did reach that far south.<sup>[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-11)</sup> However, the claim has not been substantiated; indeed, no archaeological evidence of human visitation prior to European discovery of the islands has been found.<sup>[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-12)</sup>

[Enderby Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enderby_Island#History), considerably south of the Antipodes Islands, has been found to have proof of 13th- or 14th-century [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Māori_people) use.<sup>[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-13)</sup> Similarly, a craft of 'ancient design' was found in 1810 on the subantarctic [Macquarie Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macquarie_Island), considerably south and west of the [Auckland Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auckland_Islands).  It has been suggested that the craft was burnt for fuel that year in the ensuing penguin and seal oil fires, and that it was possibly a Polynesian vessel. In the same year, Captain Smith described in more detail what is presumably the same wreck: 'several pieces of wreck of a large vessel on this Island, apparently very old and high up in the grass, probably the remains of the ship of the unfortunate [de Lapérouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-François_de_Galaup,_comte_de_Lapérouse).'<sup>[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ui-te-Rangiora#cite_note-14)</sup> However, de Lapérouse had wrecked in [Vanikoro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanikoro) in the [Solomon Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Islands) in 1788.
