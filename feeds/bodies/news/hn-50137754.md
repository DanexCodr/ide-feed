CHICAGO — Contrary to popular belief, the postmenopausal ovary is not defunct.

[In a new, paradigm-shifting study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/acel.70714) in ovaries from 28 women, scientists at Northwestern University and the Buck Institute for Research on Aging found in the decades well past menopause, the ovary remains biologically active and continues to remodel with age, showing increased inflammation and changes in tissue structure. The study also found a molecular link between ovarian aging and cancer.

The study [was published Oct. 14 in Aging Cell](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/acel.70714).

“In the ovary, we reach a point of no return with respect to fertility in which it's no longer producing eggs, and I would venture to say most people assume those ovaries are just sitting there doing nothing, similar to the appendix,” said co-corresponding author [Francesca Duncan](https://www.feinberg.northwestern.edu/faculty-profiles/az/profile.html?xid=26852), associate professor of reproductive science in medicine at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine and associate professor in residence at the Buck Institute’s Center for Healthy Aging in Women. “But that's what makes this study very different.”

The findings challenge the notion that the ovary is an innocent bystander after reproduction ends, raising new questions about whether it could play a more active role in aging and chronic disease in women.

“The ovary is not just a reproductive organ,” said co-corresponding author [Birgit Schilling](https://www.buckinstitute.org/lab/schilling-lab/), professor and director of the Mass Spectrometry Center at the Buck Institute. “It really influences how somebody ages because it speaks to all these other organs, such as the brain, and also to the bone, and women after menopause experience often a lot of bone problems. So, we think there is a connection between all these organs.”

Women now routinely live for decades after menopause, making it increasingly important to understand what the ovary is doing during this long post-reproductive period, Duncan said.

“Is the ovary the canary in the coal mine in women?” Duncan said. “Could aging in the ovary set off a cascade of changes that continues throughout the body as women get older?”

## Should otherwise healthy ovaries one day be removed?

Duncan’s group previously found similar evidence of continued remodeling in [postmenopausal ovaries of mice](https://academic.oup.com/molehr/article/32/2/gaag038/8705536?login=false). When she embarked on that research and the current study in humans, Duncan said she, too, expected to see very little activity in the postmenopausal ovary.

“I was predicting that if we took a 50-year-old ovary, a 60-year-old ovary, a 70-year-old, an 80-year-old ovary, they would all look exactly the same, have no eggs, and basically be non-functional,” Duncan said. “The fact that across species we're seeing this gives us confidence that this isn't just some random event, but something truly meaningful is happening in the biology.”

What remains unknown, and will require much more research, is exactly what the ovary is doing after menopause and whether those changes are beneficial or harmful as women age, Duncan said.

“Our data points to progressive aging features in which the ovary appears to lose its original identity and ends up doing something different,” Duncan said. “We do not yet know if there's ever a point in a woman's life when this organ is no longer beneficial and it's actually harmful. These results really point to the need to ask this question.”

## Going beyond the ovary’s hormones

To find out what was changing, the scientists went beyond ovarian hormones to analyze the composition of the tissue at high resolution. Schilling’s team – spearheaded by Buck Institute staff scientist and Schilling lab member [Mark Watson](https://www.buckinstitute.org/lab/schilling-lab/) — used advanced proteomic technologies that can identify and measure thousands of proteins at once, to analyze 5,812 groups of proteins in normal ovarian tissue from postmenopausal women ages 50 to 75.

Human ovarian tissue is difficult to obtain, Duncan said, making the 28 ovaries studied an unusually valuable collection. By focusing exclusively on women within a relatively narrow 25-year age range after menopause, rather than comparing young and old ovaries across an entire lifespan, the researchers were able to zero in on molecular changes that continue to occur during the postmenopausal years. The technology also allowed the team to examine thousands of proteins across every sample and look for patterns that emerged using multiple types of statistical analysis.

Those analyses painted a consistent picture: The ovary continues to change well after its reproductive life ends.

Ovaries from younger postmenopausal women had more proteins involved in maintaining cells and tissue structure, while older ovaries had more proteins associated with inflammation, immune activity and the ongoing breakdown and rebuilding of the tissue surrounding cells. The differences were most pronounced between ovaries from women in their 50s and those from women 70 and older.

“When we analyzed the data in multiple different statistical ways, what became clear across all these methodologies is that the ovary was not stagnant,” Schilling said. “In fact, over the decades, there were quite dramatic differences in terms of some proteins’ levels would go up with age and some proteins’ levels would go down, which tells us this organ is quite dynamic.”

The aging ovaries also showed some of the same molecular patterns seen in cellular senescence, a state in which aging or damaged cells stop dividing but remain biologically active and can send signals that affect surrounding tissue.

## A molecular link between ovarian aging and cancer

The findings also build on a discovery Duncan’s lab made a decade ago. In 2016, [Duncan’s lab was the first to find](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2016/08/infertility-scarred-ovaries) that as ovaries age, they become increasingly inflamed, fibrotic and stiff, similar to scarring in other tissues. Because cancer cells can thrive in collagen-rich, stiff environments, the scientists have been investigating whether these age-related changes could make the ovary more hospitable to cancer cells.

The new study provides a molecular view of that same remodeling process later in life. As postmenopausal ovaries aged, proteins involved in maintaining tissue structure declined, while proteins involved in inflammation and reshaping the tissue environment became more prominent.

Although all the ovarian tissue in the study appeared normal and cancer-free, some proteins previously associated with ovarian cancer were more abundant in aging ovaries, with the strongest signal among women in their 60s. The finding does not mean the ovaries were cancerous or precancerous, but suggests healthy ovarian aging and ovarian cancer share some of the same biological processes.

“It's not that all postmenopausal women get ovarian cancer, but we know that that fibrotic inflammatory environment is certainly an environment that cancer cells prefer to home in on,” Duncan said. “This is all consistent with the idea that the aging ovary is a potential third hit for ovarian cancer, with the first hit being genetic and the second hit being environmental. So can we target the aging ovary therapeutically to lower the risk of ovarian cancer, or can we develop novel biomarkers of ovarian cancer?”

The findings do not answer whether healthy ovaries should ever be removed after menopause, the researchers emphasized. Rather, they raise new questions about how the biological activity of the postmenopausal ovary might influence women’s health systemically and whether the changes researchers observed contribute to disease or are simply features of normal aging.
