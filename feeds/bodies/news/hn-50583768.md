Hera science team member, astrophysicist and Queen guitarist Sir Brian May has added an additional dimension to the ESA planetary defence mission’s early views of the Didymos asteroid system via stereoscopic imagery – revealing the dramatic features of this exotic body as never before.

![For colour 3D glasses](https://media.esa.int/global/public/common/misc/2026/11/402681/image-thumb__402681__fit_medium/3d@2x.7a566c91.webp)

Sir Brian May comments: “The Hera spacecraft acquired these two images on 06 November as it circled the Didymos system at a distance of 29.7 km with a resolution of 2.8 m per pixel, providing the perfect opportunity to create this stereoscopic image. Viewed in depth, the sharpness and smoothness of Didymos’s equatorial ridge is very apparent, compared with the rock-strewn surface of the rest of the asteroid.”

Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel, Director of Research at CNRS / Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur, adds: “Allowing us to observe in 3D with stereo pairs really makes us feel like we are there – even though these images come from 197 million km away. The result is both spectacular and scientifically meaningful.”

## Media modal

![Stereo view of Didymos asteroid for cross-eyed viewing](https://media.esa.int/global/public/common/misc/2026/11/402683/image-thumb__402683__fit_medium/didymos-for-patrick-simple-crosseye@2x.d80c6de5.webp)

Sir Brian May produced this stereoscopic view of Didymos with his colleague Claudia Manzoni. It combines adjacent acquisitions of the asteroid taken a few moments apart using Hera’s main Asteroid Framing Camera instrument by the instrument’s principal investigator Jean-Baptiste Vincent of the German Aerospace Center, [DLR](https://www.dlr.de/en).

The very slight changes between the two images mimic the way our two human eyes view the same objects from very slightly different angles to yield stereo vision. The challenge comes in aligning the two images in a manner that seems optically true to our eyes.

![Stereo view of Didymos asteroid to look into infinity to see](https://media.esa.int/global/public/common/misc/2026/11/402684/image-thumb__402684__fit_medium/didymos-for-patrick-simple-parallel@2x.81ba462a.webp)

The stereoscopic views can be seen using colour 3D glasses, a dedicated viewer or else with your own eyes through [‘free viewing’](https://www.londonstereo.com/stereophotography.html#freeview).

The 3D view can be seen using a pair of red and blue 3D glasses. The second version can be seen by crossing your eyes, while the third can be accessed by looking into infinity – or else via a dedicated viewer, such as the [OWL stereoscope](https://shop.londonstereo.com/lsc-owl-viewer.html%20), designed by Sir Brian May and available from the[London Stereoscopic Company](https://shop.londonstereo.com).

**Hera’s first images to be unveiled**

In 2022 the small moonlet of Didymos called Dimorphos – not seen here – was impacted by NASA’s DART spacecraft to shift its orbit around the larger body. Now the European Space Agency’s Hera mission has arrived to perform a close-up ‘crash scene investigation’ of the binary asteroid system, to gather vital data to turn this grand-scale experiment into a well understood and potentially repeatable planetary defence technique.
