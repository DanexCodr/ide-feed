Over the last few weeks, I worked on a map of various game dev techniques for people to refer to and learn from. Like,
	a real map! With each town being a technique's home, provided with a concise, illustrated example. And here we are. Today we
	are diving into the making of [The Game Dev Atlas](https://www.spritefusion.com/game-dev-atlas).

## That's so 1570, bro.

If you want to pull off a cohesive art direction, you need to spend most of your time learning about your reference
		materials. No C code, no IDE, no sub-agents doing things. Just explore and wander on Google Images (old-school, I know),
		Wikipedia, and online museum galleries.

Gather things, ideas, and visuals! You should feel the inspiration (literally, as you inspire fresh air) inflating your
		mind, and the first scraps of decisions starting to flow through your head, like: I want a bit of that, none of that, this
		thing specifically for that part etc.

That is the whole work: what do you want? It's on you to set the quality bar and how things should be.

If you don't already know, typography conveys most of the design weight. That's just it.

**Example:** after some research I stumbled on that Luminari font that was a
		great fit for what I *wanted* my map to look like. It is a paid font, and there were plenty of free alternatives, but
		not quite as right as the Luminari font. Instead of saying "that's ok, this free one is the same vibe", I just paid for *the* font I found just right. Never concede terrain on your instinct!

## Making towns interesting.

The point of the atlas is to showcase game dev techniques, one per town. So I decided two things:

First, I wanted small visual touches on each town's shape reflecting the technique it's about. For instance, the [Procedural Legs](https://www.spritefusion.com/game-dev-atlas/pixel-art/procedural-legs) town shows a crawling house.

![The Procedural Legs town on the map: a hall standing on six wooden legs, a smaller house walking off along the road](https://www.spritefusion.com/blog/making-a-fantasy-map-like-a-16th-century-atlas/town-procedural-legs.webp)

Second, I wanted organic town buildings, so I built a small procedural building system to add diversity to buildings. Risky
		move: I'm a big fan of Houdini, so I could have thrown away the whole thing, and this blog post would be titled "I'm making
		Houdini, but running in the browser".

## 60fps on the web.

The map application itself is written in C using the [Kaigen](https://kaigen3d.com) engine as a headless C lib. I'm a Rust guy, but I must admit the compile time freaks me out for game dev with Rust. It took me
		several iterations to reach smooth 60fps in the browser, especially with the night mode ([try it!](https://www.spritefusion.com/game-dev-atlas)).

But I'm finally happy with how it runs right now, while I'm still tweaking things atm.

*The night mode, [try it](https://www.spritefusion.com/game-dev-atlas)*

The web app is written in Svelte, because I like Svelte. So the shallow routing and anything UI happen as part of the
		SvelteKit app.
