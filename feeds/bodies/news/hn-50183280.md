If you are a software engineer, or generally work in the tech industry, there is
a good chance that you heard about the [eMudhra Intermediate CA
Compromise](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/) that was [disclosed
yesterday](https://groups.google.com/a/mozilla.org/g/dev-security-policy/c/0R3XLJL6FuE?pli=1)
(October 20, 2026). If you don’t, you may have noticed an uptick in software
updates for various products that you use.

I won’t go through the entire background of the situation as there are plenty of
other posts, including the [now public bug
report](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2083449) from Mozilla, that
describe the details of the compromise. However, I have had a number of folks
reach out regarding the impact on IoT devices, specifically microcontroller
(MCU) class devices. Some of them may have been motivated by the discourse
around the compromise, such as [Russ Cox’s Bluesky
post](https://bsky.app/profile/swtch.com/post/3mydfmedrrc2d), which included the
following sentence.

> IOT boxes that can’t or don’t update are now trivially MITM’able forever.

Scott Helme’s
[post](https://scotthelme.co.uk/eight-bits-of-trust-emudhras-ca-keys-cracked-in-milliseconds/),
which is at the top of Hacker News as of writing, also included a strong
warning.

> **IoT, embedded and OT devices.** Cameras, routers, cars, medical devices,
> smart appliances, payment terminals and industrial controllers phoning home
> for commands and updates. Their bundles are frozen until 2039, which is the
> “cannot be contained” point made real.

IoT is a tricky moniker these days. If you consider a single board computer,
like a Raspberry Pi (or really anything running Linux), to be an IoT device,
then there is a good chance that the aforementioned assertions are correct. You
hopefully have an OTA update solution for these devices, and **you should update
them as soon as possible**. On the other hand, if you have a fleet of MCUs, you
probably are not (directly) impacted by the compromise. In order to understand
why, it is necessary to have at least a cursory understanding of public key
infrastructure (PKI), and specifically web PKI.

On the device you are reading this post on, there is a bundle of root
certificate authority (CA) certificates. In fact, there are almost certainly
multiple CA certificate bundles because your platform (i.e. operating system)
ships a bundle, and your browser might as well. Mozilla has maintained a
dedicated [CA Certificate Program](https://wiki.mozilla.org/CA) since the
[initial launch of
Firefox](https://blog.mozilla.org/security/2019/02/14/why-does-mozilla-maintain-our-own-root-certificate-store/),
and Chrome followed suit when it [announced its root program in
2022](https://blog.chromium.org/2022/09/announcing-launch-of-chrome-root-program.html).
These root certificates are “trust anchors” that allow internet services that
you communicate with to prove that they are who they say they are. Every website
that you navigate to over HTTPS in your browser is verifying its identity by
presenting one or more certificates that can be traced back to one of the CA
certificates in the client’s bundle. For example, when you visited this blog
post, the server that hosts it presented your browser with the following
certificate chain.

```fallback
Certificate chain
 0 s:CN=danielmangum.com
   i:C=US, O=Let's Encrypt, CN=YR1
   a:PKEY: RSA, 2048 (bit); sigalg: sha256WithRSAEncryption
   v:NotBefore: Aug 24 08:02:52 2026 GMT; NotAfter: Nov 22 08:02:51 2026 GMT
 1 s:C=US, O=Let's Encrypt, CN=YR1
   i:C=US, O=ISRG, CN=Root YR
   a:PKEY: RSA, 2048 (bit); sigalg: sha256WithRSAEncryption
   v:NotBefore: Sep  3 00:00:00 2025 GMT; NotAfter: Sep  2 23:59:59 2028 GMT
 2 s:C=US, O=ISRG, CN=Root YR
   i:C=US, O=Internet Security Research Group, CN=ISRG Root X1
   a:PKEY: RSA, 4096 (bit); sigalg: sha256WithRSAEncryption
   v:NotBefore: May 13 00:00:00 2026 GMT; NotAfter: Sep  2 23:59:59 2032 GMT
```

You can use `openssl s_client -showcerts -verify 5 -connect danielmangum.com:443` to see it for yourself.

The first certificate (`danielmangum.com`) specifies that it is signed by the
second (`YR1`), and the second by the third (`Root YR`). The third specifies its
issuer as `ISRG Root X1`, which is the *very* popular Let’s Encrypt root CA
certificate that is present in
[every](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/net/data/ssl/chrome_root_store/root_store.md)
[major](https://ccadb.my.salesforce-sites.com/mozilla/IncludedRootCertificateReport)
[trust](https://support.apple.com/en-us/126047)
[store](https://ccadb.my.salesforce-sites.com/microsoft/IncludedCACertificateReportForMSFT).
Because `ISRG Root X1` is present in your trust store and the server sent you
the necessary set of certificates to establish a chain of trust to that trusted
root, you can be confident that you are in fact talking to `danielmangum.com`.
Let’s Encrypt has a [helpful diagram](https://letsencrypt.org/certificates/)
describing this chain of trust.

While establishing a secure connection to this site specifically may not be
critical, this system is what allows us to perform sensitive operations, such as
sending private messages, managing our bank accounts, and paying for items with
a credit card without fear of an attacker stealing the data. In the eMudhra
compromise, eight intermediate CA certificates, similar to `Root YR` and `YR1`
above, had their private keys accessible on the public internet. The private
keys also happened to be extremely weak, with the top 376 bits of the P-384 keys
being all zeroes. One of the private keys was literally the number `4`
(`00000100`). This means that someone with even moderate technical capabilities
could easily issue themselves a leaf certificate (like `danielmangum.com`), then
present the leaf and the intermediate, and it would be trusted by every trust
store that included eMudhra’s root CA certificate. Unfortunately, that included
all the same major root programs.

Some clients, namely browsers, also require that a leaf certificate be included
in a [certificate transparency (CT) log](https://certificate.transparency.dev/)
in order to be trusted. This has allowed for the major browsers to [assert with
relative confidence](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/#cause) that users of
their software were not impacted, whereas other software very well may have
been.

> The private keys for the intermediate certificates were incorrectly generated
> by the CA. This means that anyone can recover them from the certificates.

> This would have been possible since the certificates were created in April
> 2025. There are no entries in the certificate transparency logs for these
> intermediates in that period, indicating that CT-enforcing clients like
> Firefox, Chrome, Edge and Safari could not have been attacked.

> However, many clients outside of the browser ecosystem do not enforce
> Certificate Transparency. We do not know if attackers exploited the flawed key
> generation to target these clients.

The disclosure of this compromise led to a mad dash across [programming
languages](https://groups.google.com/g/golang-announce/c/66pojueaQek),
[developer tools](https://github.com/astral-sh/uv/releases/tag/0.13.4), [OS
packages](https://bugs.launchpad.net/ubuntu/+source/ca-certificates/+bug/2171507),
and essentially any software involved with establishing a [TLS
connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security), to either
filter for these compromised intermediates, or reject the eMudhra root CA
certificate entirely. To say the issue is widespread would be an understatement.

So why are MCUs unlikely to be impacted? In short, because of resource
constraints. While including a bundle, or multiple bundles, of
[X.509](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X.509) certificates on a server, desktop
machine, or even a single board computer has trivial impact (what’s a few
kilobytes among friends?), we have to be much more judicious when targeting
devices that often have less than, sometimes much less than, 1 MB of
non-volatile storage. Furthermore, rather than relying on an OS or browser
vendor to go through the painstaking process of evaluating and monitoring
candidate root CA certificates, we are typically responsible for the entire
system, either building bare metal applications or leveraging lightweight Real
Time Operating Systems (RTOS).

For example, the [HTTP
GET](https://github.com/zephyrproject-rtos/zephyr/tree/1405adc9a6042353ff791573d913fa6b202580a6/samples/net/sockets/http_get)
sample in the [Zephyr RTOS](https://github.com/zephyrproject-rtos/zephyr)
connects to `google.com`. Its trust chain looks as follows.

```fallback
Certificate chain
 0 s:CN=*.google.com
   i:C=US, O=Google Trust Services, CN=WE2
   a:PKEY: EC, (prime256v1); sigalg: ecdsa-with-SHA256
   v:NotBefore: Sep 28 18:38:33 2026 GMT; NotAfter: Dec 21 18:38:32 2026 GMT
 1 s:C=US, O=Google Trust Services, CN=WE2
   i:C=US, O=Google Trust Services LLC, CN=GTS Root R4
   a:PKEY: EC, (prime256v1); sigalg: ecdsa-with-SHA384
   v:NotBefore: Dec 13 09:00:00 2023 GMT; NotAfter: Feb 20 14:00:00 2029 GMT
 2 s:C=US, O=Google Trust Services LLC, CN=GTS Root R4
   i:C=BE, O=GlobalSign nv-sa, OU=Root CA, CN=GlobalSign Root CA
   a:PKEY: EC, (secp384r1); sigalg: sha256WithRSAEncryption
   v:NotBefore: Nov 15 03:43:21 2023 GMT; NotAfter: Jan 28 00:00:42 2028 GMT
```

Rather than having a generic set of root CA certificates that covers the
majority of the internet, the sample only includes the [single root CA
certificate](https://github.com/zephyrproject-rtos/zephyr/blob/1405adc9a6042353ff791573d913fa6b202580a6/samples/net/sockets/http_get/src/globalsign_r1.der)
(`GlobalSign R1`) that is needed to verify `google.com`. It is nearly 1 KB (DER
encoded) on its own, but the size of the certificate is not the only
consideration. Enabling all signing algorithms, curves, and hashing algorithms
used throughout the certificate chain can have a significant impact on code size
as well. Additionally, buffer sizes must be configured to accommodate the full
chain; the longer the chain, the more memory that needs to be allocated.

These constraints can lead to frustrating errors and complex debugging sessions.
However, there is something to be said for building a system that is only
focused on accomplishing a fixed, well defined set of tasks. When situations
like the eMudhra compromise arise, I find myself appreciating the inherent
limitations of these devices and the intention that goes into building the
firmware that runs on them. Perhaps this event is an opportunity for us to
evaluate whether some of that intention can be applied in other domains.

Unless your MCU needs to connect to a server that uses a chain of trust rooted
in an eMudhra root CA certificate, and you included it (or the [Sectigo root CA
certfificate used for
cross-signing](https://emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/#intermediates)) in your
firmware, your devices are likely safe from this specific vulnerability. Now all
you have to do is make sure that you patch all of your CI environments,
developer tooling, cloud services, and productivity software.
