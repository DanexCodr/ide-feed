Biologists with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service caught a rare native fish in Oswego, New York, last month that would make even the brightest fall foliage look pale in comparison. During a routine Aquatic Invasive Species Survey in Oswego Harbor, the crew caught a burbot *(Lota lota)* exhibiting xanthochromism, a rare biological condition that gives the fish a saturated yellow-orange appearance.

Although burbot is a native species, its discovery highlights the effectiveness of the Service’s Aquatic Invasive Species Early Detection and Monitoring Program, which uses a multi-gear fish-surveillance strategy to detect watchlist species in the harbors, rivers, and embayments of the Great Lakes. Most fish captured during Aquatic Invasive Species surveys are native species that we expect to occur in the lakes. Detecting rare native species indicates that the program can identify fish you are less likely to find —and may detect newly invading species even when their numbers are extremely low. So, what would catching a one-in-a-million native species suggest? An effective survey strategy, along with a bit of luck!

The finding is notable because burbot populations in the lake are extremely low, and the species typically inhabit deeper, cooler waters than those found within the harbor. It represents the first documented detection of a burbot during a fisheries survey in Oswego Harbor, although a few angler catches have been reported.

The fish’s unusual coloration further distinguishes the discovery. Xanthochromism is a rare biological condition caused by a genetic mutation that suppresses normal pigmentation and produces a yellow-orange appearance. Xanthochromism is considerably less common in wild fish than albinism or melanism, making this an especially unusual capture.

While processing the fish, United States Geological Survey (USGS) fisheries biologists familiar with the species observed that it was emaciated and appeared to be in poor health. Because of the fish’s condition and the unexplained factors contributing to the continued suppression of its population, it was sent to the Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine for a health analysis. The discovery could turn out to be lucky for the species too; this exceptionally rare fish may provide valuable insight into the health of the species’ populations throughout the basin that can inform management actions.
