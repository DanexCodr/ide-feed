# Achieving U.S. Open-Weight Frontier at One-Tenth Cost

LLM pretraining is legendarily expensive. In 2023, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said that GPT-4 training costs $[exceeded $100 million](https://www.wired.com/story/openai-ceo-sam-altman-the-age-of-giant-ai-models-is-already-over/)/$. In the following year, Dario Amodei said that frontier AI models can cost up to $1 billion to train, projecting that costs would rise to $[$10-100B by 2026 to 2027](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xm6jNMSFT7g&t=805s)/$ for pretraining alone.

In contrast, **we developed and trained Ripple-65B Preview for only $32M, **which is **one-tenth the cost of the previous open-weight SOTA on agentic coding**, as estimated to the best of our knowledge.¹ Note that $32M is our full R&D cost starting from scratch, including pretraining ladder development, data mixing, evals, debugging, synthetic data generation, posttraining recipe development, and the final hero pretraining and posttraining run.

# Steep Ascent Reinforcement Learning

Rather than investing in a brute-force RL climb, we carefully iterated on our SFT and RL recipes with a focus on compute efficiency. We opted not to buy any posttraining data, instead devising our own synthetic data pipelines, quality filtering, and coverage metrics.

Our mixed RL recipe uses $[truncated importance sampling](https://openreview.net/forum?id=xsonlP8DRV)/$, $[score centering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20807)/$, and $[linear length penalty](https://microsoft.ai/pdf/mai-thinking-1.pdf)/$ with high-quality long-horizon synthetic environments. The RL tasks are tuned for our model capabilities, and we continuously added harder tasks as the model improved.  

The end result was a steep RL climb from a strong initial SFT checkpoint. We reached a TerminalBench 2.1 score of 80.5 with only 1.4M rollouts, roughly 70x fewer rollouts than Reflection Beam, the prior SOTA.

# A Co-Designed Model Architecture

Ripple-65B Preview’s architecture was designed to be trained and served efficiently on NVIDIA hardware. A key objective was to make design decisions which reduce pretraining and posttraining infrastructure complexity while still enabling strong reasoning and agentic performance. Therefore, we chose to train a modernized dense Transformer model rather than use Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) like other labs. This decision quickly paid off, and we were able to train a strong model from scratch while maintaining a solid MFU (40%) throughout pretraining and posttraining, even with simple sharding, no custom kernels, and no quantization.

Ripple-65B Preview uses a thin-and-deep model aspect ratio, with a narrow d_model (5120) and 80 total layers. To improve reasoning performance, we overprovision the number of attention heads while increasing the SWA-to-global layer ratio and reducing SWA window size to compensate for the increased cost. In addition, we added $[FFN activation clipping](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-oss/)/$, $[gated attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06708)/$, $[global NoPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18795)/$, and $[value residuals](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.17897)/$.

# Careful Model Laddering Led to Large Improvements

We optimized Ripple’s pretraining recipe through a series of careful model laddering experiments.

Our starting point was Olmo 3’s$[model ladder](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04403)/$, from which we tested interventions at small scale with a focus on improving coding capabilities. Our v1.0 ladder implemented $[StepFun](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.04715v7)/$ batch size and learning rate scaling laws, and v1.1 implemented our new ladder which smoothly scales architectural dimensions and model depth. Ladder v1.2 switched from the post-norm baseline to pre+post norm, which was neutral at small scale but showed improvements starting at 4B. Ladder v1.3 implemented $[sigmoid gated attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06708)/$. Ladder v1.4 (2-stream $[mHC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24880)/$) showed good results at smaller scale but did not improve at 4B scale, and was therefore rejected. Ladder v1.5 switched to $[global NoPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18795)/$ and added a final LR cooldown to zero, and ladder v1.6 added $[value residuals](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.17897)/$. In the final version, we switched to our custom pretraining dataset with substantially upsampled code.

Together, the compounded changes improved compute efficiency on code as measured by dolma_stack CE by up to 16x at 4B model scale. In other words, our final Ripple model ladder can be used to achieve similar code performance to the Olmo 3 ladder with **16x less compute**. Note that the slope of the Olmo 3 ladder baseline is shallower than ladder v1.0+, which shows that the compute efficiency improvement will increase with additional scale.
