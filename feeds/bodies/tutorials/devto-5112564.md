*A Little Room to Breathe*

---

I was polishing a few coffee cups behind the counter when the bell above the door rang.

A regular walked in, his jacket draped over one arm. His phone kept lighting up every few moments.

He took his usual seat by the window and placed the phone on the table. The screen lit up again. He glanced at it, sighed, and finally turned it face down.

I looked at him and smiled.

"Let's get you something different today."

He looked up.

"No double espresso today?"

"Today, I'm making you a cup of The Pause."

He looked at me.

"That sounds less like coffee and more like advice."

I laughed.

"Decaf flat white. A little gentler, with smooth, velvety milk. Something to take your time with."

He smiled.

"All right. I'll trust you today."

I turned to prepare it.

The decaf espresso slowly flowed into a warm ceramic cup. I steamed the milk until it was silky and smooth, then poured it slowly, leaving a thin layer of foam on top.

One cup of The Pause, ready.

I placed it in front of him.

"Busy lately?"

"All day."

"Get everything done?"

He glanced at his phone.

"Not even close. There's still a pile of things waiting."

"Then how did you find time to come here?"

He smiled.

"That's the problem. I'm sitting here, but my mind is still running through everything I haven't finished."

I pointed at his phone.

"Doesn't look like it's planning to leave you alone, either."

"Haha. Pretty much."

He picked up The Pause and took a sip.

"Sometimes even when I'm resting, I feel like I should be doing something. Learning something new, finishing a plan, or at least checking my email."

I looked at him.

"Sounds like your system never enters idle mode."

He laughed.

"You know your stuff."

"Used to make a living doing it."

"You worked in IT?"

"Software testing, mostly. I also managed a test team. Back then, whenever my phone rang, I'd check whether something had gone wrong in production, whether another release had a bug, or whether the CI pipeline had turned red again."

He laughed.

"Then I guess we're in the same line of work."

"More or less. These days, my main test subject is the coffee machine."

We both laughed.

I put the clean cups back on the shelf and said casually,

"Today might be a good day for a story."

"What kind of story?"

I took an old book from the shelf beside the counter.

"One written by an ancient Chinese thinker."

---

## The Great Bird and the Little One

He looked at the cover.

"Zhuangzi?"

"Heard of him?"

"Only the name. Never read him."

"That's fine. His stories don't require much background to understand."

I opened the book.

"This one is called *Free and Easy Wandering*."

He repeated the title.

"Free and Easy Wandering? Sounds like a travel guide."

I laughed.

"If it were, I hope you'd find it useful today."

Then I told him a story.

Long ago, a Chinese thinker named Zhuangzi wrote about an enormous fish.

It lived in the northern sea and was called Kun. It was so huge that no one knew how many thousands of miles it stretched.

One day, Kun transformed into a bird called Peng.

Its back was just as enormous. When it spread its wings, they seemed to cover the whole sky.

The bird was preparing to fly south, toward a distant sea.

It wasn't an easy journey.

It needed a powerful wind to lift it. Only then could it rise through the air, climbing to a height of ninety thousand miles before heading south.

I paused for a moment and took a sip of water.

"Here's where the story gets interesting."

He looked at me.

"What happens next?"

"There's a cicada and a little dove in a tree. They see the great bird flying so high and can't make sense of it."

They might have thought something like this:

We take off from the ground and fly as far as the trees nearby. Sometimes we don't even make it that far and end up back on the ground. No big deal.

Why would anyone need to fly ninety thousand miles into the sky and travel all the way to the southern sea?

![Image1](https://dev-to-uploads.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/uploads/articles/jkisai8t5iilf52b00jl.jpg)

He laughed.

"So the little bird thinks the great bird is being a bit dramatic?"

"Something like that."

"Then which one does Zhuangzi think is right?"

I kept a finger between the pages and thought for a moment.

"I'm not sure how he'd answer that. But I don't think we have to pick a winner every time we read this story."

He looked at me.

"What do you mean?"

"Well, the great bird has somewhere to go. The little one has its familiar branches. They fly differently, so naturally they live different lives."

I pointed toward the window.

"If that little bird spent every day watching the great bird in the sky, it might forget to enjoy the branch it's sitting on."

He smiled but didn't say anything.

After a moment, he spoke.

"Sometimes I feel that way when I see other people getting promoted, starting businesses, or landing better jobs."

"Envious?"

"A little. Mostly, I start wondering whether I should be doing something more with my own life."

He looked down at his cup.

"It feels like everyone is running forward. If you stop, you'll get left behind."

I didn't answer right away.

Outside, a small bird landed on a branch. It stayed there for a moment, then flew away.

"Someone else's ninety thousand miles doesn't have to be your destination."

He looked up at me.

"That's a good line for a coffee shop."

"Then let's leave it here for now."

He smiled.

After a while, he seemed to remember something.

"This actually reminds me of a project we're working on."

"What happened?"

"It's an internal tool that was originally built for a few dozen people. Now, during the architecture review, everyone's already discussing how to scale it to millions of users. Microservices, caching, auto-scaling... the whole package."

"Do you have millions of users now?"

"Dozens."

I laughed.

"Are those few dozen people happy with it?"

"Yeah. Actually, it's been working just fine."

"Is something wrong with it?"

He thought for a moment.

"Not really."

"So when are you expecting those millions of users to show up?"

He shrugged.

"No one knows. Everyone just thinks we should be ready."

I nodded.

"There's nothing wrong with thinking ahead. But you can still start by doing a good job for the few dozen people who are actually using it."

He laughed.

"If I said that in our architecture review, we'd probably have an argument."

"Then I'll keep saying it here."

He picked up his cup and took another sip.

"At least no one here is asking me to draw an architecture diagram."

"Not without ordering coffee first."

## A Tree That Wasn't Good for Anything

I turned to another page.

"Zhuangzi also told a story about a tree. It's one of my favorites."

In the story, Zhuangzi's friend Huizi mentioned a very unusual tree.

Its trunk was thick but twisted and uneven. A carpenter couldn't cut a straight, usable piece of timber from it. Its branches bent in all sorts of directions, making them unsuitable for building anything.

When carpenters passed by, they barely gave it a glance.

Huizi thought the tree was completely useless.

But Zhuangzi asked: why not plant it in a wide-open field?

You could walk around it whenever you liked.

And when you were tired, you could lie down in its shade.

Because the tree didn't meet a carpenter's standards, no one would come along with an axe to cut it down.

![Image2](https://dev-to-uploads.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/uploads/articles/wmfg9fw4qeott35c9h5y.jpg)

He looked out the window.

"So it survived because it was useless?"

"That's one way to put it."

I looked outside too.

"But it made me think of something else."

"What's that?"

"Think about the trees in a forest that grow straight and tall. Their trunks make good timber, so people value them. Sooner or later, someone comes along with an axe."

I picked up my cup and took a sip.

"Meanwhile, the crooked ones aren't going to become beams or furniture. Nobody has much use for them. So they just keep growing where they are."

He smiled.

"Sounds like not being good enough can sometimes be lucky."

"Maybe."

I looked out the window.

"But I doubt that tree ever worried about any of that."

He followed my gaze.

"It just keeps growing?"

"Something like that."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I suppose there's a bit of a lesson for software development in there, too."

"How so?"

"We're always trying to refactor old code. Improve performance, clean up the architecture, make things more reusable. Sometimes a little tool works perfectly well, but someone decides it's not elegant enough and needs to be redesigned."

"Of course, bugs need fixing. Code needs maintenance."

I put down my cup.

"But some things are fine just doing the job they were made for."

He laughed.

"That doesn't sound like something a former test manager would say."

"Then you probably don't know how often I used to ask developers to change their code."

He burst out laughing.

After a while, he looked at me.

"So how did you end up leaving IT?"

I didn't answer immediately.

I put the cup back on the counter and thought for a moment.

"Then the AI wave came. The company reorganized, and my role was cut."

He looked surprised.

"You were laid off?"

"Yeah."

"That must have been rough."

"It was. At first, I didn't know what to do with myself."

I wiped down the counter.

"I used to spend every day looking at releases, bugs, test coverage, and delivery schedules. Whenever my phone rang, I assumed there was something waiting for me to deal with."

"And then?"

"For a while, my phone got a lot quieter."

I smiled.

"That took some getting used to. I was so accustomed to having a list of things to do every day that when the list disappeared, I didn't quite know what to do with myself."

He said nothing.

"Sometimes I'd still open my laptop to see what was new in the industry and what other people were building, as if I'd fall behind if I didn't."

I paused.

"Eventually, I did that less and less."

"And then you opened this place?"

"Yeah. I started learning how to make coffee, and eventually rented this space. I didn't realize how many things there were to deal with when you open a coffee shop."

He laughed.

"Doesn't sound much less busy."

"Not really. The coffee machine breaks, the beans need ordering, and the bills still have to be paid at the end of the month."

We both laughed.

I picked up a cup and checked its rim.

"Still, the things I spend my days on are a little different now."

He looked at me but didn't ask anything more.

---

## You Don't Have to Fly That High Today

A little later, his phone lit up again.

He glanced at it, picked it up, then finally put it back on the table.

"Honestly, sometimes I feel like I just can't switch off."

"Work?"

"Not just work. It feels like I have to do something every day. Learn something new, finish some plan, keep improving. Even when I'm resting, I feel like I should be reading a book or listening to a course."

I smiled.

"Well, you've spent a little time with Zhuangzi today."

He looked at me.

"So this counts as a learning task?"

"No. You just listened to me tell two stories."

He laughed.

I put down the cup in my hand.

"Sometimes it's fine to do nothing for a while."

He looked at me.

"You're busy running this place every day, aren't you?"

"Of course. Grinding beans, wiping tables, washing cups, remembering how everyone likes their coffee."

"So how do you manage not to rush around all the time?"

I thought for a moment.

"Not always. Sometimes I mess up a coffee, keep someone waiting, or have too many things piling up. It gets frustrating."

I pointed at the cup in his hands.

"But when you're having a coffee, you can at least finish it before moving on to the next thing."

He smiled.

I went back behind the counter and made myself an Americano.

Then I took my cup and sat at another table by the window.

He looked at me.

"You're not busy now?"

"Not right now."

We both laughed.

Outside, the leaves moved gently in the breeze.

Inside the café, the only sounds were the occasional hum of the coffee machine and the soft clink of cups against wooden tables.

Neither of us said anything.

Neither of us was in a hurry to do anything.

---

## One Cup of The Pause

His phone lit up once more.

He glanced at it but didn't reply right away. Then he put it back on the table.

"I don't think I've done anything useful today."

I looked at the nearly empty cup of The Pause in front of him.

"Didn't you just finish a decaf flat white?"

"Does that count?"

"Well, at least The Pause wasn't wasted."

He laughed.

"You know, I haven't sat like this in a very long time."

"Then stay a little longer."

After a while, he put his phone in his pocket and stood up.

"I should get going. Still have a few things to take care of."

"Take it easy. See you tomorrow."

He straightened his jacket and glanced out the window one last time.

"Maybe the little bird has an easier life than the great bird."

I smiled.

"Maybe. Who knows?"

He walked out, and the bell above the door rang.

I returned to the counter and picked up his empty cup.

Outside, the tree was still there.

Its trunk wasn't straight, and its branches weren't particularly impressive. When the wind passed through, they swayed gently, then settled back into place.

I picked up my Americano and looked out the window.

Nothing particularly special had happened today.

And that was just fine.

Some afternoons, you don't have to fly ninety thousand miles.

**Sometimes, it's enough to sit down, take your time, and finish a cup of coffee you really enjoy.**
