##  The debate we keep having is the wrong one

There are, by my very scientific count, a *lot* of posts on here called some version of "Will AI replace developers?" Most of them end up in one of two places. Either "you'll lose your job to a dev who uses AI, not to the AI itself," or "AI will just reveal who was coding and who was engineering."

{% link https://dev.to/jaideepparashar/will-ai-replace-developers-lha %}


Both are decent lines. I've nodded along to both. But look at what they have in common: they're advice for one person at a time. Get better. Learn the tools. Move up the stack. It's the 2026 edition of "learn to code", which is true, free, and not a plan for anyone except you.

And I'm clearly not the first person to smell this. Aditya Agarwal wrote a headline so close to mine that I had to sit with it for a minute:

{% link https://dev.to/adioof/ai-killed-the-junior-dev-ladder-nobody-has-a-plan-for-whats-next-4p28 %} 

So, credit where it's due, the ladder metaphor is out there and it's good. What I want to add is narrower and more annoying: **if the plan is nobody's job, why would it ever get made?**

> **Same week I'm writing this:** the top post on DEV asks almost exactly this, *"We're Building AI to Replace Ourselves. What's the Plan?"* It has 233 reactions and 200+ comments. That is not what a settled debate looks like.

Let's skip "replace or don't." Assume only a slice of the work moves to machines. Who owns the plan for the humans standing in that slice?

## 📉 What the numbers say (and what they can't)

I went looking for actual measurements instead of vibes. Here's what I found, with the catch each one needs. Every single one needs a catch.

| Source | What it found | Why you should squint |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Stanford Digital Economy Lab, *as reported by LeadDev* | Early-career US software dev employment fell by nearly **20%** since late 2022 | Observational, so it can't separate AI from rate hikes and post-pandemic over-hiring |
| SignalFire, *State of Talent 2025* report | Entry-level hiring at the 15 biggest tech firms fell **25%** from 2023 to 2024 | Big firms only, one year |
| Harvard study on generative AI adoption<sup>1</sup> | Junior employment dropped roughly **9-10%** within six quarters of a company adopting generative AI | I'm working from summaries, not the paper itself |
| *Irish Times*, Oct 14 2026 | Entry-level hiring in AI-augmented jobs fell **16.2%** year on year in Q2, vs 10.8% for those jobs overall | One country, one quarter |

Look at that spread. Somewhere between 9% and 25%, depending on who measured what and where. My read: AI is part of the story, not the whole thing. Money got expensive, companies over-hired in 2021, and "AI" is a very convenient phrase to put in a press release.

You may also have seen a scarier figure, something like 67% fewer entry-level postings. I wouldn't repeat it. It blends postings with actual hires and I couldn't trace it back to a primary source.

But here's the catch to the catch. Even the *small* numbers matter, because of the shape they describe: keep the seniors, give them better tools, quietly stop buying juniors.

## 🧱 The part I can't shake

I can live with the job-market debate. The apprenticeship problem is what gets me.

Elvis Sautet made the point well in his post on AI users versus the unemployed: the tedious stuff is how you *became* a senior. The boilerplate, the small mistakes, the debugging session where you finally learn what the error was hiding. If an agent does all of that now, where does the next person get their reps?

{% link https://dev.to/elvissautet/2026-ai-users-vs-the-unemployed-3jk4 %} 

Think of seniority as **compressed tedium**. Years of small, boring, slightly painful tasks, boiled down into taste. Take away the tedium, keep the job title, and you get engineers with the label and a thinner base underneath.

It gets worse, because what we ask seniors to do with AI is mostly *review*. Reviewing generated code well takes exactly the judgment you only build by writing a lot of code and being wrong about it. We're shrinking the supply of the skill we're about to need more of.

Here's my napkin math for it. Treat it as a cartoon, not a forecast:

{% katex %}
\text{seniors}_{t+7} \approx \text{juniors hired}_{t} \times \text{share who stay and grow}
{% endkatex %}

The savings from not hiring show up this quarter. The bill shows up around year seven. Guess which one makes it into a planning meeting.

Not everyone buys the doom, and they've got a point. Ali Karbasi argues @alikarbasicom  the junior role is changing rather than dying, and that juniors who learn *alongside* the tools can end up stronger:

{% link https://dev.to/alikarbasicom/the-death-of-the-junior-developer-is-greatly-exaggerated-d9n %}

I think he's right about the ones who do. My worry is the ones who never get the chance to start.

## 🤝 So who owns the plan?

Everyone here is behaving *rationally*. That's the whole problem.

| Who | Optimizing for | What falls through the crack |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Companies | This quarter's cost | The pipeline of future seniors |
| Juniors | Getting hired at all | A way to build depth when the agent takes the reps |
| Seniors | Output (which AI just raised) | Time to mentor anybody |
| Schools and bootcamps | The curriculum cycle | Feedback from a market that moves faster than they do |

Nobody in that table is a villain. Nobody in that table is responsible for the gap, either. That's what "nobody is building the plan" looks like in real life. It looks like a table where everyone is fine.

## 🛠️ A small plan that doesn't need anyone's permission

I can't fix an industry in a blog post. (I checked. It doesn't fit.) But there's a version of this that works at the scale of one person or one team, and it's cheap.

**If you're early in your career**, keep your reps on purpose. Pick one task a week and do it with the agent off. Then run the agent on the same task and diff the two. The gap between them is your lesson, and it beats any tutorial because it's *your* problem in *your* codebase.

**If you lead a team**, write it down. Here's a policy file I made up as a starting point. It's an example, not a standard:

```yaml
# team-ai-policy.yml (example, adapt freely)
juniors:
  agent_free_tasks_per_sprint: 1      # a real bug, full ownership, no agent
  walkthrough_with_senior: weekly     # explain why the diff looks the way it does
seniors:
  protected_mentoring_hours: 2        # on the calendar, not "when there's time"
reviews:
  ai_authored_prs_require: "a human who can explain every changed line"
```

And if YAML feels like too much ceremony, change your PR template instead. This is the whole diff:

```diff
## Before you request review
  Tests pass
+ I can explain every changed line without reopening the agent chat
+ A human other than me checked the *logic*, not just the formatting
```

That last line matters more than it looks. If nobody on the team can explain a change, the team doesn't really own it. It just happens to be deployed.

{% details Want to play with the pipeline math? (toy model, every number is invented) %}

Press <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>C</kbd> on this snippet and run it anywhere you like. The point isn't the output, it's watching what a single skipped year of hiring does seven years later.

```js
// Toy model. Every number here is made up so you can poke at it.
const yearsToSenior = 7;
const staysAndGrows = 0.5; // share of juniors who stick around and level up

function seniorsReady(year, hires) {
  // hires: { [yearHired]: howManyJuniors }
  return Object.entries(hires)
    .filter(([y]) => Number(y) + yearsToSenior <= year)
    .reduce((sum, [, n]) => sum + n * staysAndGrows, 0);
}

console.log(seniorsReady(2033, { 2024: 10, 2025: 10, 2026: 0 }));  // 10
console.log(seniorsReady(2033, { 2024: 10, 2025: 10, 2026: 10 })); // 15
```

Skip one year of hiring and 2033 has a third fewer seniors than it could have. Skip three years and it gets ugly fast.

{% enddetails %}

**If you hire**, count the *total* cost. A junior who's slower for six months and becomes your mid-level in two years is cheaper than fighting over scarce mid-levels later. IBM has been reported in the business press to be tripling its US entry-level hiring this year, after rewriting those job descriptions around the work AI is bad at. I can't verify the details myself, but the direction is interesting.

Here's a short list you can steal for Monday:

{% card %}
**Four things to do this week**

1. Put one agent-free task on the calendar
2. Review one AI-generated PR line by line, out loud, with another human
3. Hand one junior (or future junior) real work instead of busywork
4. Write one honest note on what *you'd* still pay a person to do if the tools got 10x better
{% endcard %}

{% details But what if the whole replacement story is just hype? %}

Then the plan costs you a few hours a month and you've kept a healthier team. That's the argument for it: it pays off in either world.

There's also some evidence the clean "replace the humans" pitch is harder in practice. In an August 2026 investigation, Reuters reported on Meta's internal restructuring effort, "Project OT," built around an AI-native model with smaller groups of employees, and described how it ran into an employee revolt and fell apart. One company isn't a trend line. It does suggest the plan looks tidier in a slide deck than in a real org chart.

{% enddetails %}

## 🔮 Where I land

I don't know how this ends. Anyone who tells you they do is selling a course.

What I do believe is that "AI replaces developers" and "AI changes nothing" are both a little lazy, because each one lets us skip the dull, human, unglamorous work of deciding how people learn to be good at this once the easy reps are gone.

Others are already poking at answers. One proposal I liked treats juniors as verification engineers, people who learn by checking what the machine produced, and I'd honestly read it before you read mine:
