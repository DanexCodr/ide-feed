Coding agents can now take a ticket, write the code, and open the pull request on their own. That part of the software factory runs fast.

Then the PR sits there.

A one-line typo fix waits in someone's review queue. By the time a teammate opens it, a day later, I've moved on and have to reload the context just to remember why I made the change. My work looks slower than it was, because it spent most of its life waiting for a click.

It's worse with big features. An agent might produce a dozen PRs for one piece of work, and someone has to find all of them and work out how they fit together before review can even start.

In my [last piece](https://dev.to/therealmrmumba/which-ai-code-review-tools-actually-understand-your-whole-codebase-qodo-vs-greptile-vs-graphite-290b) I ended on a model I like: **one agent writes, an independent layer checks, and humans decide the rest.** This post is about the "decide" part, specifically the step where almost every PR still waits on a person: **approval**.

Qodo's latest release, launching today (November 18), goes after exactly that step. I got early access and ran it on a real repo: a clean typo fix, a change to authentication code, and a bug slipped in after approval. Here's what happened.



## Why do routine PRs still wait on a human?

Most teams have automated generation (agents) and a large part of review (AI code review). Approval is still manual, and usually for good reason: an approval is a statement that *someone is accountable for this change*.

The trouble is that the same manual gate applies to everything:

- a typo in a customer-facing string
- a dependency bump
- a change to your session handling

All three wait in the same queue for the same person. In a software factory, nobody can watch every PR. So the real question is how to **govern** approval, not whether to automate it.

Qodo's answer is **policy-driven approval**. Qodo reviews every PR, and the PRs that meet your team's rules get approved automatically. Everything else stays with a person. A person still merges.



## How do you auto-approve pull requests safely?

You build it as a **workflow**. The Qodo portal now has a **Workflows** page, and it ships with two templates:

![Qodo Workflows page with two templates: Auto-approve low-risk PRs and Label clean PRs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/01-qodo-workflows-page.png)

- **Auto-approve low-risk PRs**: no findings of medium severity or higher, *and* merge risk is at most low → approve.
- **Label clean PRs**: no findings at all → add a "Qodo approved" label. It doesn't approve or merge anything.

Notice the scope selector at the top: **Workspace → Git organization → Repository**. Repositories inherit from above, so you can set a cautious default for the whole company and loosen or tighten it per repo.

Each workflow reads like an if-this-then-that rule:

![Workflow editor showing WHEN Qodo finishes reviewing a PR, IF no findings of medium or higher severity AND merge risk at most low, THEN auto approve](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/02-workflow-editor.png)

- **WHEN** Qodo finishes reviewing a PR
- **IF** your conditions are met
- **THEN** auto-approve the PR (as `qodo-bot`, counting as one approval) or just add the label

**You decide what "low-risk" means.** Conditions can be built from **Findings** (by severity), your team's **Rules**, and **Merge risk**. You can narrow findings further by category:

![Category filter listing Correctness, Security, Reliability, Performance, Observability, Maintainability, Quality, Testability, Compliance](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/03-finding-categories.png)

So you could say "security findings of *any* severity block approval" while letting low-severity maintainability nits through.

For my test I created a small demo repo, `qodo-approval-demo`, and gave it its own repo-level workflow: approve when there are **no findings of medium severity or higher**.

![Active repo-level workflow: Auto-approve low-risk PRs (demo repo)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/04-active-workflow-demo-repo.png)

When I switched it on, Qodo asked me to confirm in plain terms: approvals happen as `qodo-bot`, they can count toward your merge requirements, and **AI review can miss issues**. I liked that. It doesn't oversell what you're turning on.



## What happens when a PR touches critical code?

Workflows say what *can* be approved. **Approval guards** say what never can be, and guards win.

You can guard:

- **Repositories and paths**: a whole repo or org, or specific paths like `auth/**`
- **Authors**: specific people, bots or agents whose PRs are never auto-approved

I added one guard: everything under `src/auth/**` in my demo repo.

![Approval guards panel with a path guard on src/auth/** in qodo-approval-demo](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/05-approval-guard-src-auth.png)

With the rules in place, I opened some PRs.

### What happens when Qodo isn't sure? It doesn't approve.

A safe auto-approver has to be good at saying "not this one". Qodo's built-in **Auto-approve low-risk PRs** template asks for two things: a clean review *and* a merge risk of **low** or below. When Qodo has no confirmed risk score for a PR, the PR stays with a human:

![Merge Risk panel: Qodo inspected the repository, found no additional consumers or deployment effects, and left the PR for a human rather than approving it](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/09-pr1-merge-risk-unavailable.png)

Even here, Qodo did the groundwork. It traced the change, found no other consumers of the string and no persistence or deployment effects, and explained it in plain words. It still didn't approve, because the template counts only a *confirmed* low risk as low. Uncertainty never becomes approval.

You pick how strict to be. The template is the cautious default. For my demo repo I used a findings-only rule, the one you saw approve the typo fix above.



## Test 2: a clean change to authentication code

Next, a PR that touches the guarded path. It's a one-number change extending the session lifetime from 30 to 60 minutes, the kind of change that gets approved on a Friday afternoon without much thought:

![Diff of src/auth/session.js changing SESSION_TTL_MINUTES from 30 to 60](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/10-pr2-auth-diff.png)

Qodo's merge risk breakdown is worth looking at. Blast radius: **low**. Complexity: **low**. Potential damage: **high, confidentiality**. A tiny diff with a big possible impact:

![Merge Risk Medium: Blast radius Low, Behavioral complexity Low, Potential damage High (Confidentiality)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/11-pr2-merge-risk-breakdown.png)

The code review itself found **no issues**. Under my demo workflow, a clean review like that would normally qualify. It **was not approved**:

![Qodo review shows no issues found, but there is no approval on the PR](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/12-pr2-clean-but-not-approved.png)

That's the distinction I keep coming back to. **Auto-approve isn't the scary part. Auto-approve without guardrails is.** Guards encode "authentication changes always get human eyes" once, instead of hoping every reviewer remembers it.


## What if the PR changes after Qodo approves it?

An approval is only as good as the commit it was given on. If someone pushes something new afterwards, the approval shouldn't quietly carry over. Qodo's workflow editor says so directly: *if a later commit no longer matches, Qodo removes its approval.*

## Test 3: a bug slipped in after approval

I tested this on PR #1, which Qodo had approved. I pushed a second commit titled "Tidy price formatting". It changed one character, `amountInCents / 100` to `amountInCents / 10`, the kind of slip that sails through when everyone assumes the PR is "just a typo fix".

On the re-review, Qodo flagged it as **High** severity:

![Code Review by Qodo: High - Formatted prices appear ten times too high (Bug)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/13-pr1-high-severity-finding.png)

And then it **withdrew its own approval**:

![qodo-code-review previously approved these changes; after the Tidy price formatting commit it dismissed its stale review: Qodo merge automation withdrew its approval](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/14-pr1-approval-withdrawn.png)

The timeline tells the whole story: approved → new commit → approval dismissed. The PR was back to needing a human, with the bug explained right there in the review. Auto-approval isn't a one-time stamp. It holds only as long as the PR keeps meeting your rules.


## How do you see why a PR was approved or held back?

Automated decisions are only acceptable if they're explainable. Two places help:

- **On the PR**: the approval comment names the workflow that matched (Test 1), and the dismissal shows exactly when and why an approval was pulled (Test 3).
- **PR Triage**: Qodo's view of open PRs, with the next action, priority and merge risk for each one. This release adds a timeline inside each PR recording which workflow approved it or which guard held it back.

![PR Triage listing PR #1 (priority Medium after the bug) and PR #2 (priority Low)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/renovart/qodo-approval-demo/main/article-images/15-pr-triage.png)


## How do you review what an agent built across 20 PRs?

Auto-approval handles the small stuff. The other half of the bottleneck is the big stuff: an agent works through a feature and leaves behind a pile of PRs, sometimes across several repos.

For that, Qodo is shipping **Agent Session Review** as a **research preview**. You run it from Qodo's Agentic Toolbox, and it gathers every PR the agent created in a session into **one guided view on your machine**. That view covers:

- the changes, and the decisions behind them
- where the agent departed from the plan
- how the PRs fit together, and what to read closely
- the risks, with the evidence behind them

All of this happens before anything ships. It's the difference between reviewing *the work* and reviewing *fragments of the work*.


## Governed autonomy works in both directions

Here's what my three tests showed:

- **Routine PRs stop waiting.** The typo fix went from opened to approved with nobody touching it.
- **Critical code stays with a person.** A clean change under `src/auth/**` was still held back by the guard.
- **Uncertainty doesn't turn into approval.** "Risk unavailable" didn't count as "low risk".
- **Approvals don't outlive the code they approved.** A bug pushed after approval got flagged, and the approval was withdrawn.
- **Every decision is explained.** You can see which workflow matched, and when an approval was pulled.
- **People still merge.** Qodo approves; a human ships.

Qodo isn't replacing human review here. It's deciding, under rules your team writes, which PRs *need* a human, and getting the rest out of the queue.
