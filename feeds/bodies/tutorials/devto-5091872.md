*A Little Chinese Philosophy Over Coffee*

---

## An Ordinary Afternoon

The afternoon was quiet.

Sunlight came through the front window of the café, falling across the table by the window. It was the best seat in the place, though I had never officially declared it so. Regulars seemed to discover that on their own.

I was wiping down the counter when the bell above the door rang.

A familiar customer walked in, a laptop bag over his shoulder and the unmistakable expression of someone who had spent the entire day in meetings.

“The usual?”

“Please. Something a little stronger today.”

“Rough day?”

He took the seat by the window.

“You have no idea. Product wants everything done yesterday. Development says the requirements keep changing. QA keeps finding problems. Infrastructure says nobody understands how much work deployment actually takes. And the business team just wants to know why we haven't launched yet.”

I started grinding the coffee beans.

“Sounds like a perfectly healthy company.”

He looked up at me.

“Are you joking?”

“Just a little.”

I poured the coffee and placed the cup in front of him.

“Actually, there's an ancient Chinese idea that might help explain what you just described.”

He took a sip.

“Please don't tell me it's another management framework.”

“Not a framework. No slides, either. Just an old way of looking at how things relate to one another.”

I leaned against the counter.

“It's called the Five Elements.”

---

## Five Elements, Not Five Departments

He looked at me with mild suspicion.

“The Five Elements?”

“**Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water.**”

I counted them off on my fingers.

“In Chinese, we call it Wu Xing. It's an ancient way of understanding relationships and changes in the world. It isn't simply about putting five things next to each other. The interesting part is how they interact.”

He glanced around the café.

“So where have you hidden them? Behind the coffee machine?”

I laughed.

“You can see them right now. Wooden tables, metal equipment, water in your cup, heat from the coffee machine, and the ground beneath this building. You just haven't started charging rent for them.”

He laughed and took another sip.

“Fine. So how does this explain my company?”

“Let's try something. Imagine your IT company has five elements of its own.”

---

## A Little Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water

I picked up a clean cup and slowly turned it in my hand.

“Let's start with **Wood**.”

Wood grows. It reaches outward, puts out branches, and looks for more room.

“In an IT company, I'd associate Wood with the Product team. Product people decide what the company should build, what users might need, and which direction the product should grow.”

“So Product is the tree?”

“More or less. Though some product roadmaps look more like forests nobody has bothered to trim.”

He laughed.

“That sounds familiar.”

I put the cup back on the counter.

“Next comes **Fire**.”

Fire brings energy. It spreads, attracts attention, and makes things stand out.

“I'd associate Fire with Business Operations: the business and growth side of the company. They take the value created by the product and bring it to users. They help people discover it, use it, and, ideally, generate revenue.”

“Ideally?”

“Well, running a café has taught me one thing: never calculate tomorrow's revenue before the customers have even walked through the door.”

He laughed.

“Fair enough.”

I picked up the milk pitcher, rinsed it, and turned it upside down on the towel.

“Next is **Earth**. Earth supports everything. It gives other things somewhere to stand.”

“In an IT company, I'd associate Earth with Infrastructure Operations. Servers, networks, databases, deployment environments, monitoring systems, and everything else that keeps the systems running.”

“So, the people nobody notices until something breaks?”

“Exactly. When everything works, nobody asks how the ground beneath their feet is doing. The moment it disappears, suddenly everyone becomes an infrastructure expert.”

He nearly spilled his coffee laughing.

“That's painfully accurate.”

I picked up the coffee pot.

“Next is **Metal**. I'd associate Metal with Development.”

“Because developers like hardware?”

“Not quite. Think of Metal as something solid and precise, something that can be shaped into a tool. Developers turn ideas into working systems. They solve technical problems, build structures, and make things actually happen.”

“That's a pretty flattering description of us.”

“Don't get too comfortable. Every element has its problems.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“And Water?”

I glanced at the coffee cup in his hand.

“**Water** is Testing.”

He looked at me.

“Testing? Why Water?”

“Water flows. It passes through different places, finds gaps, reaches corners, and reveals the shape of the places it touches.”

I paused to refill the coffee pot.

“Testing should do something similar. It shouldn't just execute a checklist and report bugs. It should also help information flow between Product, Development, Business, and Infrastructure. It exposes risks, finds weak spots, and helps everyone see what's actually happening.”

“So QA is the water flowing through the whole company?”

“At least, that's how I see it.”

I put the coffee pot back on the warmer.

“Five elements, five different roles. But they're not five isolated departments.”

## They Nourish One Another

He looked down at the notes he'd just typed into his phone.

“Okay. What comes next?”

“Now it gets interesting. In the Five Elements, there's a relationship called generating. Put simply, one element nourishes or supports another.”

I took a napkin and drew five small circles on it.

**Wood generates Fire. Fire generates Earth. Earth generates Metal. Metal generates Water. Water generates Wood.**

![Imag3](https://dev-to-uploads.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/uploads/articles/yx1wd74oxt6w3x6l05vw.jpg)
He stared at the napkin.

“That sounds like a rather strange company structure.”

“Give it a moment.”

I pointed to the first circle.

“Product creates value. Business Operations brings that value to the market. When the company makes money, it can invest some of that revenue in infrastructure.”

I moved to the next circle.

“Infrastructure supports Development. Development builds the system. Testing examines it, finds problems, and sends information back to Product.”

“And then Product keeps improving?”

“Exactly. And the cycle starts again.”

He studied the circles in silence for a few seconds.

“So nobody can really work completely independently.”

“At least, not for very long.”

I folded the napkin and slid it across the table.

**A company keeps moving because work doesn't stop at the boundaries between departments.**

---

## Sometimes, Holding Each Other Back Is Necessary

He finished his coffee and leaned back in his chair.

“But if everyone is supposed to support everyone else, why do companies still argue so much?”

“Because there's another kind of relationship.”

I collected his empty cup and carried it to the sink.

“In Chinese, we call it overcoming. One element places limits on another.”

I rinsed the cup and put it back on the shelf.

“Think about your company. Product can't keep adding features forever. Development can't spend six months pursuing technical perfection for every little feature. Testing can't hold every release indefinitely. Business can't chase growth while ignoring quality. And Infrastructure can't reject every change just because things are stable right now.”

He nodded slowly.

“So sometimes they have to disagree.”

“Of course. A little disagreement can actually make a company healthier.”

I dried my hands.

**Overcoming isn't about destroying the other side. It's about preventing one force from growing so much that the whole system can no longer handle it.**

He glanced at the napkin again.

“That might be more useful than another team-building workshop.”

“Possibly. And it's cheaper. All you need is a napkin and a cup of coffee.”

---

## There's More to the Story

The sunlight had shifted from one side of the table to the other. His coffee was gone, and he had finally stopped checking his phone every few minutes.

“Is that all there is to the Five Elements?”

I smiled and continued wiping the counter.

“Not even close.”

“I knew it.”

“There's much more to it. The relationships are far more complicated than five names and a circle. Each element has its own characteristics, too. Some things aren't quite as simple as they first appear.”

I glanced toward the window.

“Take Earth, for example. It's a little different from the other four.”

“How so?”

I picked up his empty cup.

**That's a story for another cup.**

He laughed.

“I'll remember that.”

“Good. I'm running a café. I need regular customers.”

He packed up his laptop and stood.

At the door, he turned around.

“So, which element do you think I am? Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, or Water?”

I pretended to think carefully.

“Today? Probably Water. You've spent the whole week flowing from one meeting to another.”

He shook his head, laughing.

“See you next time.”

“Your seat will be waiting.”

The door closed behind him.

I looked at the napkin he'd left on the table. Five little circles, with a few lines connecting them.

I picked it up, folded it, and placed it beside the coffee machine.

There were plenty of other things we could talk about.

Just not today.

The afternoon was still warm, the coffee was still good, and another customer was already walking toward the door.
