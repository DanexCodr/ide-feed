I recently watched the new *Resident Evil* movie. 🎬

Watching the main character stumble from one disaster into another was absurdly funny and a little heartbreaking. But what impressed me most was his professional commitment. He is a courier, and no matter what happens around him, he never forgets the job: the package must reach its destination.

I will avoid major spoilers here. You can join this discussion even if you have not seen the movie.

But I have to say this first: the main character deserves real credit for his work ethic. He is not a trained superhero. He is an ordinary courier who takes “deliver at all costs” to its absolute limit. That level of professional commitment is genuinely admirable.

So this thought experiment is not making fun of his decision. It uses his commitment as the starting condition:

**You are just as dedicated to your profession as he is to delivering that package.**

You cannot resign, call in sick, close the ticket, or simply refuse the mission. You still have to do your job and move forward. Your only advantage is the way your profession has trained you to think.

This is not a serious disaster survival guide. It is just a fun career question inspired by a horror movie. 😄

![An office worker carrying a critical package into a collapsing city](https://dev-to-uploads.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/uploads/articles/4gt6d4xj14lrbwodpclt.png)

*The city is failing. The ticket is still open.*

Then I started wondering: what if I were in his place?

I have worked in QA for 15 years. If I carried all those testing habits into an extreme situation, what would my survival rate be?

My answer is: **50%.** 🎲

Not because I can fight monsters. I cannot.

My advantage is that a QA engineer rarely trusts a happy path just because somebody says it should work.

## QA Might Try to Stop the Story Before It Starts 🧪

Imagine receiving this task:

Deliver the only copy of a critical item into a city that is clearly falling apart. The environment is unknown. The recipient may be offline. There is no backup route.

My first questions would be:

- Who created this requirement?
- Is the recipient still online?
- Why is there only one copy of the critical item?
- Where is the backup?
- What is the rollback plan?
- Why are we continuing the happy path when production is already throwing alerts everywhere?

Without the “you must be as dedicated as the protagonist” rule, the movie might end in ten minutes. I would return the ticket with this comment:

> Preconditions are incomplete. The recipient is unreachable. The production environment is unstable. Release blocked.

Unfortunately, our rules say I still have to continue.

This is where testing experience might help. One strange event can be random. Two might require investigation. But when the roads, people, communications, and infrastructure all fail at the same time, a senior QA should recognize a systemic failure.

The answer is not to keep following the original route. Pause that path. Isolate the risk. Find a safer alternative. Then continue the mission.

## Why Testing Skills Might Improve My Odds

I would not casually bring a strangely behaving person into my only vehicle.

That would be the survival equivalent of connecting unvalidated, high-risk input directly to the only production environment that still works.

Before entering an unfamiliar building, I would also check:

- Where is the exit?
- Is there a second route?
- Can the door be opened from the inside?
- What happens if the power fails?
- Is this plan dependent on a single point of failure?

I would not assume that something is permanently dead just because it fell over once. I would also avoid putting all hope into one sample, one laboratory, one vehicle, or one person.

Fifteen years in QA has taught me at least one thing:

> Every critical process without a backup is just waiting for a sufficiently unlucky day.

That is why I give myself a 50% chance.

Testing experience might help me spot danger earlier, challenge bad assumptions, and stop a broken flow. But it cannot solve fitness, luck, limited resources, or unknown biological monsters.

Fifty-fifty is already my optimistic estimate.

## QA Could Also Die From QA Habits 🐛

Of course, being a tester does not automatically make someone good at survival.

Our greatest risk is the need to reproduce a bug.

I might escape safely, then suddenly stop and think:

> What exactly triggered that mutation? Distance? Time? Sound? Temperature?

Then I would go back to collect logs, record a better video, and attempt a second reproduction.

Or I might reach the exit, notice one unopened door, and say:

> The main flow passed. I will test one last edge case before I leave.

At that moment, my survival probability drops from 50% to 5%. 🚨

![A QA engineer choosing between evacuation and reproducing a bug](https://dev-to-uploads.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/uploads/articles/1i9mhxb63v5lerxncg20.png)

*The most dangerous sentence in QA: “One last edge case.”*

## What About Your Tech Job? 🧑‍💻

The same rule applies to you. You bring the same admirable professional commitment that the courier brings to his delivery. You cannot end the story by saying, “Not my ticket.” You must keep doing your job while using your professional skills to stay alive.

Every tech role has a possible survival advantage and a potentially fatal professional habit.

### Frontend Engineer

They quickly notice that none of the exits provide useful status feedback and the city map violates every basic UX principle.

Unfortunately, they may spend valuable time complaining that the emergency door has no hover state.

### Backend Engineer

They try to identify the source of the infection chain and shut down the upstream service.

They may also believe that restarting the core system will somehow fix everything.

### DevOps / SRE

They check monitoring, capacity, failover, and backup regions. They would never trust a cure deployed as a single instance.

However, while every alarm in the city is firing, they might still be deciding whether this is a real incident or a broken alert rule.

### Security Engineer

They trust nobody, no attachment, and no unknown device. Their isolation instincts may be the strongest of any profession.

They may also enter the most dangerous area just to prove the full impact of a vulnerability.

### Data Engineer

They build a model from infection times, locations, and behavior patterns.

First, they must accept that half the data is missing, every source uses a different format, and there is no schema documentation.

### Product Manager

They ask for the goal, target user, priority, and success metric before the outbreak begins.

Then, during the evacuation, they ask whether we can add multiplayer collaboration to the MVP.

### AI / Agent Engineer

They send one agent to scout ahead and another agent to review the first agent's conclusion.

The biggest risk is that both agents confidently report “Task completed successfully” while the critical package is still sitting where it started.
