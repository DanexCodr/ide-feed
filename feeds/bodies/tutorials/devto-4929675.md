## Empty

**The two weeks specified in the note had long since passed.**

P went out rarely. Most days it was the shop on the corner: water, two boxes of frozen food. Twelve minutes each way, same route, never looking up. Then the door locked, the curtains drawn, the screen on.

On the ninth day, P opened the send window once.

The draft was still there. The pointer hovered over Send. It stayed there a while. Then the window closed.

On the other side, the field still read `reclaim_state = UNKNOWN`.

Day ten. Day eleven. The window didn't open again.


A few days later it started raining in the morning. Not hard. Water gathered on the glass, the buildings behind it went grey, only the row of lights along the top still visible.

An offline snapshot sat in the middle of the screen, written before the observation port shut down. Nothing after that. By then the entry had already been swapped for a shell. **The shell held nothing of P's. It was still answering health checks from outside.**

```text
# offline snapshot — captured before observation-port shutdown
healthcheck        = 200
probe_interval     = 900s
probe_source       = 10.44.8.6   not self
control_plane      = detached
data_request       = timeout
reclaim_state      = UNKNOWN
```

P put the cursor on the `probe_source` line.

`probe_source = not self`. Someone asked about this shell every fifteen minutes. Not P.

P stayed on that line a while. Nothing else on screen moved. The clock in the corner ticked. Downstairs a shopping cart went past, a wheel caught in a seam and bumped twice, the sound squeezing through the gap in the frame, then fading.

P opened the connection settings. The address was still there. The credentials were still there. **Neither had been touched.**

The cursor moved to the confirmation button and stopped. **The green of the screen came up off the glass and landed on the back of a hand.**

A few seconds later, P closed the window.


The old entry's observation port was already shut. The backup had been checked once at the start, then shut too.

Since then, P had sent nothing to that address.

A temporary file, four lines.

```text
reconnect:
  state information    potentially available
  new activity record  unavoidable
  decision             no
```

Saved. The old settings moved out of the working directory, into the offline archive. **P glanced at the filename on the way and left it untouched.**


The phone lay face down to the right of the keyboard. P opened the thread with Mark. **The last exchange ended before the note.** Nothing after it.

P typed nothing.

Outside a car went through standing water, the sound dragging. P turned the phone over, set it down. Then opened the file that had stayed open for weeks and entered one line from the snapshot: `probe_interval = 900s`.

## Surfacing

Week three. P set up a read-only subscription on the outbound side. Receive. No transmission.

The table started at a dozen entries, several dozen more the next day, and by the third night it was full. Every entry came with its own certificate.

Those addresses were not in P's archive.

The subscription only read. To look at one, P needed another path.

Out of the drawer, a cellular router, rented by the day, SIM included. Plugged in, configured as the sole egress path.

P chose one address and initiated a single handshake.

The certificate was self-signed. Its CN contained a domain name.

P looked up the domain's registration. The registrant was a medical device company.

Disconnected. Packed away.

The list of addresses kept growing. P wrote them down and left them there.

## The Handover

`10.44.8.6` had been in front of P many times. Always on the `probe_source` line. Never changed. Over the next few days, P looked into it. First the route.

```text
$ ip route get 10.44.8.6
10.44.8.6 via 10.44.8.1 dev tun2 src 10.44.8.2
```

P switched windows and opened the local subnet-label cache.

```text
# local subnet-label cache
10.44.8.0/29 -> BLOOM-GW   tagged day-762
```

**`BLOOM-GW`.**

P had typed that label years earlier, when the line was built. The private block was P's numbering. The rack IDs were the provider's. This table was where the two met.


In the provider's public sublease filings, seven racks changed tenants in the same quarter. The provider had also published an annual customer-change notice on its site. Twelve pages of PDF. The first eleven pages were image scans; the last was a single table.

Seven rows. A rack ID, a previous tenant, a new tenant. **Seven rows, not one out of place.** Seven new tenants, checked one by one.

On the seventh, P found that rack ID in another public record. **Vacant.**

**Four were established. Three were shells.** All three registered less than a year earlier, one name on the shareholder list, addresses overlapping with the others'.

One of the three took longer to trace. Registered six months earlier, little capital, a single office address in the public file. P put the address into a map.

**Singapore.** The last time P had seen that name, it was in a message to Mark: **"Your report was read. From a Singapore IP."**

A building on the map, sharing a ground-level parking lot with the one next door.

**On the seventh floor of the neighboring building, one company was on ACL's public certification list.**

P added that too, at the bottom of the same file.


**Seven racks. Seven contracts. Three markets.**

Handed over in order. The legal filings in the public archive were standard, spelling out the limits of each party's obligations. No accusations. No threats. **The seven contracts were signed over thirty-one days**, first to last, no gap in the middle. **The last one was signed last week.**

The address allocation table was updated too. Both records less than a week apart.


P read all of it for a long time.

**The withdrawal was real.**

Seven contracts, seven racks, the notices, the update records, the timestamps. All of it outside, where anyone could look.


P added a line to the file. The date, seven contracts, seven companies, the address with the shared parking lot, in one line. Below it, in plain words: a possible connection.

The cursor blinked after the period. P went back to page one. The **900** seconds were still there.

## One Look

Week five. The source of the **900** seconds still had no answer.

P went back over the two relabeled rack IDs in the allocation table. One endpoint had **443** open. A separate public service directory listed the endpoint as HTTPS.

P didn't connect from this machine. Out of the drawer, the router.

Plugged in, left to come up, configured as the sole egress path.

Then the policy, one line at a time. Halfway through, P stopped and deleted two lines.

The probe. The retry.

What was left was one action. **One handshake. Touch nothing.**

```text
# one-shot observation — policy
target            = <reassigned range endpoint>
retry             = off
application_data  = off
tls_session_resumption = off
egress            = leased isolated uplink (one day)
```


Connected.

P still had the shell certificate's serial number and issue date. If the device at this address came from the same batch, the certificates should have been issued around the same time.

```text
subject      = CN=bloom-gw-07
issuer       = CN=bloom-gw-07
serial       = 4C:71:9A:…:0E
not_before   = (eleven days later than the shell's)
not_after    = (not yet expired)
san          = none
```

Self-signed. No SAN.

Eleven days.


P wrote the serial down and disconnected.

**19:41:07**.

That didn't fall on any of the twenty-one intervals.

```text
# result
tls_session      = complete
client_cert      = none
application_data = none
repeat_attempts  = 0
```

**No second attempt.**


P disconnected the router and pulled the plug. The lights went out together.

The device ran a few seconds, then went quiet. Then it went into the bag, and the bag zipped. A spare patch cable and two adapters in there too, both old, the corners worn white.

After pulling it, P didn't move right away. Checked the connection list first, made sure the session was down with nothing half-open left. Then the routing table, the default route back where it belonged. Then the temp capture directory. One file in it.

**Deleted.**


P wrote the serial down again. That same day, P opened a page saved a few days earlier, the Zurich AI Policy Center site. ACL's public assessment material mentioned it. The council operated under it.

The membership page listed the founding industry seats. **Twelve.** The twelfth was marked **to be filled**.

P looked once, and closed it.

## Nine Hundred Seconds

A few days later, P went back to page one. The **900** seconds were still there.

P pulled up the local copy saved before the port shut. Twenty-two checks, timestamps in a line, all of them before the shutdown. Arrival times, not scheduled times, carried out to three decimals. Twenty-one intervals.

P ran it through a tool.

```text
900.000 / 900.000 / 900.000 / 900.000 / 900.000 / …
```

Twenty-one values. All the same.

P went through all twenty-two again. Same. Again. Same. **The numbers formed a straight line** down the page.


Routine monitoring records usually have some jitter. Network latency, machine load, clock drift.

This had none.

**Not a millisecond off.**


P looked up the provider's operations doc. **On the public page: health checks run every fifteen minutes. Standard cadence.**

**900 seconds.**

**It matched.**


The doc was about racks. Temperature, power, fans, port state.

P stared at the numbers a while, then added a line at the top of the file: routine data-center inspection, BLOOM-GW reassigned block.


After writing it, P added one more underneath. **The interval has no jitter.**

The desk lamp went off. Only the screen was left, its light across the desk, a white line along the edge of the keyboard.

The twenty-two timestamps sat in neat rows. P ran a finger down the trackpad from top to bottom, then back.

Outside, not yet dawn. A garbage truck stopped at the corner, the arm came up, emptied a bin, put it back. Under ten seconds.

Two-story wooden buildings on both sides, painted over more than once, one colour under another. Behind them the glass towers stepped back floor by floor, the top few in fog.

P saved the file and closed the window.

## The Third Cup

The rain started again that day. Not hard. A thin sound on the umbrella.

P got off at an intersection and walked. A little over two kilometers, twenty minutes. Around a corner, a stop outside a shop still open, no going in, just standing under the awning watching traffic. At the light, P stood behind the crowd. Took two seconds after green to move.

Before going in, P looked at the back table.

Empty.

P pushed the door. The bell went, the damp cold came in with it, stopped at the threshold, and the heat pushed it back.


The counter was wood, old. A grinder, a machine, a dish for change.

Behind it, someone was wiping a cup. Slow. The cloth around the rim, down the side, back to the rim, onto the shelf.

A glance up. No question asked. Then turned, took a cup, ground, tamped.

The grinder ran two seconds. A faint smell of coffee, gone fast.

The back table was empty.


P sat. Coat over the chair back, phone on the right, face down.

Old chair. It creaked going down and coming up. P pulled it in a little, lifting it so it wouldn't drag.

The espresso came, hot. A thin mist on the cup, gone quickly. Paper coaster, an old ring on it.

P took one sip and put it down.


A bus went past the window, the cabin lights sliding across the glass, lighting the empty seat opposite for a moment, then gone.

At the far end of the counter, someone sat with an Americano, down to the bottom, the cup not pushed back.

The window seat was empty. No cup on that table.

Two people came in and sat near the door, voices low, a laugh now and then. Behind the counter, the cups went back one at a time, starting from the outside and working in.

The speaker in the corner was on, low, a woman singing, the words not clear. Someone shook out an umbrella at the door. Three drops on the mat.

P's phone stayed dark.


Half an hour.

**More than half the cup left.** The coffee had gone from hot to warm. The mist on the rim was gone.

P's finger stopped on the rim, then came back.


The two near the door left first. Stood, pushed the chairs in, carried their cups up, gone.

The one at the counter moved after. Phone face down, stood, money under the cup, walked to the door. As the customer passed P's table, neither looked at the other.

The shop had the window lights and one person behind the counter, drying a cup.


P put the coat on and stood. The chair leg scraped once. Phone into the inner pocket.

The napkin stayed under the cup. The money lay beside it, pinning one corner, exact, one cup, no tip.

The person behind the counter came for the money. Looked at the cup. Looked under the napkin. Said nothing, took the money to the dish, stepped away.

**The cup stayed on the table.**

The door opened, some of the heat went out with it, the cold filled the gap. The bell again.


The rain had stopped before P went out. P walked to the corner, stopped, looked back.

A street and a pane of glass between. Not clear from there. A figure was going in, walked to the far end of the counter, sat, back to the door.

Before sitting down, the figure turned slightly to one side.

**P stopped.**

She took a laptop from her bag. The screen lit and put a small white patch on her profile.


The road was black with rain, holding the streetlights and the light from inside. Water still on the glass, pulling the figure into a blurred double.

Behind the counter, the half-wiped cup went down. One came from the innermost part of the shelf. Ground, tamped.

**By the time the cup went in front of her, she was facing the back table.**

On that table sat a cup of unfinished espresso.


P put both hands in the coat pockets, turned, walked toward the intersection. Didn't look again.


At the corner. Red.

P took the phone out and opened the blank note on top.

Typed:

**The withdrawal was real.**

Before locking it, the line above, written days earlier:

**The interval has no jitter.**

Still there. P didn't delete it.

Locked it, put it away.

The light changed, people started moving. P didn't.

Red. Green. Red.


The next night. The same corner.

P took the phone out.


The screen was on. The saved route was still there. The recipient had not changed. P went through it once, and the encryption check came back clean.

There was an old draft in the field. P deleted it.

Typed again.

**Two words.**

**"I'm back."**

Checked the receiver. Sent.

The status went to **SENT**.

**The sender field held a single letter.**


P put the phone away. Across the street the red light lay in the standing water, went green a few seconds later, and a passing car cut the red on the road in half.

P walked into the intersection. The phone didn't light again.

**That's [The Empty Fort Strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-Six_Stratagems). The fort is empty. Whoever looks will stand guard for it.**
