## It started with a BBS

I was 12 when I ran my own BBS in DOS. It was called Burning in Hell BBS, because I was 12 and that was the most metal name I could think of. It ran on Oblivion/2 (OBV/2 if you're cool), and yes, people actually dialed in. A phone line, a modem screaming at another modem, and some kid's cursed ANSI art. That was my first taste of running something that other people used, and I was hooked immediately.

After DOS came Windows 3.1, and I stayed on Windows basically forever. I got into computers right away and I've been obsessed ever since. I started working professionally at 19 as a bench tech, and then I worked my way through pretty much every corner of the industry. Refresh and imaging, support as an IT Analyst, field services, cloud migrations for a large financial firm, O365 support, and a pile of smaller, weirder roles that don't fit on a resume without a footnote.

Somewhere in there I tried Linux. Many, many times. I'd learn a little bash here, a little something there, get excited, and then hit the wall. Hardware wouldn't work, wifi wouldn't connect, some driver wanted a ritual sacrifice. Eventually I'd pull my hair out and run straight back to the sweet, sickly embrace of Bill Gates and Steve Ballmer.

The funny part is I always knew I was in a prison of my own choosing. A pretty limited one. I just had no idea how free the people on the other side actually were.

## Three years ago, something changed

About 3 years ago I realized I could use ChatGPT to get through the growing pains. That was the missing piece. All the stuff that used to make me quit (the 2am "why is there a black screen" problems) suddenly had a patient teacher sitting next to me.

Here's the part I don't talk about much. I have severe ADD, and it did me no favors when it came to learning Linux. Or coding. Coding always felt like it was on another planet, because I could never get through a damn book. I'd read the same page four times and nothing would stick. I bought the books, I really did. They made nice shelf decorations.

What changed was having something I could just talk to. I could ask the dumb question, ask it again in a different way, get an answer that fit the exact thing I was staring at, and keep moving. That's how I learn, it turns out. In the last 3 years I've learned more than in the 20 before it, and I'm not exaggerating even a little.

The moment I knew it was working was when I updated my kernel and then couldn't log into my computer. Old me would've reinstalled Windows by sunrise. New me got dropped into a tty with ChatGPT in my ear and fixed the whole thing. I didn't quit. I never went back after that.

I landed on Linux Mint and loved it for the first few years. Then Claude Code came out and something clicked that I'm still thinking about. I could run agents in my terminal.

The terminal is really the key to this whole story, so let me explain why. You can do everything from it. Want to move your mouse cursor? Done. Edit a file? `sed`. Compare and combine files? Easy. Shell scripting, cron jobs, systemd timers? Now you're cooking with gas. It came slowly, and then one day it was just there: I'm free to tear things apart and rebuild them. The source is open and sitting out in the world. I can mix and match, redesign, and make things exactly how I want them.

## Niri broke my brain (in a good way)

A few months ago I installed CachyOS and gave Niri a shot. Niri BLEW MY MIND. If you haven't seen it, it's a scrolling tiling Wayland compositor, and it was like someone read my mind about everything I hated in floating window managers and fixed all of it. Windows live on an infinite strip. You scroll. That's it. I've never wanted to go back.

Then I tried Quickshell, which lets you build your whole shell (bars, panels, launchers, lock screens) in QML, and boom. This was what I'd been looking for my whole life without knowing it.

Omarchy had just come out around then, and after poking at it for a bit I had the same thought I think DHH must've had. I can build my own desktop environment, exactly how I want it.

So I did.

## What CLOUDWERX OS actually is

It's a keyboard first desktop on CachyOS, niri and Quickshell. There's a Rust binary called `cloudwerx` and it's the stable API for the whole machine. Everything goes through it. ## Search, and why I'm a little obsessed with it

This is the feature I'd miss most if you took it away. You hit `Super+Space`, start typing, and it finds a file anywhere on the computer in a few milliseconds. By name, by the words inside it, or by what it's about even if you can't remember a single word from it.

Right now my machine has **13,973,978 items** in the name index, **40,762 documents** with their text indexed, and **477,799 passages** from 36,818 files turned into vectors for meaning search. The screenshot below shows it answering a name search for `/changelog` in 11 ms.

![Searching names, with a preview pane](https://res.cloudinary.com/darl1cllx/image/upload/v1795766753/search-names_lraxcg.png)

You switch modes with a prefix, or with the tabs under the search box.

| Type this | You get |
|---|---|
| `invoice` | apps and commands first, then the best file matches |
| `/invoice` | every file and folder named like it, ranked |
| `/~/Code/` | browse a folder, or complete names inside it |
| `#tax deduction` | documents, notes, PDFs and code containing those words |
| `~notes about moving house` | documents about it, even with totally different words |

There's a filter syntax too, and it stacks with everything. `ext:pdf size:>10mb mod:week in:~/Documents` does exactly what you think it does. Name matching is fuzzy, so `rtrn` finds `return-2025.pdf`.

### How it actually works

There's a Rust daemon called `cloudwerx-searchd` running as a user service. No root, no background cron job. It keeps three separate indexes, and the launcher, the CLI and the settings page all talk to it over a unix socket with JSON lines.

```text
launcher / cloudwerx search / settings page
                  |
         cloudwerx-search.sock (0600)
                  |
          cloudwerx-searchd (Rust)
        /         |            \
  names (RAM)   text (tantivy)   meaning (vectors)
        \         |            /
              inotify watches
```

**Names** live in a compact in-memory table, 24 bytes plus the name for every file and folder on the machine. It's the same idea as Everything on Windows. A full walk of the disk runs in parallel at idle priority. Each search scores every entry with nucleo (the fuzzy matcher from the Helix editor) across all cores, after a 64 bit character set test throws out most names in a single instruction. That's how you get single digit milliseconds across millions of entries. My home folder updates live through inotify, and the rest rescans on a schedule. I deliberately didn't use fanotify, because it would watch the whole disk but needs root, and I'm not putting a root daemon on my search box.

**Words inside files** go through tantivy, which is Lucene style BM25. It reads text, Markdown, HTML, CSV, source code, PDFs (through `pdftotext`) and Word, Excel, PowerPoint, OpenDocument and EPUB files by cracking open their zipped XML. I build the queries by hand so typing feels instant. Every word also matches the common indexed words it starts, so `keybind` finds `keybinds`, phrases match exactly, a hit in the file name counts double, and the snippets highlight what matched.

**Meaning** is my favorite. Documents get split into passages of about 700 characters, and each passage is embedded with Model2Vec static embeddings (`potion-retrieval-32M`, the best static retrieval model I could find). A static model turns each token into a vector with a table lookup and averages them. That's hundreds of times faster than running a transformer, with no GPU and no model server, so a whole home folder gets embedded in about a minute. The vectors are stored as 8 bit integers and searched exhaustively in parallel, which is exact and takes around 5 ms. Junk like hashes, base64 and minified code gets skipped before it ever gets embedded.

The `#` search mixes the text and meaning rankings with reciprocal rank fusion. A document that matches your words and your intent floats to the top, and a meaning match can surface a file that uses none of your words at all. Meaning matches only join in when they're strong (similarity of at least 0.45 and close to the best hit), because static embeddings will always hand you some nearest neighbor, even when the honest answer is "nothing".

![Searching inside files, with highlighted matches](https://res.cloudinary.com/darl1cllx/image/upload/v1795766753/search-content_fy3a2q.png)

![Searching by meaning](https://res.cloudinary.com/darl1cllx/image/upload/v1795766754/search-meaning_rhxk7i.png)

That second one is me typing "how do I make the desktop look different", and getting results from files that don't contain those words. The footer says it found it by meaning. (The first word search in the screenshot above it shows 447 ms because it was cold. Warm it's usually somewhere between 3 and 15 ms.)

Indexing never gets in the way of work. The threads run at nice 15 with the idle I/O class, the first content index does the newest files first and commits every 20 seconds, and a manifest of modification times means a restart only re-reads what changed. Everything stays local. Nothing leaves the machine.

And once you find the file, it's not a dead end. `Enter` opens it, `Ctrl+Enter` shows it in the file manager, `Alt+Enter` opens it in Noteism, `Ctrl+T` drops you into a terminal in its folder, and `Ctrl+K` opens every other action. Files you open from here rank higher next time.

## My apps all grow up here

This is the part that's changed how I work. CLOUDWERX OS is now my breeding ground. Every new project, app or idea I have gets grown inside it, with its own plugin on the bar and its own page in settings, hooked up to everything else.

**Noteism** is the code and text editor I've been building for a year and a half. Picture VS Code and Obsidian having a very good time together. It's fast, it's pretty, it's agentic, and it renders markdown in a way where you can type straight into code blocks, like Typora does.

That screenshot is Noteism with nothing open, so you can see the default screen. It's version 1.8.3 right now, and we've been updating the hell out of it lately. I wrote this whole post in it, by the way. Every word, in live preview, with the images dropped in as I went.

The big new thing is a complete AI panel. It's off until you turn it on, and when it's off there's no panel, no menu, no status item and no network calls. When it's on, `Ctrl+Shift+A` opens a chat beside your notes that can actually do things. It reads and searches your notes and files, looks at the note you're in and your selection, edits and rewrites, creates notes, opens files, checks and draws Mermaid diagrams, looks at pictures and loads skills. Every change it makes shows up as a line diff you accept or reject first (`Ctrl+Enter` to accept), and `Ctrl+Z` undoes it either way. You add context with `@` for notes, files, folders and the selection, `/` for quick prompts and skills, and chats are saved, named, searchable and come back when you restart.

Then there's Tab completion, and yes, I mean the Cursor kind. Grey text suggests the rest of what you're writing, and a marked change suggests the next edit nearby, like a rename you started or a typo a few lines down. `Tab` takes it, `Ctrl+Right` takes one word, `Esc` dismisses it.

It's all bring your own accounts. Anthropic, OpenAI and Gemini keys, any OpenAI compatible server (Ollama, LM Studio, vLLM, OpenRouter), your Claude Code sign in, and your ChatGPT sign in through Codex. Keys live in the system keyring, encrypted, and never reach the window or a chat. Encrypted notes are never sent to AI at all. A few other things I love: it counts tokens live in the status bar, it can make 18 kinds of Mermaid diagram from a selection, and it has a "changed on disk" bar so a Git pull or a sync tool never silently stomps your text.

It also ships as an AppImage now, so it runs on almost any Linux, not just mine. It is coming soon at [noteism.app](https://noteism.app).

**Herdr** is where all my agents live. I run all my agents and sub-agents in there, and with my own handoff skills I can drop a `TASK.md` on an agent and it'll go assign sub-agents to knock out every task, orchestrating the whole thing, picking the engine, the model and the thinking level either perfectly or exactly how I pre-set it. I've got a library of 125 skills that agents load on demand, so they stop making things up about APIs.

![Herdr settings](https://res.cloudinary.com/darl1cllx/image/upload/v1795766744/set-agents_f7yqwn.png)

Some details, because I'm a nerd about it. Herdr has two halves. `hc` (herdr-core) is a small server that runs terminals and agents and keeps them alive when the window closes. `herdr-app` is the desktop window. It's drawn with GPUI, the GPU toolkit from the Zed editor, so there's no web view and no Electron, and mods are JavaScript written against gpui-kit. Agents report whether they're working, blocked, idle or done with an escape sequence (OSC 7501), and the app turns that into badges and toasts in the sidebar. Everything is files in `~/.config/herdr-app`. Save one and the window reloads it in about a second.

My layout on top of it is called cloudwerx-herdr. It started life as someone else's layout (Bettr, by dissimulate) and I've taken it a long way. It has usage cards for Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity and OpenCode with a meter for each limit window and the time left until it resets, CPU, GPU and RAM, an agent activity panel with running sub-agents, a context and cache breakdown, and a Tasks panel that renders the `TASK.md` of whatever project you're in and updates live while an agent edits it. The theme gets rewritten from my desktop theme every time I change it, so Herdr follows along. And the bar has an island for it, with jumps straight to a pane. I am a HUGE fan of the new herdr-app, it's kind of genius. Get it at http://herdr.dev

Being able to see every agent, what it's doing, and how much of its limit it has left, all in one window, changed how I work. I love this thing.

I also wrote TaskPass for it, a small native editor (Rust and GPUI) for one job: writing long careful `TASK.md` files, where every task gets worked by a brand new agent session with its own engine, model, autonomy and effort.

## The tools I built so agents can work on my machine

A funny thing happened while I was building all of this. I kept running into the same three problems whenever an agent worked on the desktop, so I fixed each one properly and put them out in the open. They're all CLOUDWERX LAB projects, they're all small Rust binaries, and all three are public.

### Ghosthand: a real hand on a real pointer

[github.com/CLOUDWERX-DEV/Ghosthand](https://github.com/CLOUDWERX-DEV/Ghosthand)

I got tired of agents that could write an entire UI but couldn't right click a menu on it. A screenshot tells you what's there. It can't tell you what happens when you hover or drag. So Ghosthand gives an agent my actual mouse and keyboard. Click, double click, right click, drag, scroll, hover, type text, press shortcuts, then take a screenshot and see what happened.

How it works is simple. It creates a virtual absolute pointer through `/dev/uinput`, so the display server just sees a normal mouse. That means it isn't tied to one compositor. No portals, no plugins. It reads my real keyboard layout so it types what it should, and `focus` tells the agent which window will get the keys before it types.

The part I care about most is the gate. Before anything moves or types, a card shows up on my screen saying who's asking, why, and exactly what it's about to type. Nothing happens until I click Allow. No answer counts as a no. While an agent is driving, a little "Agent Control" tag and a ring follow the cursor, so I always know it isn't me. One key takes the pointer back at any time, and it refuses to run while the session is locked. For a long testing job I approve one session instead of a card per click.

It's one small Rust binary, with a background daemon keeping the virtual devices alive so a run starts in milliseconds. I've tested it daily on niri. The README is honest that Hyprland, sway, X11, KDE and GNOME are parser tested but not driven live by me, so bug reports are welcome.

Here's the prompt. This is what I see before an agent touches anything. It says who's asking, why, how long I've got to answer, and exactly what it's about to type, word for word. Then Deny, Allow for 10 minutes, or Allow. If I don't answer, it counts as a no.

It jumps around Settings pages, types into the search box, and opens the launcher to search for a file. I didn't touch anything after clicking Allow.

### agent-sudo: root without a password, with a seatbelt

[github.com/CLOUDWERX-DEV/agent-sudo](https://github.com/CLOUDWERX-DEV/agent-sudo)

Regular `sudo` wants a password from a terminal. A script or an agent can't type one, so it hangs forever, or you do the dangerous thing and put `NOPASSWD: ALL` in your sudoers file and now one bad command takes the whole machine with it. An empty variable in `rm -rf "$DIR/"*` is all it takes.

`agent-sudo` (or `asudo` for short) sits in the middle. It runs the command as root with no prompt, but first it reads the command against ten named guardrails that refuse the catastrophic stuff: `rm -rf /`, `mkfs` on a disk, `dd` onto `/dev/sda`, editing `/etc/shadow`, fork bombs. It reads the command, it doesn't just regex a string, so `env`, `nice`, `timeout`, `sh -c "..."`, `$(...)`, redirections, `..` and symlinks don't hide anything. Every run goes into an audit log at `/var/log/agent-sudo.log` with when, who, where and how it ended, and I get a desktop notification each time.

```sh
agent-sudo pacman -S --noconfirm ripgrep
agent-sudo --explain dd if=x of=/dev/sda    # which rule fires, and why
agent-sudo --logs 10                        # what ran lately
```

I want to be straight about what it is. It's a seatbelt, not a security boundary. It stops mistakes and careless commands. It can't stop something that's actively trying to get around it, because anything running as root can do anything, and the README says so in a big warning box. The installer checks the sudoers rule with `visudo` before it touches anything, and it's only two dependencies.

### skills-cli: stop paying for skills you aren't using

[github.com/CLOUDWERX-DEV/skills-cli](https://github.com/CLOUDWERX-DEV/skills-cli)

Skills are great, but the usual way of using them doesn't scale. Put a hundred of them where your agent looks and every single conversation starts by paying for the name and description of all hundred, whether you need them or not. The agent also gets worse at picking the right one, and one careless read of a big skill can dump a couple hundred thousand tokens of docs into the context.

So I flipped it around. The library lives on disk, and the agent gets one tiny instruction. From there it searches, loads and fetches only what it needs.

```sh
skills search "pdf merge"
skills load pdf-tools
skills ref get pdf-tools merge        # one reference page, not the whole folder
skills run pdf-tools split.py in.pdf  # only the output comes back
```

My own library is 130 skills in 3,377 files, about 14.8 million tokens. The instructions alone are around 400 thousand. Listing every one of them takes about 4 ms. And the agent only ever sees what it asked for, with a 30,000 token guard so a slip can't flood the context. It also makes new skills (`skills new`), installs them from git, validates them, and can pin one as a slash command. `skills skill` prints a guide built into the binary, so an agent can learn the whole tool with one command.

## My laptop is also a security camera

I can watch my laptop's camera and listen to its microphone from my phone, from anywhere, and nothing is exposed to the internet. It works over Tailscale only. There's nothing to forward on the router, and the server listens only on the machine's Tailscale addresses and refuses anything outside the tailnet.

You turn it on in Settings › Security Camera, open the address on your phone, and add it to the home screen for a full screen app. The camera only opens when the first viewer arrives and closes about ten seconds after the last one leaves, so it's off while nobody's watching. Up to eight phones at once. By default only devices signed in to my own Tailscale account are allowed, checked with `tailscale whois`, so someone else on a shared tailnet gets refused and logged. And the camera's activity light is always on while it streams. It's never hidden or faked, which I think is the right call.

Tailscale shows up everywhere else too. Settings › Tailscale handles devices, exit nodes, SSH and file sharing, and in Files you can right click a file and choose Send over Tailscale to Taildrop it to any of my devices.
