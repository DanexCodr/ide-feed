Choosing between self-hosted and managed Keycloak affects more than where your identity service runs. It determines who maintains the infrastructure, who handles security updates, and how much responsibility your team takes on when users need to authenticate.

When you add authentication to an application, it's easy to focus on the login screen. You choose between passwords and passkeys, connect an identity provider, and make sure users can access their accounts. But there's another question worth asking before your application goes live: **where are your users' credentials stored, and who is responsible for protecting them?**

Keycloak gives developers control over authentication and identity management. You can run it on infrastructure you manage, or use a managed Keycloak service that takes care of much of the underlying operation.

Both approaches can work well. The right choice depends on your security requirements, your team's infrastructure experience, your budget, and how much operational responsibility you're prepared to take on.

Let's look at what happens to user passwords in each setup, what changes when you choose a managed service, and how to decide which option makes sense for your application.

## What Actually Happens to a Password in Keycloak?

Imagine someone creates an account in your application. They enter an email address, choose a password, and click Create account. Where does that password go?

In a typical Keycloak setup using its built-in user storage, Keycloak receives the password during account creation and stores a password hash in its configured database. A password hash is the output of a one-way password-hashing function. When the user signs in later, Keycloak verifies the submitted password against the stored hash. If an external user store handles authentication, credential storage and verification may follow a different process.

The database doesn't need to contain the original password in readable form. Keycloak uses a password-hashing algorithm and configuration designed to make guessing passwords computationally expensive. The exact algorithm and parameters depend on the Keycloak version and configuration.

Keycloak's default password-hashing algorithm depends on the version and deployment configuration. Keycloak 24 changed the default from `pbkdf2-sha256` to `pbkdf2-sha512`. Starting with Keycloak 25, Argon2 became the default for non-FIPS deployments, while FIPS deployments use a compatible algorithm such as `pbkdf2-sha512`. Existing credentials may retain their previous hash until the user next signs in and Keycloak re-hashes the password under the current configuration.

To inspect or configure password policy settings, open the Keycloak Admin Console and navigate to **Authentication → Policies → Password policy**. 

Review the policy configured for your realm before assuming it matches the defaults for a particular release.

### Where Keycloak Stores Password Credentials

In a standard Keycloak deployment using its built-in user storage, password credentials are stored in the database configured for Keycloak. The `CREDENTIAL` table holds credential records associated with users, including password-related data and metadata.

Two fields help illustrate how that works:

- `SECRET_DATA`: stores sensitive credential data, including password-hashing information.
- `CREDENTIAL_DATA`: stores additional credential metadata, such as information about the credential type and its configuration.

These fields contain structured data, not a readable copy of the user's original password. The exact contents depend on the credential type and Keycloak version.

For example, a password credential record may contain data resembling the following. These are illustrative examples, not actual values from a live Keycloak database.

```json
// SECRET_DATA — illustrative example
{
  "value": "[password hash omitted]",
  "salt": "[salt omitted]",
  "additionalParameters": {}
}
```

```json
// CREDENTIAL_DATA — illustrative example
{
  "algorithm": "argon2",
  "hashIterations": "[configured value]",
  "additionalParameters": {}
}
```

The exact field names and structure can vary by Keycloak version and credential implementation.

### Password Hashing Isn't the Whole Security Story

This distinction matters because password hashing and database security solve different problems. Hashing helps protect passwords if someone obtains a copy of the credential records, but it doesn't make a compromised identity server or database harmless. Attackers who gain administrative access may be able to alter accounts, change authentication settings, or access other sensitive information.

For example, your deployment still needs to protect:

- Database access and administrative credentials.
- Backups containing credential records and other sensitive data.
- Keycloak configuration and privileged administrative accounts.
- The infrastructure and network surrounding the identity service.

### Where Is the Rest of the Identity Data Stored?

Your Keycloak deployment also holds information beyond password credentials, including user profiles, groups, roles, and configuration data. Depending on your setup, it may connect to external identity providers or use additional user-storage systems. The exact location of user data therefore depends on how the realm and user storage are configured.

For a standard deployment using Keycloak's built-in user storage, the password hashes are held in the configured database. If your environment uses an external user store, the credentials may be managed there instead.

This is the starting point for comparing self-hosted and managed Keycloak: the credential-handling model can remain the same, while the people responsible for the surrounding infrastructure change.

## What Self-Hosted Keycloak Means

With self-hosted Keycloak, you deploy and operate the identity service on infrastructure under your team's control. That might be a virtual machine, a container platform, Kubernetes, or another supported environment.

You choose the deployment architecture, configure the database, manage network access, and decide how the service fits into your existing infrastructure. This gives you considerable flexibility, which can be valuable when an application has specific compliance requirements or needs to integrate with internal systems.

### What Your Team Is Responsible For

The trade-off is that your team must take responsibility for operating the service reliably.

That includes:

- Applying Keycloak security updates and testing upgrades.
- Maintaining the database and testing backups and restores.
- Monitoring availability and planning for service failures.
- Protecting administrative accounts, TLS certificates, and network rules.
- Reviewing configuration changes and tracking security advisories.

There's also the less visible work: testing recovery procedures and making sure they still work when you need them.

### When Does Self-Hosting Make Sense?

Self-hosting can be a good fit when you need deep infrastructure control and have the people and processes to maintain it. It can also make sense when your organization already operates identity infrastructure and has established procedures for security, monitoring, and incident response.

But running Keycloak yourself doesn't automatically make your authentication more secure. The result depends on how well the service, database, network, and operational processes are configured and maintained.

---

## What Changes With Managed Keycloak?

Managed Keycloak shifts much of the infrastructure work to a service provider. You still use Keycloak for identity management and authentication, but the provider operates the managed environment according to its service offering.

For a development team, that can mean less time spent maintaining servers, planning upgrades, monitoring infrastructure, and managing database operations. It can also make it easier to get an identity service running without first building an entire deployment and maintenance process.

### What the Provider May Manage

The provider may manage:

- Infrastructure provisioning and maintenance.
- Database operations and backups.
- Keycloak updates and upgrades.
- Availability monitoring and infrastructure recovery.

### What Still Belongs to Your Team?

However, managed Keycloak doesn't mean someone else automatically handles every aspect of authentication security.

Your team still needs to configure authentication flows, manage realm settings, assign appropriate roles, protect administrative access, and integrate the identity provider correctly with your application. You also need to decide how users are onboarded, how accounts are recovered, and what permissions they receive after signing in.

Data ownership and access deserve attention, too. Before choosing a provider, understand where your identity data is stored, who can access it, how backups are protected, and what happens when you decide to leave the service.

Managed Keycloak is useful when you want to use Keycloak's identity capabilities without taking on the full burden of operating the infrastructure.
